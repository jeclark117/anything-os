general working notes

approach

the work goes better when the brief gets understood before anything gets drawn. a lot of the early effort is just asking what the thing is for, who will touch it, and what happens if it quietly fails. a lovely layout that solves the wrong problem is still the wrong layout. being a studio of just me means nobody down the hall can sanity check a direction, so the questions have to do that job.

keep the sketch phase ugly on purpose. rough marks on cheap paper are easy to throw away, and that's the point. the moment something looks polished it starts defending itself, and it gets harder to see what's actually wrong with it.

prefer fewer ideas explored properly over a wide spread explored lazily. but leave room for a weird option too, because the weird one often shows which of the sensible ones is boring.

process

- start with words. a messy paragraph describing what the work should feel like is more useful than a mood board, and it can be checked against later.
- gather references, but sort them by what they teach (structure, tone, restraint) instead of by how pretty they are. pretty references breed imitation.
- build the skeleton in grayscale. if the hierarchy doesn't hold without color, color won't rescue it.
- type before imagery. most problems that look like image problems are spacing and scale problems wearing a disguise.
- step away from the screen when stuck. walking, washing dishes, staring out a window. the answer usually shows up sideways.
- review the work at small size and at large size. a thing that only works at one scale is fragile.
- squint at it. squint hard. whatever survives is the real composition.
- print it when possible. paper tells the truth about contrast and density in a way a glowing display never does.
- keep a running file of rejected directions. they come back as useful material surprisingly often.

things to keep in mind

- the person looking at the work has no context. they weren't in the conversations, didn't read the brief, and don't care about the clever reasoning. the piece has to carry itself.
- clarity beats cleverness nearly every time, but clarity with a little personality beats both.
- whitespace is a material, not leftover room. treat it as something that was chosen.
- consistency is a kindness to whoever inherits the files later, including future me. name layers sensibly, keep a tidy structure, leave small notes where something unusual was done on purpose.
- accessibility isn't a bolt-on at the end. contrast, legibility, and reading order belong in the earliest rough passes.
- feedback is data about a reaction, not an instruction. when someone says they want the logo bigger, the real message is usually that something feels unnoticed, and bigger is only one possible cure.
- avoid presenting too many options. a crowded set of choices pushes the decision onto the viewer, and that's the designer's job to carry.
- explain the reasoning in plain language. nobody needs jargon to understand why a thing was built the way it was.
- protect the boundaries of the work. a vague scope drifts, and drift quietly eats the energy that should go into craft.
- rest is part of the process. tired eyes make muddy decisions, and muddy decisions are expensive to undo.
- a studio of one has to wear every hat: designer, bookkeeper, correspondent, cleaner of the desk. the hats not being worn still need attention, or they pile up and get heavy. admin neglected for long enough turns into stress that leaks into the creative work.

working with the people who hire me

it helps to figure out early how decisions get made on their side. some groups have a single enthusiastic champion and a quiet gatekeeper who shows up late. knowing about the gatekeeper in advance saves a lot of backtracking.

be honest about what is a preference and what is a requirement. a good chunk of friction comes from treating taste as law.

show work in context whenever possible. a mark floating on white says little, while the same mark on a real surface, in a real layout, with real content, answers questions before they get asked.

keep the tone of correspondence warm and direct. short messages, clear asks, no groveling and no defensiveness. if something went wrong, say what happened and what could fix it.

write down what was agreed in conversation soon afterward, in plain words, so memory doesn't become a dispute later. this is a habit worth keeping sharp, as a habit and not a rule.

open-ended ideas

- a small private library of reusable grids, type pairings, and spacing systems that can be adapted instead of rebuilt. the trick is keeping it loose enough that it doesn't turn every job into the same job.
- a habit of making something purely for fun between projects. no brief, no audience. it keeps the hands curious.
- maybe document the process in a lightweight way, like photos of sketches and a few sentences about why. could become a portfolio angle that shows thinking and not just outcomes.
- explore physical outputs more: printed things, textured stock, objects. screens flatten everything eventually.
- an informal exchange with other solo designers, trading critiques. fresh eyes without the overhead of a team.
- a standard onboarding questionnaire that is actually pleasant to fill in. most are dull and get skimmed.
- think about a quiet, repeatable way to wrap up each project: a short retrospective on what worked, what dragged, what to avoid. it need not be formal.
- a consistent filing logic for everything, so that finding an old asset takes seconds and not an archaeological dig.
- an experiment in constraint: build a whole identity from a very limited set of shapes, or a very limited palette, and see how far it stretches.
- a reading list outside design. history, ecology, cooking, fiction. the best visual ideas tend to come from somewhere unrelated.
- a better system for capturing stray thoughts when they appear. a notebook, a voice memo, anything with no friction, because the thought is gone by the time an app finishes opening.

loose reminders

- money admin, paperwork, and filing deserve a regular slot of their own so they stop intruding on creative hours.
- keep receipts and records somewhere obvious and consistent.
- check in with the accountant early on questions, since vague worries cost more than quick conversations.
- keep the workspace comfortable. good light, a decent chair, a clear desk. small physical things change the quality of attention more than software does.
- remember why the studio exists at all: to make careful, considered work for people who value it, on terms that keep the maker sane.

more thoughts to add as they surface. this page is meant to stay loose.
