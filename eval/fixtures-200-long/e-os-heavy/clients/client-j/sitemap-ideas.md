# Client J: sitemap ideas

working notes, loose

**approach**

Start from the visitor, not from the pile of things the business wants to say. Most people arrive with a small practical question and a short supply of patience. The structure should answer that question before they have to go looking, and everything else can sit a little further back.

Think in jobs rather than pages. What is somebody trying to get done when they land here? Find out what's available, check whether the place suits them, work out how to reach it, decide whether to trust it. Pages are just containers for those jobs, and sometimes a job doesn't need its own page at all.

Keep the labels plain. Clever naming looks nice in a deck and costs the visitor a moment of confusion. If a label needs explaining, it's probably the wrong label.

Treat the structure as a hypothesis. It's a guess about how people think, and it gets better when it meets real behavior. Hold it loosely until somebody who isn't me has poked at it.

**process**

- Gather the raw material before drawing anything: what the business actually has, in what shape, and how much of it is in good enough condition to show. Photos, copy, signage, stacks of old flyers, screenshots of reviews. The content decides the structure more often than the other way around.
- Ask the client about their regulars. Who walks in, what do they ask, what do they grumble about. Those questions are a free outline.
- Sketch on paper or a whiteboard in loose boxes and arrows. Keep it ugly on purpose so nobody gets attached to a layout before the structure is right.
- Walk the map as a visitor on a phone with a thumb and a bad connection. Then walk it as a skeptical visitor on a laptop who wants proof.
- Write real headings before any visual design, so the layout gets built around actual words instead of filler text.
- Present the map as a story ("somebody turns up wanting this, here's where they go") rather than only as a diagram. Diagrams invite nitpicking over boxes. Stories invite feedback on the experience.
- Leave room for things that change often, like announcements, specials, and small updates. A structure with no place for those ends up with them bolted on awkwardly.
- Keep the working notes untidy and searchable. A pile of half-formed thoughts is more useful than a polished summary written too early.

**keep in mind**

- Fewer top-level destinations is usually kinder, but cramming everything under vague umbrella headings just moves the hunting somewhere else.
- Anything involving a transaction deserves extra care. The moment money or personal details enter the picture, the stakes rise: trust cues, clear confirmation, what happens when something goes wrong, who deals with it behind the scenes. A feature that looks simple as a box on a map can hide a whole operational burden for a small business. Worth asking who will actually be tending it in the ordinary running of things.
- Small businesses are rarely staffed for constant upkeep. Whatever gets built should be something the owner can keep accurate without dreading it. Stale information is worse than missing information.
- The small screen is probably how most people will meet this. Design for it as the real thing, not a squeezed afterthought.
- Accessibility isn't a polish step. Contrast, text size, keyboard navigation, meaningful link text, sensible heading order. Cheap to build in, miserable to retrofit.
- Search engines and humans want similar things: clear headings, honest descriptions, pages that each have a purpose. If pages compete to say the same thing, merge them or differentiate them.
- Practical local details matter more than designers like to admit: where it is, when it's open, how to find the door, where to park. People hunt for these constantly. Put them where a hurried person would look.
- Be wary of assumptions about what the client's customers want. Ask, observe, and check against whatever evidence exists, like reviews and the questions people already send.
- Keep a list of what was deliberately left out and why, so the reasoning survives when somebody asks later why there isn't a page for a particular thing.
- Photos carry a huge share of the first impression. Weak photography can sink a good structure, and strong photography can forgive a mediocre one. Worth raising early with the client rather than discovering it late.
- Words on a site are a design material. Tone, length, and rhythm affect how the whole thing feels. Short and warm usually beats thorough and stiff.

**open-ended ideas**

- What if the home page were more of a signpost than a brochure? Barely any text, a few strong paths, and a clear statement of what the place is.
- A living area that shows what's current, such as what's available right now and what has changed lately, acting as the front door for returning visitors.
- Story fragments scattered through the site instead of a lone biography page. A line about the people, a photo of the workspace, a note on where things come from, tucked in at the moments where doubt tends to creep in.
- A footer that earns its keep: practical details, a map link, ways to reach somebody, plain-language policies written like a human wrote them.
- A lightweight shortcut for regulars, like a bookmarkable link or a remembered preference, so they can skip the introduction they no longer need.
- Try writing the whole structure as a conversation: question, answer, follow-up question. If the conversation feels awkward, the map probably is too.
- Test with a stranger by showing only the labels, no design and no content, and asking where they'd expect to find various things. Their hesitations are the useful part.
- Imagine the site when it's nearly empty, with thin content and few assets. A structure that depends on abundance will look sad with little. It's worth sketching a pared-back variant and a generous variant, then comparing how each handles a visitor in a rush.
- Echo the site's vocabulary in print and signage so the physical space and the screen speak the same language.
- Large photographs as doorways rather than text links. Pretty, but they can hide meaning, so test whether people actually understand where they lead.
- A "start here" path for newcomers that quietly teaches the structure as they move through it.
- Seasonal or situational variations of the front page, driven by what's most useful to visitors at that point, without turning the whole structure upside down.
- A short plain-language note near anything that asks for personal details, explaining what happens next. Reassurance is cheap and people notice its absence.

**loose questions**

- How much of a visitor's journey happens before they ever reach the site? A map listing, a review, a friend's text. The site may be a later stop than anybody assumes, which changes what it needs to do on arrival.
- What does the business want people to feel in the moment they land?
- Is there anything the owner is quietly tired of explaining? That's often the best content.
- What would a visitor wish they'd known sooner?
- Where might the structure break if the business changes direction, adds something, or drops something?
- Which parts of the process can be shown to the client in a rough state without it looking unfinished?
- What would a competitor's site get wrong that this one could get right, and is that actually a difference visitors care about?
- Who else touches this site: staff, suppliers, friends who share links? Their needs are easy to forget and they tend to be the loudest when something's broken.
- If the whole thing had to be explained over the phone to somebody who couldn't see it, would the structure hold up?

**small reminders to self**

Sleep on structural worries before acting on them. Revisit the rough sketches after a break, because they often look wiser or sillier than expected. Keep the client's own words close, since their phrases for things are usually better than anything invented in a studio. And when a debate about structure drags on, go back to a real visitor with a real question and see which version serves them better.
