# Client S

general working notes

**getting started**

before anything gets drawn, i try to pull the brief out of the client's head and into plain language. not a formal document, just a loose back and forth about what the piece is meant to do for them. who will see it, where it will hang or scroll past, what a passer-by should feel when they catch it from across the street. if i can't say that in a plain sentence, i'm not ready to start.

questions that keep paying off:
- what does the audience already know, and what do they assume?
- what would make this feel wrong, even if it looked nice?
- is there a visual habit in the community that this should echo, or deliberately break from?
- who has the final say, and who just has opinions?
- what is the piece competing with for attention, and where?

people often can't answer these straight away, and that's fine. the half-answers still tell me where the worry lives.

**gathering references**

reference hunting is where it's easy to wander off and come back with nothing. keep it loose and keep it moving. pull things that aren't illustration at all: old signage, textile patterns, packaging, hand-painted shop lettering, botanical plates, matchbox art, faded handbills, children's drawings. the goal is a feel, not something to copy.

pin everything on a board, then step back and squint. the common thread usually shows up by itself. if the board looks like a copy of somebody else's work, widen the net. if it looks like a mess, there's probably a mood in there that hasn't been named yet. name it out loud, even if it sounds silly.

**sketching**

start with rough thumbnails. make them small and ugly. shapes before detail, always. a composition that reads as a silhouette will survive any amount of later fiddling, and one that only works when it's polished never really worked.

things that help:
- flip the sketch upside down and in a mirror; balance problems jump out immediately.
- squint until the image blurs and see what's left.
- push for variety. if every thumbnail is the same layout in disguise, stop and force a radically different arrangement: a tight crop, a huge empty area, a strange viewpoint, a tiny subject in a big space.
- keep the rejected ones. they're rarely wrong, just early or in the wrong job.

try not to fall in love with a sketch too soon. the idea that feels clever at the start often relies on a trick that gets tired quickly. the quieter idea usually has more staying power.

**color and type**

pick a palette early but leave room to cheat it. limited palettes feel intentional; a limited palette plus a loud accent feels alive. check values in grayscale before trusting hue. if the image falls apart without color, the structure is weak, and color is being asked to rescue it.

think about how the piece will actually be seen: coated stock, rough stock, a screen at arm's length, a wall that gets sun and rain. colors shift in every one of those, but contrast holds up. build the image on contrast and let hue be the flavor.

type should be part of the picture rather than a label stuck on afterward. hand-lettering is worth considering when the subject is warm or local, because it carries a human fingerprint that a catalog face can't. leave breathing room around anything essential. test legibility from far away and at a tiny size. anything that matters should survive being squinted at, and anything that doesn't can afford to be decorative.

**working through feedback**

feedback is usually feelings dressed up as instructions. "make the logo bigger" often means "i'm not sure people will notice this." ask what worry sits under the note, then solve the worry, which is sometimes a different fix entirely.

- keep early rounds rough so people react to ideas rather than polish.
- show process when it builds trust; hold it back when it invites nitpicking about things that aren't settled.
- when feedback conflicts, don't average it. find the shared goal underneath and aim there.
- keep a plain record of what was asked and why, so later conversations don't drift or turn into memory contests.
- be kind to the person giving notes. they're usually carrying pressure from people i never meet.

**files and handoff**

name the layers and keep them tidy, because future-me is a stranger who has forgotten everything. save a flat version, a transparent version, and a print-ready version with generous bleed. keep source files together with fonts, references, and a short note about what's what. export for the worst display situation, not the best one.

it's worth leaving the folder in a state where a stranger could open it and understand the logic without a call. this matters most when a request comes back a long while later and the context has evaporated.

**solo studio habits**

- step away from the screen when stuck. walk, wash dishes, draw badly with the wrong hand.
- keep a scrap file for ideas that didn't fit. they come back in other jobs wearing different clothes.
- protect the odd parts of the work. they're the reason people hire a small studio rather than grab a template.
- keep the admin side tidy and boring so it doesn't leak into creative energy.
- jot down what went well and what dragged while it's still fresh, then leave it alone.
- be wary of working in a vacuum. even a rough glance from a friend with no design background is useful, since they react like the public does.
- rest the eyes. fresh looks catch errors that tired ones forgive.

**things to keep in mind**

- the brief is a starting point, not a cage, but breaking it needs a reason that can be explained in plain words.
- simple is not the same as easy. the hardest part is usually deciding what to leave out.
- local work has a special responsibility. neighbors will see it, remember it, and have feelings about it. avoid clichés that flatten a place into a postcard.
- hand-made marks give warmth, but only if they're deliberate. wobble for its own sake reads as carelessness.
- readability beats cleverness in anything meant to be seen in passing.
- credit and acknowledgment matter, to collaborators and to the community the work belongs to.
- a good piece should still feel fresh after the tenth glance, not just the first, so leave a little mystery or a small detail to discover later.

**open-ended ideas**

- build a small library of hand-made textures: ink blots, torn paper, dry brush, scanned fabric, pressed leaves. reuse them across jobs so the work shares a fingerprint without repeating itself.
- start a personal series made purely for fun, with no client and no purpose, to keep the hand loose and the taste honest.
- experiment with limiting tools on purpose: only a blunt pencil, only cut paper shapes, only a brush far too large for the job. constraints invent styles.
- keep a visual diary of local details: doorways, hand-painted signs, odd plants, overheard phrases, the way light hits a particular wall.
- try printing with deliberately misregistered layers and see whether the happy accidents can be steered.
- sketch out a plain-language guide for clients explaining how the process tends to unfold, so expectations get set early and gently. maybe with drawings instead of bullet points.
- explore how a still image could have a quiet moving twin for screens, with only the smallest motion: a flicker, a drift, a slow breath.
- collect a vocabulary of "feelings" with matching visual moves, like warmth, nostalgia, mischief, calm, and see how each maps to shape, color, and texture.

**questions to chew on**

- what makes a piece feel truly local without leaning on cliché?
- how much detail is enough before an image stops breathing?
- when does a recognizable style turn into a rut?
- is a deliberately unfinished edge ever the right call, and how would a client feel about it?
- what would this studio make if nobody were paying, and why isn't more of that work visible?
- how can the process be made lighter without making the results thinner?

some days the best move is simply to make a mark and see what it asks for next. the rest is editing.
