# Client I: call

general working notes, stuff to keep nearby

**how i like to approach a job**

i like to soak up the subject before drawing anything. the urge is to open a blank canvas and start pushing shapes around, but the shapes come out generic when i haven't earned them yet. better to collect language first: the words the owner reaches for, the words they flinch at, the stuff that makes them wince. a reaction to something wrong is usually clearer than a description of something right, so showing rough wrong things early is a cheap way to learn.

when somebody asks for a feeling, translate it into behavior. a mood word isn't an instruction. ask what it does on the page. does it get loud through scale, through contrast, through sheer restraint, through a color relationship that feels slightly illegal? jot down what each mood would look like in practice and see which translation makes the person lean forward. that lean is the signal.

i try to separate finding the idea from dressing the idea. the finding happens in pencil, in ugly thumbnails, in bad collage, in pushing words around until a phrase clicks. the dressing happens after, and it goes faster when the idea is solid. if i catch myself polishing something i can't explain in a plain sentence, that's a sign to back up.

**process, roughly**

- gather references, but also gather anti-references. what must this absolutely not look like? the avoid pile sharpens the aim as much as the inspiration pile does.
- sketch fast and wide. quantity of rough thinking beats quality of early thinking, because the good move is rarely where you expected it.
- name each direction in a sentence before building it out. if the sentence is mushy, the direction is mushy.
- build the strongest version of every direction instead of hedging. a watered-down route makes the stronger route look riskier than it is.
- make alternatives truly different from each other. same idea in different outfits isn't a choice, it's an illusion of a choice.
- stress test everything. shrink it until it's barely legible, blow it up until it's absurd, strip the color, view it on a cheap screen, glance at it while walking past. whatever survives is the real design.
- leave it overnight when the schedule allows, then look with fresh eyes and cut whatever feels like decoration.

**things to keep in mind**

- being a small studio means there's no handoff, no colleague who will ask "why did you do that?" so i have to ask myself, and write the answer down. future me is basically a stranger and deserves a paper trail.
- lead presentations with the problem, not the pretty. when the owner sees the problem framed the way i see it, the solution lands as obvious rather than arbitrary.
- don't over-explain. if a piece needs a paragraph of apology, it isn't working. a short rationale is enough, and the work should do the rest.
- bold is not the same as loud. bold can be a huge empty space, a single harsh edge, a typeface that refuses to apologize. loud is just volume. keep checking which one i'm actually making.
- cute is a trap that sneaks in through rounded corners, friendly mascots, and soft pastel safety. watch for reflexes that sand things down without being asked.
- when a person makes the final call alone, they carry the whole weight of it emotionally. make that easier: clear differences, clear tradeoffs, no jargon fog.
- ask what they would defend at a dinner party. it surfaces conviction faster than asking what they like.
- keep notes on how each person gives feedback. some react in the moment, some need to sleep on it, some speak in metaphors. adapting to that saves a lot of friction.
- scope creep arrives politely. a tiny "while you're at it" can swallow a week of energy. notice it early, name it kindly, and keep the boundaries visible.
- never fall in love with a sketch so hard that it blocks better ideas. fondness is useful for energy, dangerous for judgment.
- rest matters. the work gets worse in a tired brain in ways that are invisible from inside.

**how to talk about work**

plain words beat design vocabulary. "this feels heavy, in a good way" lands better than a lecture on visual hierarchy. if a term would make a regular person's eyes glaze over, swap it. describe what the thing does to a viewer, not what it is made of.

when presenting alternatives, say out loud what each direction gives up. every strong choice costs something, and being upfront about the cost builds trust. it also keeps the owner from feeling tricked later.

when feedback comes back vague, treat it as a map with missing roads. ask for a comparison: closer to this, further from that. people who can't articulate a preference can almost always point at examples.

**open-ended ideas to chew on**

- start a swipe file of ugly things that nonetheless work. what makes a clumsy poster magnetic? what makes a technically perfect one forgettable?
- build a cliché kill list for each industry that comes through the door. if every competitor uses a certain shape or color, that's open territory for being different.
- try constraint exercises: type only, shape only, a single cropped photo, a palette stolen from something unrelated like a rusted machine or a bruised sky.
- draw with the wrong hand or the wrong tool for a while, just to knock loose habits.
- keep a graveyard folder of abandoned directions. a rejected idea for one project is sometimes the perfect answer to a different problem later.
- think about how work ages. what will this look like when it's scuffed, faded, stretched, printed badly, or copied by a rival? bold things should survive abuse.
- consider building a small bank of reusable questions for kickoff conversations, the kind that open people up rather than produce stock answers.
- experiment with presenting rough work in a way that invites tearing it apart, rather than polished work that invites polite nodding.
- look at what other crafts do. architects, cooks, tailors, sign painters, typesetters. each has a way of dealing with constraints that design can borrow.
- explore sound and motion as a gut check. if the identity were a noise, what noise? if it moved, how? answers often reveal the personality faster than static comps do.
- maybe write a short manifesto for the studio, only for myself, about what kind of work is worth making and what kind is worth turning down.

**loose reminders**

keep the pencil nearby. keep the references messy. keep the rationale written. be generous with the owner and stubborn with the work. when in doubt, make the thing that scares me a little, then check whether it scares the right way.

and remember that most good ideas feel slightly wrong at the start. if it feels instantly comfortable, somebody has probably already made it.
