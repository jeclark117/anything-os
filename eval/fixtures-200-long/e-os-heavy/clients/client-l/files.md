# Client L: files

working notes, general

**approach**

the work goes better when the problem gets stated plainly before anything gets drawn. what is this for, who is looking at it, what do they do right after they see it. if those answers are fuzzy, the visuals end up carrying a job they can't do. i try to write the problem in plain sentences, and if the sentences sound like a brochure, the thinking isn't finished.

most of the useful decisions happen early and quietly, in the gaps between "what they asked for" and "what they actually need." those two things overlap, but rarely line up perfectly. the gap is where the interesting work lives.

a rough sketch on paper still beats a polished screen for finding direction. the screen invites fiddling with details before the structure has earned them.

**process, loosely**

- start wide. gather references, but also gather anti-references, the things that feel wrong and why. the reasons are usually more useful than the images.
- make the ugly version early. a bad layout that exists teaches more than a perfect layout that's still in my head.
- keep a running list of questions for the client rather than asking each as it pops up. batching them respects their attention and makes me notice which questions actually matter.
- work in grayscale for as long as possible. color can hide weak hierarchy.
- squint at everything. if the important thing doesn't survive blur, it isn't important enough on the page.
- step away from a piece before judging it. a walk, a meal, a different task. fresh eyes catch spacing problems that tired eyes have made peace with.
- test the work in the context where it will live, not just on a blank canvas. a logo looks different on a crowded shelf, a tiny screen, a stamped surface, a cluttered inbox.

**on feedback**

feedback tends to arrive as solutions ("make it bigger," "try blue") when the real information is the feeling underneath ("i can't find it," "this seems cold"). worth translating before reacting. asking what a comment is trying to fix usually opens up better answers than the literal request would have.

also worth remembering that people often react most strongly to what's familiar. a safe-looking option can win a room simply because nobody has to imagine anything. presenting work with a short story about why it looks the way it does helps the unfamiliar option get a fair hearing.

**things to keep in mind**

- a constraint is a gift more often than a nuisance. a tight palette, a limited set of type styles, a strange format: these shrink the decision space and sharpen the result.
- consistency matters more than cleverness. a system that holds together across a dozen touchpoints beats a brilliant gesture that only works in the hero spot.
- whitespace is a design element, not leftover room. it takes courage to leave it alone.
- legibility first, personality second, and personality can still be loud once legibility is handled.
- accessibility is part of quality, not a separate pass. contrast, size, and clear structure help everybody, including the person squinting at a phone in bright light.
- a project's mood is set by its first impressions. the tone of early emails, the way a proposal is laid out, the speed of replies: all of it is design too.
- don't over-explain the work. if a piece needs a paragraph to justify it, either the piece or the paragraph needs revisiting.
- trends age quickly; fundamentals don't. grid, rhythm, contrast, proportion.
- leave room for accidents. some of the best moves come from a misplaced layer or a failed export that looked better than the plan.

**working alone**

being a studio of one means every role lands on the same desk: strategist, designer, producer, bookkeeper, janitor. the risk is that the loudest role wins on any given afternoon. when the producer is shouting, the work gets rushed; when the designer is shouting, the schedule slips. a bit of internal balance helps, and so does naming which hat is on while making a call.

isolation has a cost too. nobody pushes back in the room, so assumptions go unchallenged longer than they should. a habit of explaining a project out loud, even to an empty room, exposes holes surprisingly well. trusted peers outside the studio are valuable for the same reason, as long as they're asked a specific question rather than "what do you think?"

energy is a resource to be managed, not just hours. some work wants a clear head and long stretches; other work is fine in scraps. matching the task to the energy available avoids a lot of grinding.

**handoff and housekeeping**

- name layers and components as though a stranger will open the file later, because eventually a stranger will, and that stranger is often the future version of me who forgot everything.
- keep working versions and final versions visibly apart so nobody grabs the wrong one.
- write a short plain note alongside delivered work explaining how the pieces are meant to fit together and what's deliberately flexible versus fixed.
- leave the project in a state where someone else could pick it up cold. good handoffs are a form of respect for the client and for whoever comes next.
- record why a choice was made, not only what was chosen. the reasoning is the part that gets lost.

**open-ended ideas**

- a personal library of failed directions, tagged by what went wrong. patterns might show up in what keeps failing.
- a habit of redrawing something admired from memory, then comparing, to see what the memory kept and what it dropped. what survives is probably the essence.
- a recurring side exercise with no client: one constraint, a short burst of making, no polish. purely to keep the hands loose.
- try presenting work in a different order than usual: start with the weakest option and finish with the strongest, or reverse it, and watch how the reaction changes.
- what would a project look like if the deliverable were the process itself, documented as it unfolds? might be a good way to show clients the thinking.
- a checklist of questions to ask at the start of any brief, refined slowly by noting which questions turned out to matter in hindsight.
- explore how sound, motion, or texture could enter projects that are traditionally only visual. even small touches might change how something feels.
- consider what a studio looks like when it's deliberately small on purpose. what does that smallness make possible that a bigger shop can't do?
- an annual-ish reflection ritual, loosely structured: what felt alive, what felt like obligation, what was avoided and why.
- look sideways for inspiration. signage, packaging, hand-lettered notices, old manuals, nature. the best references often come from nowhere near design.

**a few loose reminders**

good work is usually quieter than expected. the urge to add is strong; the discipline is in taking away until only what's necessary remains, then checking whether even that is too much.

curiosity is the actual engine. skills can be taught and tools can be swapped, but the habit of looking closely and asking why something works is what keeps the output from going stale.

and be kind to the early drafts. they're supposed to be awkward.
