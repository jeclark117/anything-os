# Client K: invoice

- Invoice 16, $1,000 deposit
- Paid 2026-10-08
- Second half at delivery

general working notes

**approach**

Most of the job is figuring out what not to make. A brief usually shows up as a pile of wants, and the useful move is sorting them into what the project is actually for and what is decoration added because somebody got nervous about leaving it out. Keep asking what the thing needs to do for a person who meets it cold, with no context and no patience.

- Start from the problem, not the look. The look tends to fall out of the constraints if they're named properly.
- Say the goal back in plain words before sketching anything. If the client winces, better to find out early.
- Rough beats polished in the early stages. Polished work invites feedback about polish, and that's rarely the conversation that's needed.
- Keep a running list of questions that come up mid-work and batch them, rather than pinging the client every time something is unclear.
- When the brief and the budget of attention don't match, say so out loud. Quiet mismatches turn into resentment later.

**process**

Work happens in loose passes. Gather references, hoard far too many, then cull hard. Mood boards are mostly useful for finding out what the client reacts against, which is often more informative than what they say they like. A strong "no, not that" saves a lot of wandering.

After gathering, sketch badly and quickly. Paper still wins for this because it's hard to fuss over. Then move into the screen only when there's a direction worth refining. The worst habit is opening the computer too soon and polishing something that was never a good idea.

Some things that help the middle stretch:

- Work in layers of fidelity. Structure, then type and spacing, then color, then detail. Skipping ahead makes the earlier layers brittle.
- Leave a piece alone before judging it. Fresh eyes catch the awkward spacing and the clever idea that isn't actually clear.
- Print or enlarge things, shrink them, squint at them, flip them. Each view lies in a different way.
- Keep alternate directions alive a bit longer than feels comfortable. Killing options too early narrows the work.

**feedback**

Feedback comes in layers too. There's what the client says, what they mean, and what they'd be happy with if they saw it. Those aren't the same. "Make the logo bigger" sometimes means "I don't feel seen." Sometimes it means the logo is too small.

- Ask what's bothering them before reaching for a fix.
- Separate taste from function. Taste is negotiable in a friendly way; function usually isn't.
- Write down the feedback exactly as it was given, then the interpretation underneath. Later it's easy to forget which was which.
- When feedback contradicts itself, point at the contradiction gently and let them resolve it. Resolving it silently on their behalf feels helpful but can backfire.
- Be careful with the urge to defend. A defended idea gets stubborn fast.

**keeping the paperwork honest**

The business side deserves the same care as the design side. Clear scope up front prevents most of the arguments that wear a studio down. What counts as finished, what counts as a revision versus a new request, and what happens when the project wanders: these are worth putting in plain language before work starts, not after it's already uncomfortable.

- Records should be boring and findable. A future self hunting for a detail at a bad moment will be grateful.
- Notes about a conversation are worth more when they're written right after it, while the tone is still fresh.
- Keep a habit of confirming things in writing, even if the conversation was friendly. Friendly conversations get remembered differently by each side.
- Money talk goes better when it's treated as ordinary. Awkwardness is contagious.
- A tidy handoff at the end, with files named sensibly and a short explanation of how to use them, counts as part of the work.

**things to keep in mind**

Solo work means every role is covered by the same brain: designer, bookkeeper, salesperson, janitor. Context switching is expensive and doesn't announce itself. Grouping similar tasks helps more than willpower does.

- Energy is a resource, not just time. Some kinds of work need the good energy and some can be done on fumes. Matching them is a quiet superpower.
- Saying no keeps the work good. A studio that takes everything ends up making forgettable things for everybody.
- Boundaries with clients are easier to hold when they're set early and warmly, not enforced late and sharply.
- Taste is a muscle. It gets better with looking, not just making. Feed it on purpose.
- Rest isn't a reward for finishing. Tired work shows.
- Old projects are a reference library. Looking back at what held up and what aged badly is a cheap education.

**open-ended ideas**

Loose thoughts, none of them settled, some probably bad:

- A small private archive of "almost" work: directions that were close but not chosen, annotated with why. Could turn into a source of fresh starting points and a record of how taste shifts.
- A plain-language explainer that travels with each project, written for the client's future colleagues who weren't in the room. Might reduce the "why is it like this?" questions that arrive long after the work is out.
- A checklist for the very start of a project that's more about listening than logistics: what does success look like, who will be annoyed if this goes wrong, what has been tried before.
- Experiment with showing fewer options but explaining each more fully. Does that raise the quality of feedback, or does it just make clients feel boxed in?
- A habit of ending a project with a short reflection while the details are still warm: what was easy, what dragged, what surprised. Even a messy paragraph might be enough.
- Trade critique with other independent designers now and then. A trusted outside eye is hard to replace, and it might help to hear how others handle awkward client moments.
- Think about what the studio is known for, if anything. Is that the same as what it wants to be known for? The gap is worth sitting with.
- Maybe write some of this up as public notes, rough and honest. Not as marketing, more as a way of thinking out loud and attracting the kind of client who likes thinking that way.

**loose ends**

Some things don't fit anywhere yet. How to price curiosity. Whether to keep a visible archive of failures. What a good working rhythm looks like when projects overlap and none of them cooperates. How much process to show clients versus how much to keep behind the curtain. These stay open on purpose; closing them too fast would probably just mean closing them wrong.
