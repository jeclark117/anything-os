# Client D

working notes, general

**approach**

start with the room, not the file. sit in the space if possible and watch where people's eyes land, where they hesitate, where they squint. a menu gets read in dim light with a drink in hand and a conversation going on. signage gets read from across the street by a distracted person who is probably walking. neither gets studied. design for the glance, then reward the people who look longer.

figure out what the place actually is before touching type. not what the owner says in pitch mode, but what regulars say when they describe it to a friend. the gap between those descriptions is usually where the real brief lives.

the menu and the signage should feel like the same voice at different volumes. signage is the call across the room, the menu is the conversation at the table. same personality, different distance.

**process, loosely**

- gather the real artifacts first: current menus (especially the stained, scribbled-on ones), photos of the storefront in bad weather and good, any old signage that got left in a back room
- ask the staff what annoys them. servers know which items get misread, which section gets skipped, which column confuses people
- sketch on paper before the screen opens. thumbnails, ugly on purpose. screen work makes everything look finished far too early
- build the type system before the layout. try a combination at the smallest size it will ever appear and the largest, and see if it holds together at the extremes
- print rough mockups at actual size and carry them around. tape them to a wall, stand across the room, spill water on them
- show work in context, not as flat rectangles. a menu in a hand on a real table sells itself better than a floating artboard
- keep a log of why choices were made, in plain words. useful later when someone asks "why is it like this" and the answer has been forgotten

**menu: things to keep in mind**

- hierarchy over decoration. diners scan for category, then dish, then description, then cost. let the layout follow that path instead of fighting it
- the content keeps changing. items come and go, names get longer, specials get added in pen. build in slack. a layout that only works when the content is perfect will look broken the moment reality arrives
- paper and finish matter more than they seem to. can it be wiped down, does it survive grease, does it curl, does it feel cheap or cared for in the hand
- ask the printer about limits early, because discovering them late is expensive in every sense
- dietary and allergen marks need to be consistent and obvious. small symbols that don't need a legend are better than a key at the bottom nobody reads
- legibility for tired and older eyes in low light. contrast, size, line spacing, and not setting small text in a thin weight
- currency symbols and dotted leader lines are often just noise. worth testing a version without them
- the digital version, if there is one, should share the same hierarchy rather than being a shrunken copy of the print
- descriptions deserve the owner's voice, edited lightly. a menu written like a legal document tastes worse

**signage: things to keep in mind**

- judge it from the farthest realistic distance. blur the image and see what survives. if the name disappears, the design isn't finished
- weather, glare off glass, fading from light, grime. imagine it dirty and slightly neglected, because that is its normal state
- artificial light changes color. check how it looks lit and unlit, and whether the character changes between the two
- local rules around signs vary and tend to surface late. permits, size limits, and what can hang over a sidewalk are worth finding out about early
- talk to whoever fabricates it before the design hardens. they will have opinions about minimum stroke thickness, fine detail, and what falls apart at scale
- mounting and structure: what it hangs from, what the wall is made of, who is allowed to drill into it
- think in terms of touchpoints rather than a sign. the window, the door, the awning, the sidewalk board, the takeaway bag, the receipt. the system should make each of those easy to extend even if they aren't all being redesigned now

**working with the owner**

- the owner knows food and people. the designer knows layout and legibility. keep conversation in the owner's language: how it should feel, who it's for, what a guest should say on the way out
- show fewer directions with clearer reasoning rather than a wall of options. a wall of options invites choosing by mood
- ask for real content early. final wording, final item names. placeholder text hides problems until they are painful
- feedback usually arrives as feelings: too busy, too cold, too fancy. translate before acting. ask what they are reacting to
- watch for scope creeping in dressed as small favors. a small favor repeated enough becomes a separate project
- people get attached to whatever they saw earliest. present rough work as rough, and avoid showing anything that isn't ready to be loved
- be honest when a request works against legibility. it lands better with a quick demonstration than with an argument

**open-ended ideas**

- the menu as an object: a fold, a texture, a tactile detail that people remember and mention to others
- a zone designed for handwriting, or a stamp or stencil, so the specials look intentional when staff improvise. the system should survive being used by tired people at a busy hour
- type that borrows from the building itself: old lettering on the facade, tile patterns, door hardware, the proportions of the windows
- a palette pulled from the food: char, herb, sauce, crust. test whether the colors make the food look better or worse under real lighting
- small illustrated marks for sections instead of text headers. risk: cute. reward: faster scanning. test it
- a short note on the back of the menu in the owner's own voice, like house rules or a thank-you. low cost, high warmth
- signage that reveals a second layer up close, a detail only visible when you're at the door. a reward for slowing down (maybe too precious, worth sketching anyway)
- a plain-language guide for the staff on how to use the system without breaking it. what to change freely, what to leave alone
- reference material from outside restaurants altogether: packaging, labels, transit wayfinding, old hand-painted shopfronts, hardware store shelving. restaurants copying restaurants gets samey fast
- a version of the identity that works in complete darkness, with only a lit window and a name

**questions worth asking repeatedly**

- what does a guest need to know at a glance?
- what happens when the paper is stained, the sign is dirty, and the lights are low?
- is this memorable, or just tidy?
- who maintains this when the designer isn't around?
- what would get cut if it had to be simpler? cut that, then see if anything was missed
- does it still feel like the place if the logo is covered up?

**general reminders**

restraint is underrated. leave space. not every surface needs a treatment, and not every item deserves a flourish. the best menus tend to feel like a calm person talking, and the best signs feel like they were always there.

check the work against the room, not against the screen. the room is the real client.

keep a loose folder for stray observations: a good bit of lettering on a delivery truck, a receipt layout that was surprisingly clear, a sign that failed in an interesting way. most of the useful ideas come from there, not from the brief.

and sleep on type choices when possible. a combination that feels right in the heat of the moment can feel wrong after a break, and the reverse happens too.

general working notes

**approach**

the work goes better when i treat the brief as a conversation instead of a document. people rarely know what they want visually, but they know what they hate, what makes them nervous, and what they wish their customers understood. those three things are worth more than any moodboard. ask about the nervous stuff early, while nobody is defensive yet.

- find out what the business actually feels like from the inside. who works there, what the room smells like, what gets complained about on a bad shift. good identity work usually comes from a true detail, not a clever concept.
- look at what the neighbors in the same category are doing, then deliberately look somewhere else for ideas. signage, packaging, old printed ephemera, hand-painted shop fronts, book jackets. borrowing from outside the category keeps things from looking like everything inside it.
- keep a short running list of feel words. warm, dry, brisk, generous, a bit cheeky. when a layout starts drifting, check it against the list instead of arguing with it.
- write down what the project is NOT. the exclusions sometimes clarify more than the goals do.
- ask what they'd be embarrassed to see on the finished piece. it's a weirdly effective way to find the edges of their taste.

**process**

sketch on paper before touching the screen, even badly. the screen makes everything look finished too early, and finished-looking things are hard to throw away. paper keeps it loose, and loose is where the surprises are.

go wide before going narrow. generate rough directions, more than feels reasonable, and let the weak ones die quickly. the early ideas are usually the obvious ones. the interesting stuff tends to show up after the obvious stuff is exhausted, which is annoying but reliable.

type before color. if the layout works in black and white, color becomes seasoning instead of structure. then squint at it. whatever survives the blur is what people will see from across a room, and that should be the right thing. if the wrong thing survives, the hierarchy is lying.

Restaurant. Wants a menu and signage redesign. Their budget is $6,500.

Wants to start spring 2027. Send examples of past menu work.

use real copy as soon as possible. placeholder text hides problems like awkward line lengths, a heading far longer than expected, a description that needs trimming. if the real words aren't available, write plausible ones, ugly and specific beats smooth and generic.

print things at actual size, hold them at arm's length, pin them to a wall, leave the room, walk back in. fresh eyes are a tool and they cost nothing. a layout that looked great zoomed way in on a screen can fall apart in the hand, and the reverse happens too.

when presenting, show fewer directions with more conviction rather than a buffet. explain the reasoning in plain words tied to the client's own goals, not design jargon. show work in context, mocked up on a table, on a wall, on a phone-sized screen, because abstract rectangles on a blank page hide whether anything actually works.

keep files tidy. sensible names, a clear trail of versions, fonts and assets gathered somewhere they can't vanish. the future version of me opening a folder is basically a stranger who wasn't there, and should be treated with some kindness.

after a round of work, jot down why things look the way they do. a few plain sentences are enough. it saves a lot of reconstructing later when someone asks why a particular choice was made.

**things to keep in mind**

- scope creeps in quietly. small asks that sound like nothing add up into a different project. it helps to have a polite habit of naming what's included and what would be a separate conversation, ideally before it comes up rather than after.
- feedback is data about a feeling, not a design instruction. when a person says make the logo bigger, they usually mean it doesn't feel confident yet. dig for the feeling, then solve that, which often has nothing to do with size.
- restraint is a feature. most pieces improve when something is removed. when stuck, try taking away instead of adding.
- accessibility isn't a bolt-on. contrast, legibility at small sizes, how things look on a cheap screen, in bad lighting, or printed on rough paper. if it only works in ideal conditions, it doesn't really work.
- print is unforgiving. colors shift, thin lines vanish, paper soaks up ink and changes everything. ask the printer questions early, and get proofs on the real material whenever it's possible.
- a solo studio means nobody else catches mistakes. build in habits: read the text aloud, proofread backwards, step away and return, ask a trusted friend to glance at it with no context and say what they notice.
- protect energy. creative work is lumpy, some stretches flow and some are a slog. forcing it rarely helps, and a walk usually solves what staring doesn't.
- keep the portfolio honest. show process, not only polished end results, and be clear about what part was actually mine versus what was handed over.
- a good referral is still good service. a poor-fit project handled gracefully can build more trust than a mediocre job pushed through.
- clients are usually nervous about the process, not just the outcome. clear communication about what happens next, even when nothing exciting is happening, goes a long way.
- remember that the people using the finished thing never saw the brief. it has to work on its own, with nobody there to explain it.

**open-ended ideas**

- build a personal library of type pairings that have worked, with notes on why. use mood tags rather than rules, since rules get brittle and moods travel better.
- scan textures. paper grain, torn edges, ink bleed, fabric, peeling paint. a growing folder of real material to push digital work away from sterile.
- collect physical reference like matchbooks, receipts, tickets, packaging, hand-lettered signs. an archive you can touch beats a folder of screenshots, and the tactile stuff leaks into the work in useful ways.
- write case studies as honest stories, including what went sideways and what got cut. those might be more interesting to prospective clients than glossy summaries.
- a reusable discovery questionnaire, short and written in plain language, that surfaces fears and favorites instead of asking people to describe their style.
- informal critique swaps with other independent designers. trade rough work and blunt reactions, no polish required.
- personal poster experiments with no client, no brief, and nobody to approve them. just a place to try layouts that would feel too risky otherwise.
- a swatch box of paper stocks, foils, and finishes to hand people in person. nothing sells a material choice like letting someone feel it.
- explore designing flexible systems that a small business can extend without needing help each time, templates with guardrails, a few firm rules and a lot of room inside them.
- think about how an identity feels in non-visual ways. how a place sounds when you ring them, how a receipt reads, how an email signs off, how staff greet people at the door. the visuals are only part of what people remember.
- study how old signwriters handled spacing by eye. practice drawing letters by hand to train the instinct that software tends to dull.
- keep a running list of everyday design annoyances, confusing doors, unreadable forms, labels that fight their own packaging. patterns in the list quietly turn into principles.
- try designing something using only constraints chosen at random, like a limited palette or a single typeface family, just to see what the limits force into existence.
- consider what a studio newsletter or small printed zine might look like, not as marketing but as a way to think out loud in public about the craft.

**small reminders**

clarity over cleverness. if it needs a long explanation, it probably needs another pass. the quiet version is often the stronger one. good work is mostly careful attention applied to boring details, plus a willingness to throw away things that were hard to make. and it's fine to leave a problem alone overnight; the answer is frequently waiting when you return.
