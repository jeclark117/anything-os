# Client T: intro

- Client c's owner introduced us 2026-10-12
- Say thanks to client c

general working notes

**approach**

start by getting the problem out of the client's head and onto the table. people usually arrive with a solution already glued to the problem: a logo that looks like this, a site that behaves like that. the useful move is prying them apart until the real need is visible. what's left is usually smaller and stranger than the brief suggested.

questions that help:
- what happens if nothing gets made? the answer shows how much the work matters and to whom.
- what are you embarrassed by right now? that points at where the current thing is failing.
- who has to touch this after it leaves my hands?
- what would make you quietly proud?

keep early conversations loose. a stiff questionnaire gets stiff answers. a wandering chat about what they love, what annoys them, and what they wish customers understood gets the good material.

collect the vocabulary a client uses for their own business and reuse it. when the words in the work sound like their words, the work feels like theirs.

look at what surrounds the project: the people who will handle it, the places it will show up, the rough conditions it has to survive. a design that only works in a clean mockup isn't thought through.

**process**

working on my own means every role is mine: researcher, designer, bookkeeper, janitor of the digital desk. the upside is coherence and speed. the downside is that nobody pushes back, so the pushing back has to be built into the process on purpose.

ways to borrow fresh eyes:
- print it out. view it tiny, view it huge, view it in grayscale, view it upside down.
- explain the work out loud to an empty room. if the explanation goes mumbly, the idea is mumbly.
- show rough work early, before it feels safe. rough work invites real opinions; polished work invites approval, and approval teaches nothing.
- step away from a draft and return after the head has cleared. things that looked brilliant can look embarrassing, and the reverse happens too.

sketch on paper, badly, and keep going past the obvious answers. the obvious ideas are everybody's ideas. the interesting stuff usually shows up after the easy ones are used up and the hand keeps moving anyway. aim for range rather than polish at that stage.

when stuck, change the medium before changing the idea. if the screen is stalling things, go to paper; if paper is stalling, cut shapes out; if everything is stalling, make the ugliest version on purpose and see what it teaches. a related trick: write the headline of the project as if it were a newspaper story, then design backwards from the story.

housekeeping that pays off: name files plainly, keep a scrap folder of rejected ideas (they occasionally become the answer to a different problem), and keep a swipe file of things that caught my eye with a line about why. the why matters more than the image.

**things to keep in mind**

- clients rarely know what they want but almost always know what they dislike. that's workable.
- feedback is information about a reaction, not an instruction. "make it bigger" usually means "it doesn't feel confident." ask what's underneath before moving anything around.
- scope creep arrives quietly, dressed as a small favor or a "while you're in there." it's worth noticing the moment it shows up and naming it kindly, since it's easier to talk about before it becomes a habit.
- trust builds from small proofs: answering plainly, saying what's still unclear, admitting a miss without drama. silence breeds anxiety on the client's side even when the work is going fine.
- taste arguments go nowhere. convert them into purpose arguments: does this help the reader do what they came to do?
- accessibility belongs in the thinking from the start. contrast, legibility, reading order, motion that doesn't make people queasy. retrofitting it is painful and shows.
- type does most of the work, and spacing does most of the rest. color is mood plus signal, so watch for signals that collide with meanings the audience already carries from elsewhere.
- when presenting, lead with the reasoning, then the visuals. otherwise people react to the surface and skip the logic.
- be wary of falling in love with a direction too early. attachment makes feedback feel like an attack.
- write down what was said in meetings while it's fresh, including the offhand remarks. offhand remarks are often the truest thing said.
- hand-off isn't the end of the work. think about who maintains it later and leave a trail: naming, notes, reasons.
- the client is rarely the end user. designing for the client's taste at the expense of their audience is an easy trap.

**communication habits that seem to help**

- short messages, plain words, a clear ask per message.
- say what's known, what's unknown, and what's waiting on whom.
- recap after calls in brief lines so misunderstandings surface while they're easy to fix.
- when sharing rough work, say how to look at it: "this is a direction, ignore the placeholder words, react to the structure." it keeps people from nitpicking the wrong layer.

**open-ended ideas**

- a small library of "why it looks like this" paragraphs for each project, in plain language, that clients can reuse when explaining the work to their own team or customers.
- a gallery of rejected directions, anonymized so nobody's business is exposed. process as a kind of product.
- a habit of making something purely for pleasure between jobs: a poster, a type sketch, a pattern with no brief. those experiments leak into paid work in surprising ways.
- deliberately restricted exercises: a narrow palette, a tiny set of type styles, no imagery at all. constraints force the thinking that abundance lets you skip.
- a short field guide for clients on giving feedback well. questions like "what did you notice right away" and "what felt off" instead of "do you like it."
- brand beyond the screen: how it feels when held, heard, tapped, unwrapped. most small studios stop at the pixels.
- hand the work to a stranger without explanation and watch where they hesitate. humbling and very useful.
- a better way to show process: not a polished case study but a messy trail with the wrong turns left in.
- pulling in a writer, photographer, or developer for projects that stretch past what I can cover myself.
- the boring parts of the business, like how proposals are laid out, how invoices read, how onboarding feels. those documents are design too, and clients meet them before they meet the work.
- the studio's own voice. does the way it sounds in email match the way the work looks?

**for the foggy stretches**

- hard thinking goes better when the head is clear; admin and tidying suit the foggy stretches.
- walking helps more than staring.
- eat properly, stand up, look out a window at something far away.
- tired designs feel anxious and over-explained. overwork leaves a smell on the work.
- rest isn't a reward for finishing, it's part of how the finishing happens.
