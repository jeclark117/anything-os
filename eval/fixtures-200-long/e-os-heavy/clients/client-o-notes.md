# Client O notes

general working notes

**approach**
- listen more than i talk. the brief is usually a guess about what might help, and the real problem tends to sit a little off to the side of it
- ask what the work is supposed to make people feel before asking what it should look like. feelings are easier to design toward than words like clean or modern or bold, which mean something different to everybody
- gather what already exists around a project: old materials, the way people talk about the thing, the awkward leftovers nobody wants to mention. that tells me more than a mood board does
- look for the smallest version of an idea that still feels like itself, then grow outward from there
- keep a loose list of questions i can't answer yet. the unanswered ones are usually where the interesting work is hiding
- try to understand who the work is really for. the person paying and the person looking are often different people with different needs

**process**
- rough before polished. paper sketches, ugly boxes, plain words standing in for images. nothing gets pretty until the structure holds up
- build in layers: structure, then rhythm, then type, then color, then detail. color too early tends to cover for weak ideas instead of exposing them
- the squint test. blur the screen or step back from the page and see what survives. if the hierarchy collapses, it was never really there
- make the boring parts good. spacing, alignment, line length, how a caption sits under an image. people can't name these things but they feel them immediately
- leave work alone until it stops feeling precious, then come back and look at it like a stranger would
- explain a design out loud in plain sentences. if the sentences are muddy, the design probably is too
- keep every abandoned direction in a folder. rejected ideas often turn out to be right for a different project
- look at the work in the real world as soon as possible: printed, on a phone, on a wall, in bad light. screens flatter everything

**things to keep in mind**
- samples of past work prove taste, but they don't prove fit. fit comes from conversation, and from showing that i understood the problem rather than just admired the surface
- a small studio is judged on reliability as much as on talent. clear communication, no surprises, tidy files, answers that arrive when expected
- scope creep arrives politely. it usually sounds like "while you're in there"
- silence after a good conversation is more often about their calendar, their budget cycle, or their internal politics than about the work. easy to forget, easy to take personally anyway
- feedback tells me about the person giving it as much as about the design. worth asking what's underneath a comment like "make it pop"
- notice the phrases people repeat. repeated phrases usually point at a fear or a wish
- taste is not a fixed thing. what feels fresh drifts, so it's worth keeping an eye on whether my instincts are leading or just repeating
- don't mistake being busy for being useful. some of the most valuable hours are spent staring at a wall
- the quiet admin side of running a studio, like invoices, backups, naming files, keeping records, deserves real care. it is the difference between feeling professional and feeling scattered
- not every opportunity is a good one. a poor fit costs more energy than an empty stretch of the calendar
- be wary of the urge to over-explain. a confident, short answer often lands better than a nervous, thorough one

**on staying in touch with people**
- the best follow-up is something useful with no ask attached: an observation about their space, an article that fits their problem, a small idea sketched on a whim
- avoid the check-in that only says "just checking in." it asks for attention without offering anything back
- keep a record of what each person cares about, what they said they were struggling with, what they were excited about. makes later conversations warmer and less transactional
- people remember how a conversation felt more than what was said. patient, curious, and slightly funny goes a long way
- there is a difference between persistence and pressure. persistence is about being easy to find and pleasant to hear from; pressure is about needing something

**on pitching and presenting**
- lead with the problem as they would describe it, then show how it looks from a slightly different angle
- show fewer directions, and mean them. a wide spread of options often signals that i haven't made up my mind
- tell the story of the decisions, not just the finished pictures. people trust a design more when they can follow the reasoning
- leave room for them to add something. work that feels completely finished can make people feel like spectators
- proposals could read shorter and more human, with less armor and fewer hedges

**open-ended ideas**
- write brief case notes after each project: what worked, what didn't, what surprised me. a private archive that might reveal patterns
- build a library of reusable questions for discovery conversations, sorted by what they uncover
- keep a style dictionary of the words clients use and what they turned out to mean
- make small self-started projects with no approval needed: posters, odd typefaces, strange layouts, anything that scratches an itch. these often feed paid work in unexpected ways
- try restricting a project hard, such as a narrow palette, type only with no imagery, or a grid that refuses to bend, and see what the limit produces
- imagine a studio that only took on work it could do slowly. what would that change about the kind of work that gets made?
- start a visual diary of everyday design that nobody intended as design: handwritten signs, shop labels, crossed-out notices, repairs that became decoration
- explore collaborating with a writer, photographer, or illustrator on speculative pieces, kept loose and unpressured
- experiment with showing work in progress in public, messy and half-explained, to see whether it attracts the kind of people who would value the process
- consider writing a short, honest guide for first-time clients about what working with a designer is like. could double as a filter for fit
- think about what a good ending to a project looks like. a handover that teaches the client how to look after the work might be more valuable than any single file
- wonder whether there's a way to make the studio's own materials feel as considered as the client work, since they're often the part that gets neglected

**odds and ends**
- walking helps. so does cooking, so does cleaning the desk, annoyingly
- pay attention to which work drains and which work gives energy back, and notice what they have in common
- solo work gets lonely in a specific way. showing rough work to a trusted friend is useful even when they know nothing about design, maybe especially then
- keep curiosity wide: architecture, old books, packaging, handwriting, maps, anything with a strong point of view
- read things that have nothing to do with design. the best ideas often arrive sideways
- be kind to past work. it was the best available answer at the time, with the information and energy there was
- pride and doubt tend to travel together. useful to remember when either one gets loud
