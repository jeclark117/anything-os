# Archive process

- Move the folder to the external drive
- Keep exports in the cloud

general working notes

**approach**

the studio runs on being able to find things again. sounds dull, but it's most of the job. a project is only as good as the ability to pick it back up with a cold head, so notes get written for a stranger who happens to be me, tired, with no memory of the reasoning.

work tends to go best when a brief is allowed to stay loose at the start. tight briefs produce tidy work that nobody remembers. loose briefs produce mess, and the mess is where the interesting stuff hides. the hard part is sensing when to stop exploring and start narrowing. that moment is usually felt before it's understood.

- start wide, cut hard
- keep rough material around, because it often explains the polished material
- name things plainly so search actually works
- write down the why, not only the what
- a sketch that looks bad is still information

**process**

there's a rhythm that shows up on most jobs even when nobody planned it. listening comes before anything else. what the client says they want is rarely the whole story, and the real brief tends to surface in the offhand comments near the end of a conversation. worth writing those down immediately.

after listening comes collecting. references, textures, old work, things from completely unrelated fields. the aim isn't to copy, it's to build a pile of raw material that can be argued with. a pile that's too tidy is a sign the collecting stopped too early.

then comes making ugly versions fast. the ugly versions are for finding out what's wrong, not for showing anybody. they're allowed to be wrong in loud, obvious ways. the quicker a bad idea fails, the sooner the better idea gets room.

refining is the slow part and the part that's easiest to overdo. there's a point where more polish stops helping and starts sanding off the character. learning to recognize that point is a career-long project.

handover deserves more care than it usually gets. a good handover answers questions before they're asked: where things live, what the intent was, what's safe to change and what isn't.

- listen, collect, make, narrow, refine, hand over, tidy up
- loops are normal; going backward isn't failure
- the tidy-up stage gets skipped when energy runs out, so it's worth protecting

**things to keep in mind**

- naming conventions matter more than they feel like they do. a messy name is a small debt that compounds quietly
- versions should be distinguishable at a glance, without opening them
- leave breadcrumbs inside files, not just around them
- fonts, palettes, and spacing rules are decisions, and decisions deserve a short written reason
- people forget what the original problem was. restate it at the top of anything that gets shared
- feedback arrives in emotional wrapping. unwrap it before reacting
- the thing a client dislikes is often not the thing they're pointing at
- rest improves judgment more reliably than another round of tweaks
- a design that needs a long explanation usually has a problem
- anything that can't be reproduced from the notes is at risk of being lost

licensing and permissions are the boring corner that bites hardest. whenever an asset comes from somewhere else, jot down where it came from and what the terms were, while the memory is fresh. reconstructing that later is miserable.

also worth remembering that the finished piece is only part of the record. the process material, the false starts, the rejected directions, can be valuable to a future version of the studio that's trying to understand its own habits.

**working with clients**

a client relationship tends to go well when expectations are stated plainly and early, and badly when everyone is being polite. politeness that hides disagreement is expensive. it's usually kinder to say the awkward thing sooner.

it helps to keep a running list of questions that never got answered. often the unanswered questions are more telling than the answered ones. a client who avoids a question is giving information.

- keep tone warm but specific
- summarize conversations in writing so memory isn't the only record
- separate taste from requirement
- explain tradeoffs in terms the client already cares about

**open-ended ideas**

- a visual index of past projects, organized by feeling rather than by category. warm, austere, playful, heavy, airy. might reveal patterns that a chronological list hides
- a short private essay per project about what went wrong. not for sharing, just for pattern-spotting
- a habit of photographing physical process material: scribbled pages, taped-up proofs, desk clutter. the physical side vanishes faster than the digital side
- a library of tiny experiments that never belonged to any job. odd type pairings, color studies, layout puzzles
- a collection of phrases clients use for vague desires, matched with what they eventually turned out to mean
- thinking about what a stranger would learn from the studio's files if they opened them cold. would they find a story, or a junk drawer?
- a standing question for the end of each project: what would make the next similar job easier?
- experimenting with leaving deliberate gaps in a design, so the finished thing has room to breathe and be changed
- pairing every new technique with a plain-language note on when it's useful and when it's showing off

**loose thoughts**

something about solo practice is that nobody else holds the context. everything the studio knows lives either in files or in a single head, and heads are unreliable. so the files carry the weight, and the files only help if they're legible. legibility is a kind of generosity toward a future self.

there's also a tension between wanting everything organized and wanting to keep moving. too much organizing becomes procrastination in disguise, while too little produces chaos that eats energy later. the sweet spot probably shifts depending on mood and workload, and it's fine for it to shift.

another tension: keeping everything versus keeping what matters. hoarding feels safe, but a huge pile is hard to search, and an unsearchable archive is almost the same as no archive. curation is part of care.

it's also worth noticing which tasks get avoided. the avoided tasks are usually the ones that matter most for long-term health: backups of the process, cleaning up naming, writing the retrospective. avoidance deserves curiosity, not scolding.

finally, good work leaves a trace of the person who made it, but good records leave almost no trace at all. they just quietly make things possible. that's a nice kind of invisible craft, and probably underrated.

- keep the notes casual so they actually get written
- let ideas sit in this pile until they either grow or fade
- unfinished thoughts are allowed to stay unfinished
