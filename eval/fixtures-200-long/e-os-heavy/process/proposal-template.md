# Proposal template

general working notes, loose and unfinished

**approach**

- listen more than talk early on. people usually arrive with a solution already in mind, and the useful work is walking backward from it to what is actually bothering them. a request for a fresh logo is sometimes a request to feel taken seriously.
- treat the brief as a hypothesis, not scripture. it's a guess made by a non-designer about what a designer might fix.
- ask what happens if nothing changes. the answer shows how urgent and how emotional the problem really is.
- small honest questions beat clever ones. "what's been frustrating lately?" gets further than any framework.
- keep the work legible to the client. if they can't explain why something looks the way it does, they can't defend it to their colleagues or customers, and then it quietly dies.
- notice the words they keep repeating. those are usually the real brief. mirror their language back before introducing mine.
- be curious about the boring parts of their business. the dull stuff is where the real constraints live.

**process, roughly**

the shape tends to be absorb, wander, narrow, make, test, refine, hand over. it is never that tidy, and the stages blur into each other. the wander phase is where the good stuff comes from, and it's also the phase most likely to get squeezed when things feel tight. worth protecting a little.

- gather everything they already have: old documents, screenshots of things they admire, complaints from their customers, half-finished attempts. the mess is informative.
- sketch badly on purpose, on paper, before opening any software. a bad sketch costs nothing to throw away and nobody gets attached to it.
- build the ugly version of the whole thing before polishing any part of it. proportion problems show up early that way.
- show work in rough form sooner than feels comfortable. the discomfort is the price of fewer surprises later.
- write down why a choice was made at the moment it's made. memory rewrites reasons.
- leave a gap between finishing something and judging it. fresh eyes catch more than extra effort does.
- keep a running file of dead ends. they tend to become useful on some other project.
- when stuck, change the medium. sketch what was meant to be typed, type what was meant to be drawn, talk it through out loud to an empty room.
- check the work at the smallest and largest sizes it will ever live at, and in the worst lighting, on the worst screen. clients rarely see it under ideal conditions.

**things to keep in mind**

- scope creeps in through kindness. a small favor, a quick tweak, a "while you're at it". each is reasonable on its own, and together they turn into a different project. notice the moment a favor becomes a pattern.
- clients are often anxious about spending money and about looking foolish. a calm, plain explanation is worth more than a polished deck.
- silence from a client isn't always disinterest. often they're waiting on somebody above them, or they're embarrassed about a delay on their side.
- say the awkward thing early and kindly. unspoken concerns grow teeth.
- running a solo studio means being the designer, bookkeeper, receptionist, and cleaner simultaneously. the admin side deserves the same care as the visual side, or it leaks into everything.
- tired work looks tired. rest is part of the craft, not a reward for finishing it.
- not every inquiry is a fit, and referring someone elsewhere can be a kindness to everybody involved.
- keep documents human. a page that reads like a legal threat sets a strange mood for what should be a friendly working relationship.
- watch out for requests for free exploratory work disguised as a "small test." it's worth a gentle conversation about what is actually being evaluated.
- taste is not the same as judgment. what I like and what serves the client's problem overlap less often than I'd hope.
- be honest about what is outside my skills. bluffing is expensive for everyone.
- clients remember how they felt more than what they received. clarity and warmth count as deliverables.

**open-ended ideas**

- a plain-language glossary to share with clients, covering the jargon that quietly intimidates people: bleed, kerning, vector, mockup, and so on, explained like a friend would.
- a short "what I heard" recap after early conversations, written in their words, so they can correct me before I build on a misunderstanding.
- a library of sentence openers for hard conversations: pushing back, admitting a mistake, asking for clarity, saying no gracefully.
- a reflection template for the end of a project: what surprised me, what dragged, what I'd steal from myself next time.
- a reference collection organized by feeling rather than category. "calm but not boring," "confident without shouting," "warm and a little odd."
- present options as a conversation rather than a reveal. what if the meeting is mostly the client reacting out loud while I listen?
- record short voice notes right after meetings, before the tidy version of events replaces the real one.
- an informal exploratory chat for people who aren't sure they need a designer at all. could be a good way to meet the problem before any paperwork exists.
- ask clients what they would be sad to lose from their current look or voice. protects the good parts of what already exists.
- a small ritual for closing out a project so it doesn't trail off: a last look together, a note on what to watch for as the work ages, a kind goodbye.
- a "kill list" of design habits I lean on too often: the safe typeface, the same grid, the reflexive drop shadow. naming them makes them easier to resist.

**questions to keep turning over**

- what makes a client feel safe enough to be honest about the real problem?
- how much explanation is too much, and when does it become a way of hiding?
- when does a template help, and when does it flatten something that should have stayed particular?
- what would a version of this studio look like if it were built around calm instead of output?
- how do you stay generous without becoming a pushover?
- what do I want people to say about working with me when I'm not in the room?
