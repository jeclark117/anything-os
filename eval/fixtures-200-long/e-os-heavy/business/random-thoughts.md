# Random thoughts

loose working notes, nothing urgent

**approach**

- most briefs show up blurry, and the blur is usually informative. a vague request shows where the real conversation is hiding, so rushing to sharpen it can flatten the interesting part.
- the work is less about producing a nice object and more about helping a client figure out what they were trying to say to begin with. the visual side tends to settle once that's clear.
- start with the question behind the question. "we need a refresh" is rarely about visuals.
- make rough things early, ugly on purpose, so people react honestly instead of politely. polished work invites polite feedback. scrappy work invites real feedback.
- keep the reasoning visible. a design you can explain in plain sentences holds up under scrutiny better than a design that only works because it looks good.
- being small means I can be slow where it matters and fast where it doesn't. worth protecting that.

**process, as it tends to go**

There's usually a loose soaking-up phase: reading whatever the client has lying around, looking at their neighbors in the category, noticing what feels tired. Then a sketching phase on paper, because paper stops me from nudging pixels before the idea is any good. After that comes a narrowing phase where most of what I made gets thrown out. It feels wasteful but it's really the point. The pile of rejected stuff is what makes the survivors trustworthy.

Patterns I keep noticing:

- the best ideas show up while I'm doing something unrelated, like walking or washing dishes. keep a notebook within reach, physical if possible.
- when I'm stuck, changing the medium helps more than pushing harder. screen to paper, type to shape, shape to words.
- constraints are friends. a tight palette or a limited set of typefaces forces better judgment than unlimited freedom.
- presenting work is its own craft. the story around a design matters nearly as much as the design, and it deserves the same care instead of improvising in the moment.
- feedback arrives wrapped in feelings. try to translate "I don't like it" into what the person is actually worried about.
- when the work feels too easy, be suspicious. it probably means I'm reaching for a familiar move.

**on clients**

Most people who hire a small studio want to feel looked after as much as they want good design. That doesn't mean being a pushover. It means being clear, being consistent, and not disappearing into the work for stretches without any signal. Silence makes people nervous, and nervous people start designing by committee.

It also helps to figure out early who actually holds the authority to approve things. A lot of friction comes from feedback given by a person who turns out not to be the decider. Worth asking gently, without making it awkward.

Some clients want to be led. Some want to be collaborators. Some say they want the first but behave like the second. Learning to read which is which probably saves more trouble than any amount of process documentation.

**things to keep in mind**

- scope creep starts as friendliness. a small extra favor feels nice, then quietly becomes the expectation. notice it early, and notice my own part in it.
- not every project deserves my best energy, but every project deserves my honest energy. there's a difference.
- write things down while they're fresh. memory edits itself, usually in my favor, which isn't helpful.
- leave space between projects. work done back to back gets samey and I start repeating myself without noticing.
- ask for what I need in plain language: access, content, a person who can approve things. delays almost always trace back to a missing piece of those.
- be wary of falling in love with a concept. attachment makes it hard to hear that it isn't working.
- rest counts as part of the work. tired designers make safe, forgettable choices.
- accessibility isn't a finishing touch. contrast, legibility, and clarity belong in the foundation, not patched on later.
- keep files tidy. future me is basically a stranger who has no idea what "final_really_final" was supposed to mean.
- say no to work that would make me worse at the work I want. hard to do when things are quiet, easier when they're not, but the principle holds either way.

**open-ended ideas**

- what would it look like to share more of the process publicly? not polished case studies, but the messy middle. might attract the kind of clients who value thinking over deliverables.
- a small set of reusable questions for kicking off projects, maybe a printed card or a short document that gets clients thinking before we ever talk.
- more physical output: printed samples, textures, objects people can hold. screens are flat and forgettable by comparison.
- a recurring habit of making work with no client at all, just to keep the muscles loose and see what I'd make unprompted.
- collaborating with people in neighboring fields, like writers, photographers, developers, to see what cross-pollination happens. a small studio can borrow scale by partnering well.
- documenting how I make judgment calls so the instincts become teachable, to myself if nobody else.
- a reference library sorted by feeling instead of category. "quiet but confident," "playful without being childish," "warm and a little odd."
- thinking about what I want to be known for as opposed to what happens to arrive. these aren't always the same thing.
- a better ritual for closing out projects: a short reflection on what worked, what didn't, what surprised me.
- maybe write more about the craft itself. explaining a thing is a good way to find out whether I understand it.
- a shelf or folder for failures. things that didn't work and why. oddly useful and oddly comforting.

**craft reminders**

Spacing does more than people give it credit for. So does restraint. When a layout feels off, the fix is usually subtraction rather than addition. Type choices should be able to survive being squinted at from across a room. Color should do a job, not just decorate.

Good work often looks inevitable afterward, as though it could never have been any other way. That's a trick of hindsight, and it hides how much wandering it took to get there. Worth remembering when I'm in the wandering part and it feels like nothing is working.

Also: details are where trust gets built. A client may never consciously notice the careful kerning or the thoughtful empty space, but they feel it, and it shapes whether they believe the rest.

**loose questions**

- how much of my process is habit and how much is actually necessary?
- where does the studio's personality show up, and is it showing up on purpose?
- are there parts of the work I've been avoiding because they're uncomfortable rather than because they're unimportant?
- what do clients say about working with me when I'm not in the room?
- is being small a limit or a feature? it shifts with my mood, but I suspect it's more of a feature than I give it credit for.
- what would I make if nobody was paying and nobody was watching?
- which habits would I be embarrassed to explain to a newcomer, and which ones would I be proud of?

**a note to self about momentum**

Momentum is fragile. A good stretch of work builds on itself, and a messy stretch of admin and interruptions can undo it surprisingly fast. Protect the quiet, generative hours, and let the shallow tasks pile up in their own corner until there's energy for them. Not everything needs an immediate reply. Not everything needs a perfect reply either.

When in doubt, make something. Even a bad something is more useful than a clean, empty page and a head full of plans.
