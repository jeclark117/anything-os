# Ergonomics

working notes

approach

- start from the problem, not the look. most weak work comes from falling for a visual idea before understanding what the thing is supposed to do for the person using it.
- keep the brief honest. if what's asked for and what's needed drift apart, say so early instead of quietly designing for the wrong target.
- rough before refined. ugly sketches are cheap and let me throw things away without feeling bad about it.
- fewer moving parts wins. if a layout needs a paragraph of explanation, the layout is the problem.
- design for the tired, distracted user, not the ideal reader who studies every corner of the screen.
- constraints are useful. a tight palette, a narrow type system, a limited grid. they make choices for me and keep the work coherent.

process

Most projects move through a loose sequence: listen, poke at the edges, sketch badly, show something rough, react, tighten. The order bends constantly and that's fine. What matters is that the rough stage gets enough room. If I rush straight to polished frames, feedback turns into arguments about color and spacing when it should be about whether the thing makes sense at all.

Listening is most of the job early on. Ask what the work needs to accomplish, who gets annoyed if it fails, and what has already been tried. The answer to that last question is gold, because it shows where the real friction lives. Write down the exact words people use to describe their problem. Those words often turn into the best headlines and labels.

References are for orientation, not borrowing. Collect widely, then step away from the pile before sketching so the work doesn't become a remix. Look outside design too: signage, packaging, old manuals, hand-painted shop fronts, textiles. The strongest ideas tend to arrive from somewhere unrelated.

When the direction is still fuzzy, make the same thing in clearly different ways on purpose: calm, loud, strange. Seeing the spread shows which qualities actually matter and which were just habit.

files and versions

- name files so a stranger could follow them. descriptive beats clever.
- keep a rough log of why things changed, not just what changed. future me has no memory of the reasoning.
- never overwrite the last good state. branch off, experiment, keep the safe copy close.
- tidy layers and components as I go rather than in a panic at handoff.
- record font licenses, asset sources, and export settings while the information is still fresh.

feedback

Feedback is data about a reaction, not an instruction. When someone says "make it pop," the useful move is to ask what felt flat. Separate taste from function: taste is personal and negotiable, function is about whether people can actually do the thing. Answer with a question or a small visual test rather than a defense. When I feel defensive, that's usually a sign there's something to learn in the comment.

When presenting, show fewer options with a clear point of view instead of a buffet. Explain the reasoning in plain language tied to the goals people stated. Leave room for silence. People need a moment to react before they can say anything useful.

things to keep in mind

- clarity before cleverness. a clever idea that confuses is just decoration with an attitude.
- accessibility isn't a bonus layer. contrast, readable sizes, clear focus states, sensible reading order. check them during the work, not after.
- use real content early. placeholder text hides problems, while actual copy breaks layouts in useful ways.
- test the awkward cases: very long names, empty states, slow connections, tiny screens, huge screens, error messages.
- print and screen lie to each other. what looks right on a bright display can look muddy on paper.
- scope creeps politely. when requests pile up, name the creep kindly and point out what it touches.
- step away from a piece before judging it. fresh eyes catch spacing problems that tired eyes bless.
- a mismatched project costs more than an empty gap in the schedule, so it's worth noticing early when something isn't a fit.

working solo

Working solo means nobody nearby to bounce things off, so I need substitutes. Read the copy aloud. Squint at the layout. Flip it upside down. View it small, then huge. Pin it to a wall and walk away. Explaining a screen to an imaginary skeptical colleague is surprisingly effective, mostly because I notice what I can't justify.

Admin is part of the craft. Clear scopes, clear stages of review, clear handover materials. Boring paperwork protects the relationship later, and it protects my own attention too.

It also helps to keep a distinction between making and judging. Making wants looseness and speed. Judging wants distance and a cooler head. Doing both in the same breath produces timid work.

open-ended ideas

- a personal library of small patterns: ways to handle empty states, tables, onboarding, error copy. grow it slowly from real projects instead of building it all in advance.
- a short, brutally honest "what went wrong" note after each project. could become a useful habit, could become a chore. worth testing.
- try designing a screen with no color, then add color only where it earns its place.
- explore sound and touch alongside the visual. what would this product feel like with the screen off?
- redesign something ordinary and overlooked, like a receipt, a form, a label on a jar, a bus-stop sign. treat it as practice with no audience.
- write microcopy in the voice of someone patient and a little funny and see how much it changes the mood.
- build a small set of prompts for stuck moments: what if this were quieter? what if it were for a child? for someone in a hurry? if it had to work in black and white?
- invite critique from people outside design. they point at the obvious things insiders stop seeing.
- study how things age. which designs still feel right long after trends move on, and what do they share?
- imagine a slower portfolio: fewer pieces, more story about reasoning and dead ends.
- keep a folder for things that make me laugh or wince. those reactions are signals worth studying.

loose bits

Good design often feels inevitable, which means the effort is invisible and hard to explain to people who only see the result. Be patient about that. Don't over-explain the work to compensate.

Curiosity is the real tool. Software changes constantly, but the habit of asking why something works stays useful no matter what's on the screen.

Some open questions I keep circling. How much polish is enough at each stage? When does a system stop helping and become a cage? How do you show care in a deliverable nobody will notice unless it's missing? Is there a way to make quiet, careful work more visible without turning it into a performance?

Also worth remembering: a design that's easy to describe is usually easy to use. If I can't say what it does in plain words, the problem is probably upstream of the pixels.
