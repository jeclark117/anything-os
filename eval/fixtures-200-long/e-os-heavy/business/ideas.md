# Ideas

general working notes

**approach**

- listen more than talk at the start. what a client needs is usually buried in how they describe the problem, not in the brief itself
- ask what the thing should make people feel, then ask what it should make them do. the answers are rarely the same, and the gap between them is where the interesting design lives
- collect references without judging them. a messy pile says more about taste than a tidy moodboard ever does
- write down the obvious solution early so it stops haunting the process, then try to get past it
- restraint is usually the better instinct. if a layout needs a lot of explaining, it probably isn't finished
- good work tends to feel inevitable afterward, which is exactly why it's hard to show how much thinking went into it

**process**

Most projects follow a loose rhythm, even when it doesn't feel like it. There's a stretch of wandering around the problem, a stretch of making rough things quickly, a stretch of narrowing, and a stretch of polish that always takes longer than it should. The trick is noticing which stretch I'm actually in, because the habits that help in one hurt in another. Polishing too early kills exploration. Wandering too late wastes everyone's patience.

- sketch on paper before touching a screen, even if the sketches are embarrassing
- make the ugly version first. it's easier to fix a rough idea than to rescue a precious one
- step away when a piece starts to feel flat, and come back before deciding it's bad
- view things at a different size, or print them. problems hide in the default view
- keep earlier versions around. the discarded direction often holds the one good detail the final is missing
- test type and spacing with real content, not placeholder text. real words break layouts in useful ways
- squint at it. if the hierarchy disappears, it wasn't there

**things to keep in mind**

Clients rarely want what they ask for. They want the outcome they imagine the thing will produce. Getting to that outcome sometimes means gently reframing the request, and that's easier when trust has been built early through small, visible acts of care: replying clearly, explaining choices in plain language, admitting uncertainty without drama.

Feedback is information, not a verdict. When someone says they don't like something, the useful move is to ask what they were expecting. The stated complaint is often a symptom. "Make the logo bigger" can mean "I don't feel seen" or "I don't trust this yet" or just "I'm nervous about the audience."

general working notes

these are loose notes about how the studio tends to run when it's running well. not rules, more a pile of reminders that keep proving useful. stuff gets added when something surprises me and trimmed when it stops being true.

approach

- listen longer than feels necessary. most briefs are a feeling dressed up as a list of requests, and the feeling is the part worth chasing
- ask what the thing is for before asking what it should look like. a site meant to reassure is a different animal from a site meant to persuade
- look at references briefly, then close the tabs. too much looking and everything drifts toward the same average
- sketch badly on purpose. if a layout can't survive as rough boxes on paper, polish won't rescue it
- let constraints do some of the designing. a narrow palette, a small family of type styles, a stubborn grid. limits make choices faster and the result more coherent
- treat the real content as the real material. layouts built around placeholder text tend to fall apart the moment actual words show up

process

the shape of a project is usually familiar even when the subject isn't. there's a stretch of fuzzy exploration where nothing looks right, and that's fine, it's supposed to feel that way. then a direction starts to pull, usually because a particular detail clicks: a headline treatment, a color pairing, a way of cropping images. after that the work is mostly making everything else agree with that detail.

it helps to name the direction in plain language before building it out. if i can't describe it without waving my hands, it isn't ready. something like "calm but not sleepy" or "confident without shouting" gives me a test to hold each screen against.

showing rough work early is uncomfortable but cheaper than polished work that misses. people react better to something unfinished because they feel allowed to push on it. a finished-looking comp invites approval or rejection and nothing in between. a rough one invites conversation.

feedback needs translating. "make it pop" usually means something feels flat. "it feels busy" tends to mean the hierarchy is unclear, not that there's too much on the page. "can we see more options" can mean they aren't convinced yet, or that they're nervous about owning the call. a gentle follow-up question is worth more than a quick reaction.

build things so a stranger could open the file and understand it. name layers and classes with care, keep structure tidy, leave little notes where something clever might look like a mistake. the stranger is often future me.

general working notes

how i approach a new project

start by hearing people out before proposing anything. most early conversations with a prospective client are really about working out what they believe the site is for, and that is rarely what they say at the very beginning. ask what a visitor should feel or do after landing, and what would make the whole project feel like a win from their side. the answers shape structure more than any mood board does.

try to get a read on the client's taste before showing a thing. references they like, references they can't stand, and why. the why is the useful part. people struggle to describe what they want but react very precisely to what they dislike.

- keep a running list of questions that surface early, and resist answering them all immediately
- jot down the words the client uses about their own work, because those words frequently belong in the copy
- notice who really makes the final calls, which isn't always who's on the email thread
- ask what they've tried before and what annoyed them about it

process thoughts

i like working from the material outward. layouts built around placeholder text usually fall apart when real copy arrives. if the client can't supply copy yet, a rough outline of what each page needs to say is enough to design against. headings, then supporting bits, then the visual treatment.

sketch on paper before touching a screen. rough boxes and arrows, ugly on purpose. it stops me getting precious about pixels early and makes it easier to bin a direction that isn't working.

build in layers: structure, then type and spacing, then color, then imagery, then motion. each layer should still hold up if the layers above it are stripped away. a layout that only works because of a clever animation isn't really working.

- set up a small family of text styles and spacing rules early and reuse them hard
- test with real text at awkward lengths: long names, short headlines, missing images
- look at the design small before getting comfortable with it large
- squint test: blur the screen and see whether the hierarchy still reads
- try the layout with the images removed entirely, and again with the text removed

working solo

being the only person in the studio means nobody catches my blind spots, so it helps to build in ways to catch them. step away from a layout and come back with fresh eyes. read copy out loud. show work-in-progress to a friend outside design and watch where their eyes go without explaining anything.

scope creep is the quiet danger. small requests stack up and blur together. keeping a documented trail of what was asked for, and whether it belonged in the original brief, keeps conversations about extras friendly and factual instead of awkward.

protect thinking space. admin expands to fill whatever room it's given. batching emails, invoices, and housekeeping stops design work from being chopped into slivers.

document as i go. short notes at the end of a work session about where i was headed and what i was unsure about save a lot of re-orientation afterward.

- keep a parked list for ideas that are good but don't belong in the current project
- save rejected directions rather than deleting them; they can become the answer for a different project
- notice when i'm avoiding a hard part of a design, and name it

communication habits

- explain the reasoning behind choices in plain language, not design jargon
- show fewer options with stronger rationale rather than a buffet
- show work in context (on a device mockup or a live preview) rather than as flat images
- ask for feedback on specific things, like whether the structure makes sense or the voice feels right, rather than "what do you think?"
- when feedback is vague, translate it back: "it sounds like you want this to feel warmer, is that right?"

difficult feedback is usually about a hidden need. "make the logo bigger" frequently means "i can't tell right away what you do." solve the underlying problem and the surface request tends to evaporate. separate the person's reaction from the worry underneath it, and respond to the worry.

it also helps to say out loud what's working, not just what needs changing. clients relax when they can see what's staying.

on references and inspiration

look outside web design for ideas: print, signage, packaging, architecture, old posters. work that borrows only from other sites ends up looking like them. collect images with a note on what specifically caught my eye, otherwise the collection turns into a pile of vague admiration.

when a client shares a reference, ask what exactly they respond to. it might be the typeface, the pacing, the photography, or just the feeling of confidence. pull that thread rather than copying the surface.

writing for the web

copy is design. headings should carry the story for people who only skim, and most visitors skim. short paragraphs, plain verbs, plain button labels. if a button label needs explaining, the interface is the problem.

helping clients with their words, even when it's outside the brief, is worth thinking about, because weak copy undermines a good layout. maybe that deserves its own treatment as a separate offering at some point.

revisions and feedback loops

rounds of revisions go better when each round has a clear purpose: structure, then look, then polish. mixing them invites circling back. give clients a simple way to say what kind of feedback they're giving, so a comment about the overall structure doesn't get tangled up with a comment about a shade of color.

small details that earn trust

- a thoughtful error page
- readable email confirmations
- sensible focus order
- a footer that actually helps
- favicon and social preview not left as defaults
- link text that makes sense out of context

open-ended ideas

- a reusable starter kit: a blank structure with sensible defaults for navigation, footers, contact forms, and accessibility basics, so projects begin from a solid base without looking templated
- a short questionnaire for prospective clients that doubles as a gentle filter for fit
- a plain-language handoff guide so clients can update their own text without having to call me
- a library of small, tasteful interaction patterns to drop in when a project needs a bit of life
- a reflection ritual after each project: what worked, what dragged, what i'd change
- a case study format that tells the story of the problem rather than just showing finished screens
- writing more about process in public; it could attract clients who value thinking over decoration
- finding ways to describe the studio's value that lean on outcomes and clarity rather than lists of deliverables
- a swipe file of small details i admire from the wild: a nice hover state, a clever footer, a well-handled empty state
- a standing habit of redesigning a boring everyday form just for practice
- exploring how to make a site feel handmade without sacrificing speed or accessibility
- thinking about what a really good about page does, and why most of them don't

quality checklist (loose)

- accessibility: contrast, focus states, keyboard navigation, alt text, sensible heading order
- performance: image size, fonts, unnecessary scripts
- responsive behavior: check the awkward in-between widths, not just small and large
- copy: broken links, mismatched capitalization, placeholder text left behind
- forms: error states, confirmation messages, what happens when a field is left empty
- metadata: titles, descriptions, social preview images
- view the page as a stranger would: can they tell what this is right away?

things i keep relearning

- the best work usually comes from constraints, not open canvases
- white space is a design decision, not leftover room
- clients remember how the process felt more than any particular screen
- a calm, well-organized handoff makes a project feel finished even if the visuals are modest
- the urge to add is stronger than the urge to remove, and removing is usually the better move
- if a decision keeps getting revisited, the real question probably hasn't been asked yet
- taste develops by looking widely and noticing what pulls at me

loose thoughts on fit

some projects are a joy because the client trusts the process; others are exhausting because every choice gets relitigated. worth watching for early signals: how a prospect talks about past designers, how quickly they respond, whether they can articulate what they need. this isn't a rule, just patterns to keep an eye on.

also worth remembering that a good fit goes both directions. the clients who get the most out of the work are usually the ones who treat it as a collaboration, bring honest context about their audience, and are willing to be surprised by an approach they hadn't pictured.

- say the reasoning out loud when presenting. people respond better to a story about a choice than to the choice alone
- protect the quiet parts of a design. whitespace, pacing, and the boring structural decisions carry more weight than the flashy bits
- consistency beats cleverness over the long run. a system people can predict is a system people can use
- accessibility is part of the craft, not a layer added at the end
- read the copy. design that ignores the words is decoration
- be suspicious of trends that feel exciting right away. they tend to age badly
- notice when I'm designing for other designers instead of for the audience

**working solo**

Being a studio of one means every role lands on the same desk: designer, bookkeeper, salesperson, project manager, janitor. The risk is that the loudest role wins. Admin expands to fill whatever space it's given, and the actual making gets squeezed into the edges. It helps to treat focused making as the main event and everything else as maintenance around it.

Isolation is the other hazard. Without colleagues, there's nobody to say "that's not working" in passing. Substitutes matter: trusted peers to swap work with, a habit of explaining a problem aloud to an empty room, and a willingness to ask for a critique before it feels safe to.

- keep a running scrap file of half-thoughts so nothing gets lost between projects
- write down what went well and what was painful at the end of a job, while it's fresh
- be honest about energy. some tasks deserve the best part of the working stretch and some can be done tired
- rest is part of the process, not a reward for finishing it
- boundaries with clients are kindness in advance. vague edges produce resentment on both sides

**open-ended ideas**

- a habit of showing process, not just results. rejected directions, early sketches, and wrong turns make the final feel earned and help people understand what the work involves
- short written reflections on a single design decision, told honestly, including what didn't work
- a small collection of tiny experiments made purely for curiosity, with no client and no brief, to keep the hands loose
- a way to give back: share templates, checklists, or lessons that would have helped me earlier
- invite collaborators from neighboring crafts, like writers, photographers, and illustrators, and see what happens when the studio gets a little less solitary
- a recurring practice of looking at old work with new eyes, to see what held up and what didn't
- think about what the studio sounds like. not just what it looks like, but the tone of how it talks to people
- explore whether a quieter, more consistent presence would serve better than occasional loud bursts
- ask past clients what they remember most about working together. the answers might not match what I assume I'm known for

**small reminders**

Curiosity is the thing to protect. Skills can be trained and tools can be swapped, but the habit of wondering why something works, or why it doesn't, is what keeps the work alive. When a project starts to feel mechanical, that's usually a signal to change the question rather than push harder on the answer.

Finally, it's fine for ideas to sit unfinished for a while. Some of them are only ready after other things have changed around them, and the pile of maybes is a resource, not a burden.

- accessibility isn't a finishing pass. contrast, focus states, readable sizes, and sensible heading structure are cheaper to get right from the start than to retrofit
- performance is part of the design. heavy images and stacked effects have consequences even if they never show up in a mockup
- empty states, error states, and loading states are where a design shows whether it was really thought through. design the awkward moments too
- real devices lie differently than resizable browser windows. check things on actual phones with actual thumbs
- write the microcopy. a button that just says "submit" is a missed chance to sound like somebody
- type does most of the heavy lifting. when a page feels off, check spacing and type before reaching for new colors or new graphics
- leave room. whitespace isn't wasted space, it's pacing
- watch for scope creep dressed up as polish. "just a quick tweak" has a way of reopening questions that were already settled
- say what's out of scope plainly and kindly, early, and again if needed
- protect energy. the best work happens when i'm curious, not when i'm defending a position

running things solo

- i'm the designer, bookkeeper, support desk, and janitor. that's fine in moderation, but the context switching is the real cost, more than the workload itself
- boring systems pay off. templates for proposals, a standard folder structure, reusable checklists. the less i reinvent, the more attention is left for the actual work
- quiet stretches are for tending the garden: tidying files, writing down what i learned, improving templates, making things for no reason at all
- busy stretches are for trusting the systems already built
- outside eyes matter. working in isolation makes it easy to mistake familiarity for quality

communication

- plain words beat jargon, especially with people who hire designers because they aren't designers
- a short recap after any conversation keeps everybody honest and saves arguments later about what was said
- bad news travels better early. nobody has ever been upset that a problem was flagged too soon
- tone gets lost in writing, so err on the side of warm
- explain the why behind a choice, not just the what. people defend what they understand

when stuck

- change the medium. if screens aren't working, try paper. if paper isn't working, go for a walk
- swap the question. instead of "how should this look" ask "what should the person feel right here"
- remove something. the stuck feeling is often too many things competing for attention
- squint at the problem in grayscale, then with no images, then with only the type left
- explain the problem to somebody else, even an imaginary somebody
- go and make something unrelated and small. a poster, a pattern, a silly logo for a fake business. the unstuck feeling often leaks back into the real work

open-ended ideas

- a running scrapbook of small interface moments that delighted me, each with a line about why it worked. patterns over screenshots
- a teardown habit: take apart a site i admire and write down, in plain words, what it does and what it costs the visitor
- a small library of reusable sections like heroes, testimonials, footers, and contact blocks that i can adapt rather than rebuild from nothing
- a glossary for non-designers that translates words like kerning, hierarchy, and breakpoint into everyday language. could double as onboarding material, a handout, or a newsletter
- explaining things out loud is a good way to find the gaps in my own understanding. teaching, writing, or recording screen walkthroughs would all surface the places where my process is intuition rather than knowledge
- a before-and-after archive that puts rough thinking next to the final result, since the messy middle is what people rarely see and most want to understand
- constraint games for fun: design a landing page using only system fonts, or only black and white, or with no images at all, and see what survives
- a question bank for discovery calls that drops the generic questions and keeps the ones that produced surprising answers
- a casual circle of other independent designers for swapping critique and complaints
- a "things i got wrong" log. embarrassing, but probably the most useful document in the studio

closing thoughts

a lot of this comes down to attention. notice what people are actually trying to do, notice where the page gets in their way, notice when i'm fiddling with details to avoid a harder question.

the work is rarely about the tool. tools change, shortcuts change, the underlying questions about clarity, trust, and ease mostly don't. worth remembering when the learning curve of something new feels loud.

it's also worth remembering that finished beats perfect more often than it feels like it should, and that a small thing made with care tends to travel further than a big thing made with anxiety.
