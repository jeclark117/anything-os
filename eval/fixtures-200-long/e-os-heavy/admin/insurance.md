# Insurance

general working notes

**approach**

the studio runs on a simple idea: make the work legible before making it pretty. when a project lands, the urge is to open the software and start pushing shapes around. resist that. begin on paper, or in a plain text file, and write down what the thing is supposed to do for the person looking at it. if that sentence is mushy, the layout will be mushy too.

being a solo studio means there is nobody to hand a problem to, which is both freeing and a little dangerous. the fix is building small checkpoints into the work so drift gets caught early. a rough version shown early beats a polished version shown late, nearly every time. clients forgive roughness. they don't forgive surprises.

keep the vocabulary of a project tight. choose the words, colors, and shapes that belong to this job and let everything else go. a project with a small vocabulary feels confident even when the budget of attention is thin.

also worth remembering: the work is not the files. the work is the change in how somebody understands or feels about something. files are just the container.

**process**

- listen before proposing. the brief is rarely the real problem. the real problem tends to hide in the offhand remarks at the end of a call.
- gather references widely, then throw most of them away. what survives shows the taste of the project.
- sketch ugly. thumbnails, scribbles, boxes with arrows. the goal is volume of ideas, not quality of any given idea.
- take a direction and push it until it breaks, then look at what's still standing. the broken parts are information.
- write down why a choice was made, in plain sentences, right when it happens. memory edits the story afterward.
- name files so a tired future version of me can parse them without thinking. no "final" in a filename, ever. it is a jinx.
- leave a project alone for a stretch before calling it complete. fresh eyes catch kerning problems, muddy hierarchy, and the thing that was bothering me but that I'd talked myself out of noticing.
- show work in context whenever possible. a logo on a white page lies. a logo on a tote bag, a screen, a sign, a receipt tells the truth.
- when stuck, change the medium. if it's stalled on screen, print it. if it's stalled in print, cut it up and rearrange the pieces by hand.

**things to keep in mind**

- the client is not the audience. the client is a proxy for the audience, and good work serves the audience even when that's slightly inconvenient for the proxy.
- feedback is a symptom, not a prescription. when somebody says "make it pop," ask what feels flat. when somebody says "can the logo be bigger," ask what feels lost.
- constraints are friends. a narrow palette, a fixed grid, a limited set of typefaces all force invention in places where unlimited choice would produce mush.
- whitespace is not empty. treat it as a material with weight, the way a sculptor treats air around a figure.
- accessibility is part of craft, not an add-on. contrast, legibility, and clear structure make the work better for everybody, not only for the people who need them most.
- admin is part of the studio too. paperwork, renewals, invoices, and filing are boring and easy to let slide, and they quietly shape whether the creative side can keep running. a small recurring habit of tidying them beats a panic later.
- protect the energy that makes good work possible. a rested brain solves layout problems in minutes that a depleted brain circles for hours.
- scope creep rarely arrives with a label. it shows up as "while you're in there." notice the phrase, and notice how it feels to hear it.
- be honest about what the studio is good at. being known for a few things deeply is stronger than being known for everything vaguely.
- credit the sources of inspiration, at least privately. it keeps the practice honest and keeps the influence from turning into imitation.

**loose ideas, no commitment**

- a running scrapbook of found typography: hand-painted signs, receipts, packaging from places that never hired a designer. useful as a palette cleanser.
- a habit of redrawing a familiar object from memory, then comparing it with the real thing. the gaps show what the mind considers essential.
- a small library of reusable questions for kickoff conversations. not a form, more like a deck of prompts to flip through when the conversation stalls.
- an experiment with designing in strict grayscale until the very end, to see whether structure improves when color can't rescue it.
- a private archive of projects that failed, with notes on why, written kindly. the failures teach more than the wins, but only if they're written down.
- try explaining a design to somebody outside the field, out loud, and record what words they reach for. those words are often better than the jargon.
- maybe a recurring self-assigned project with no client, just to keep taste sharp and give the experiments somewhere to land.
- consider what a studio newsletter would look like if it were purely images and a sentence each. low effort, high honesty.
- explore sound as a design material. even in static work, thinking about how something would sound if it were spoken or tapped changes the rhythm of the layout.
- look at how other crafts handle revision. woodworkers, bakers, and editors all have their own relationship to the rough draft, and each is worth stealing from.

**small reminders**

keep backups boring and automatic. keep a clean folder structure and resist cleverness in it. keep a list of questions to ask a new collaborator before the work starts. keep the desk clear enough that a new idea has somewhere to land.

the studio is small on purpose. the aim isn't growth for its own sake. the aim is work that feels considered, relationships that stay easy, and enough room in the schedule of the mind to notice what's interesting. when those three things are intact, the rest tends to sort itself out. when any of them slips, that's the signal to slow down and look at what's drifting.

and when in doubt, simplify. take one thing away, look again, and see whether the work got better. usually it did.

working notes, general

loose collection of habits and reminders. not a manual, more the stuff I'd tell a past version of me who was panicking at the desk.

**approach**

Projects go better when the problem gets restated in plain language before anything gets drawn. Not the brief's language, mine. If I can't say what the thing is for in a sentence a stranger would follow, the layout stage turns into decoration.

Clients rarely know what they want, but they usually know what they don't want. Collecting dislikes early is cheaper than guessing at likes. Ask for examples of work they hate, and listen to the reasons more than the examples.

The studio is small, and that's a strength. There's no committee standing between a hunch and a test. Protect that by keeping process light and not building rituals just because bigger shops have them.

Taste is a tool, not a verdict. A direction can be handsome and still wrong for the job. Try to catch the moment when I'm defending something because I like it rather than because it works.

**process bits**

- start on paper. bad drawings are fast and nobody gets attached to them.
- work wide before narrow. make plenty of rough directions, kill most of them quickly, and only polish what survives.
- keep a parking lot file for ideas that don't fit the current job. they almost always fit a later job.
- name files like a stranger will open them. future me is a stranger.
- save versions often with a short note on what changed and why, so backing up is painless instead of a archaeological dig.
- show work in context early. on a mockup of the real thing, not floating on a white page. almost everything looks fine floating on white.
- the blur test: squint, or shrink it, or step back from the screen. if the hierarchy collapses, it was never really there.
- try a limited palette and a limited type setup and see how far they go before reaching for more. constraints tend to produce the interesting decisions.
- leave room. empty space is not wasted space, and clients who ask to fill it are usually nervous, not wrong about the content.
- when stuck, change the medium. cut paper, trace, rebuild the thing at a ridiculous scale, flip it upside down. the problem often loosens.

**things to keep in mind**

Feedback is data about the viewer's experience, not a design instruction. When someone says make the logo bigger, the real note is often "I can't find it" or "I don't trust this yet." Dig for the underlying complaint and solve that.

Scope creeps quietly. Watch for the small ask that is actually a new project wearing a small ask's clothes. Naming it kindly and early is much easier than untangling it late.

Rest the eyes. A fresh look after stepping away catches uneven spacing, awkward line breaks, a typo in the headline that nobody saw because everybody read what they expected.

Admin is part of the work. The boring paperwork side of running a studio gets heavier the longer it sits untouched. Keep records tidy enough that finding a document doesn't swallow a whole afternoon. A little tidiness now saves a lot of irritation later.

Be honest about capacity. Saying a project doesn't fit is better than delivering something tired. Tired work is easy to spot, and it costs more than the awkward conversation would have.

Print is unforgiving. Colors shift, thin lines vanish, paper soaks up ink in ways a screen never admits. Proof on real stock whenever the budget of attention allows, and assume the screen is flattering everything.

Check accessibility early, not as a final pass. Contrast, size, reading order, how it behaves for someone who can't see the color distinctions. Test on a cheap screen in bad light, because that's where a lot of people will meet it.

Copy matters as much as the visuals. Awkward words undo handsome layouts. If the text is placeholder, the design is placeholder too, and everyone can tell.

Keep the client's customers in mind more than the client. The person approving the work isn't usually the person who has to use it.

Credit and attribution matter. Keep track of where reference material came from so inspiration doesn't quietly slide into borrowing.

Boundaries around communication help. Work done in a reactive scramble is rarely the best work. Messages can wait for a reply that's actually thought through.

**ideas, open-ended**

- a library of small failed experiments, each annotated with why it failed. failure notes are probably more useful than success notes.
- reverse-engineer an old piece of packaging or signage I love, just to understand why it works, then see which parts transfer.
- trade critique informally with another solo designer. no obligation, just fresh eyes from someone who doesn't share my blind spots.
- build a personal stock of textures. scan fabric, torn paper, stained wood, the scraps on the desk, handwriting. nothing digital feels as alive as something that started physical.
- a mood board made entirely of non-design things: hand-painted shopfronts, crooked labels, the lettering on old machinery, shipping marks on crates.
- what would a project look like if the deliverable were the process instead of the artifact? a living guide rather than a finished set of files.
- write a plain-language explainer about how revisions work, so clients know what to expect and how to give useful feedback. might also make me better at asking for it.
- test how a brand sounds, not just how it looks. read the name aloud, imagine it said over a bad connection, imagine it misheard.
- the cobbler's children problem: the studio's own materials are always the last thing to get attention. worth wondering what the studio's own identity would look like if it were treated like a real client, with a real brief and real pushback.
- try designing something deliberately ugly, then figure out exactly which choices made it ugly. a useful way to name the rules by breaking them.
- keep a running list of questions that come up in projects and don't have clean answers. why does this typeface feel warm and that one clinical? why do certain grids feel calm? the list itself might become the interesting thing.
- slow work. what happens if a piece is allowed to sit untouched until it stops feeling urgent, then gets revisited? some work probably improves from neglect.

**loose reminders to end on**

Curiosity is the main asset of a small studio. When it fades, the work gets competent and dull. When something starts feeling like a chore, that's a signal worth reading rather than pushing through.

Look at things that have nothing to do with the current project. Walk somewhere unfamiliar, read something outside the field, watch someone else do their craft. The best solutions tend to arrive sideways.

Keep the work legible to people who aren't designers. If a decision can't be explained simply, it may not be a decision yet, just a preference with good lighting.

And leave some mess on the desk. A tidy desk is nice, but a few abandoned experiments lying around often turn into the next idea.
