general working notes

**approach**

- start from what a visitor is actually trying to work out when they land on a studio site. mostly it's "can this person help me, and do they get what I'm after?" everything else is decoration.
- write like you're talking across a table. if a sentence would sound strange said aloud to a prospective client, it probably needs rewriting.
- let the work carry the weight where it can. copy should point at the projects and step back, not narrate them in a loud voice.
- keep a scrap file of phrases that sound true. the good lines usually come from emails and offhand remarks, not from sitting down to "write copy".
- read everything out loud before trusting it. the ear catches stiffness the eye forgives.

**voice**

being a solo studio is a strength and also a trap. it's tempting to hide behind "we" to sound bigger, but it reads as slightly off, and people can tell. plain first person feels warmer and more honest. the pitch is closeness: the person you talk to is the person doing the work.

- avoid words that every studio uses and nobody believes: passionate, innovative, bespoke, holistic, elevate.
- prefer verbs over adjectives. "I untangle messy brand systems" beats "I provide best-in-class brand solutions."
- allow a little dryness or humor, but only where it's natural. forced charm is worse than none.
- keep the same temperature from page to page. a warm intro followed by a stiff, corporate list feels like two different people wrote it.

**process notes**

a rough loop that tends to work for any block of text:

- dump everything messy, with no editing and no worrying about order.
- walk away, even if only for a short stretch, and do something unrelated.
- come back and circle only the sentences that still feel alive.
- build the structure out of those, then fill gaps sparingly.
- cut again. then cut a bit more, because the instinct to explain is stronger than the instinct to trust the reader.

when stuck, try explaining the idea to an imaginary friend who works in a totally different field. what they'd ask is usually what the copy is missing. or write the thing as a message to someone specific, then strip the greeting off.

another trick: write the version that's too blunt, then soften only as much as needed. starting blunt keeps the meaning intact, whereas starting polite tends to bury it.

**things to keep in mind**

- people skim. headings, short paragraphs, and clear first lines matter more than elegant long ones.
- every page should answer "so what do I do next?" even if the answer is quiet, like a link or an invitation to write.
- specificity builds trust. a concrete detail about how a project actually went is worth more than a paragraph of claims.
- be careful about promising outcomes. describe how the work is approached and let the results speak elsewhere.
- accessibility isn't a bolt-on. plain language, readable contrast, and meaningful link text all help real people, and they usually make the writing better too.
- search visibility matters, but writing for machines first makes the prose lifeless. write for a person, then check that the obvious terms appear naturally.
- watch for jargon that feels normal inside the studio and means nothing outside it. design vocabulary can be a wall.
- consistency of small things (capitalization, how projects are named, punctuation in lists) quietly signals care. it's boring and worth doing in a final pass.

**on structure**

lists are useful but dangerous. a list invites completeness, and completeness is rarely what a visitor wants. a handful of strong items usually does more than an exhaustive catalog, because it implies there's confidence behind the choices. if everything is listed, nothing is emphasized.

one way to test a list: imagine each item removed and ask whether the page gets worse. if not, it was filler.

similarly, an intro can often lose its first sentence. the real start tends to be buried in the middle of the opening paragraph, after the throat-clearing.

- lead with what's distinctive, not with what's generic.
- put the most useful information where the eye lands first.
- let the closing be short. long sign-offs dilute whatever came before.
- consider whether a paragraph might work better as a sentence, or a sentence as a heading.

**tone around process and working style**

people hiring a small studio are often nervous about the experience as much as the result. will it be easy? will I be heard? will things drift? copy can quietly settle those nerves by describing how collaboration actually feels: clear check-ins, honest feedback, no surprises. it doesn't need to be a formal methodology. a couple of grounded sentences about how conversations go can say more than a diagram.

it might also help to name what the studio is not. "not a big agency, not a template shop" does positioning work without bragging, though it risks sounding defensive if overdone.

**open-ended ideas**

- a short "how I think" piece, written loosely, that sits apart from the formal pages and shows judgment rather than claiming it.
- pull a real line from a past conversation (anonymized, with permission) and let it do the persuading in place of a polished testimonial.
- try writing the pages as if for a friend of a friend who has never heard of design studios, then see what survives.
- a small running section of work-in-progress sketches or notes, kept informal, which shows the studio is alive and thinking.
- explore whether a short audio or handwritten element could carry personality that text can't. even a scan of a messy sketch can humanize a page.
- maybe a plain-language glossary for terms clients often feel awkward asking about. useful, a bit generous, and it quietly demonstrates expertise.
- test different tones in private: warmer, drier, more formal. read each aloud and notice which one feels like a person rather than a brand.
- consider an FAQ built from questions people genuinely ask, in their own phrasing, rather than invented ones.
- look at how other solo practitioners outside design (writers, ceramicists, carpenters) describe their work. the best examples tend to be plain, physical, and unafraid of small details.

**loose thoughts, no particular order**

- rewriting is mostly deleting. the draft that feels too thin is often just honest.
- confidence reads as calm. if the copy feels like it's trying to convince, something is probably still unclear underneath.
- white space applies to words too. let a line breathe.
- a sentence that sounds clever and says nothing is the most expensive kind to keep.
- ask what a skeptical reader would underline, then check whether that's what was meant to land.
- sometimes the right fix for weak copy is better structure, not better words.
- notes to self are allowed to be ugly. polish belongs to later passes.
- keep an eye out for places where the site says the same thing in different outfits. repetition feels like emphasis to the writer and like padding to everyone else.
- remember that the site is a conversation starter, not a full portfolio, résumé, and manifesto at once. it can leave things out on purpose.

**questions worth sitting with**

- what would a new visitor misunderstand about the studio if they only read the headings?
- which phrase feels most like me, and where else could that register show up?
- is anything here written to impress rather than inform?
- what's the smallest version of this page that still does its job?
- if a stranger quoted one line back, would I be glad it was that line?

**closing scraps**

good copy tends to feel inevitable once it's done, as if it could not have been written another way, even though getting there was messy. the mess is part of the process and worth protecting: leave room for bad drafts, odd tangents, and lines that go nowhere. most of what ends up on the page was found by writing past the obvious.

keep the tone kind to the reader. assume they're smart, busy, and slightly unsure, and write as someone who's glad they showed up.
