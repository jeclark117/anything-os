# Referral program idea

working notes

general approach

- listen longer than feels necessary at the start. the stated problem is rarely the real problem, and the real one tends to surface when people stop performing and just describe their day.
- keep the brief short enough to remember without looking. if it needs a manual, it's probably hiding a disagreement nobody has named yet.
- make rough things early. rough work gets honest reactions, polished work gets polite ones.
- let constraints do some of the thinking. a tight palette, a narrow set of layouts, a restrained approach to type. the limit is often where the personality comes from.
- say out loud what has been left out and why. people trust work more when they can see the edges of it.
- treat the obvious idea as a starting point, not an enemy. it's usually obvious for a reason, and the interesting move is figuring out what it's missing.

process, loosely

Most projects drift through the same rough stages even when they pretend not to: soak up context, wander, narrow down, make, refine, hand over. The wandering stage is the one that gets squeezed when things feel busy, and it's the one that pays off most. Some habits that help:

- keep a running pile of references that have nothing to do with design. hand-painted signs, instruction leaflets, ticket stubs, worn packaging, the lettering on old tools.
- sketch on paper even when it feels slow. the slowness is the point, it keeps ideas loose and keeps polish from sneaking in too early.
- write the explanation of the work before the work is finished. if the rationale reads thin, the design probably is too.
- let a piece sit overnight before judging it. things that looked clever late in the evening often look fussy in daylight.
- show work in context, not floating on a blank canvas. a logo on an actual envelope tells the truth faster than a logo on white.
- when a direction isn't working, resist the urge to patch it. patches pile up and the piece starts to feel nervous.
- keep a record of the paths not taken. they're often useful later, for a different project or a different mood.

working with the people who hire the studio

- be clear about what kind of feedback is useful. "it feels off" is welcome, but follow it with "where did your eye land" and "what did you expect to see."
- separate taste from function. taste is personal and negotiable, function is checkable.
- when a client is nervous, slow down rather than speed up. reassurance comes from clarity, not from volume or enthusiasm.
- explain choices in plain words and skip the jargon. if a rationale can't survive being said in a kitchen, it's not ready.
- a short recap after any conversation prevents a lot of misremembering later. tidy paper trails are kind to everybody.
- the relationship is part of the deliverable. people remember how it felt to work together long after they've forgotten the exact shade of blue.
- notice what people are afraid of. fear of looking cheap, of looking unserious, of being copied, of being ignored. design decisions often hide inside those fears.
- it's fine to say "I don't know yet." it's better than a confident guess that has to be walked back.

being a solo studio

- there's no colleague to catch mistakes, so build other safety nets. read copy aloud, print things out, look at layouts upside down, check them on a small screen, check them on a bad screen.
- boundaries are a design problem too. they work better when they're designed on purpose rather than improvised under pressure.
- generosity compounds, but so does overextension. resentment is an early warning that something has been given away that shouldn't have been.
- rest is part of production, not a reward for it.
- avoid being the bottleneck for every small thing. write down repeatable steps so they don't have to be reinvented each project.
- keep the admin boring and consistent. templates for the dull stuff free up attention for the interesting stuff.
- pay attention to what energizes and what drains. over the long run that quietly steers what kind of work the studio becomes known for.
- reputation is built mostly from small things: replies that are clear, files that are named sensibly, work that arrives in the shape it was promised to arrive in. that's not a commitment, just an observation about where trust comes from.

on taste and craft

- Send a thank-you gift for referrals
- Not a discount

- spacing is most of it. when something feels calm, confident, or trustworthy, it's usually rhythm and room rather than decoration.
- contrast needs a reason. emphasis everywhere is emphasis nowhere.
- default settings are decisions somebody else made. question them before accepting them.
- type should be able to survive being printed badly, shrunk down, or squinted at from across a room.
- accessibility isn't a layer added at the end. treat it as a starting condition, and the work usually gets better for everybody.
- name files and layers as if a stranger will open them. often that stranger is the future version of yourself.
- the details nobody consciously notices are the ones people feel. alignment, consistent corners, the way a line of text breaks.
- taste develops by looking at a lot of work and being honest about why some of it moves you and some doesn't.

open-ended ideas

- a small library of kickoff questions that surface values rather than preferences. what would the business never want to be mistaken for? what does it quietly envy in others?
- short reflections after a project wraps: what surprised, what dragged, what was quietly delightful.
- making the messy middle of the process more visible to clients, maybe through a loose shared board, so they feel included rather than left waiting for a reveal.
- a personal glossary of the words clients use to describe what they want ("clean," "modern," "warm") alongside what they've tended to mean by them.
- an occasional audit of older work. what has aged well, what hasn't, and why.
- thinking about how the studio's own materials could explain how it works without sounding like a pitch.
- writing or teaching about process. explaining something forces clarity, and it tends to reveal which habits are real and which are just stories.
- an honest, unglamorous "wouldn't do this again" list, covering types of projects, kinds of requests, and patterns that led to friction.
- experimenting with tangible proofs and material samples in how work is presented, since physical things land differently than files do.
- a practice of making small personal projects with no client at all, purely to keep the eye and hand curious.
- exploring what a calm, slow version of the studio would look like and what would have to change to allow it.

small reminders

- good enough to ship is a real standard, as long as "good enough" is judged honestly and not out of tiredness.
- ask for the thing that's needed. most requests are easier to grant than to imagine.
- curiosity beats cleverness almost every time.
- when stuck, change the medium before changing the idea. go from screen to paper, from color to black and white, from pictures to words.
- credit influences. it costs nothing and keeps the work honest.
- keep some projects deliberately quiet and small. they're good for the craft in ways the loud ones aren't.
- the point of all this is to make things that help people see or understand something more easily. everything else is a means to that.

loose threads to pick up when there's space

- how to talk about the studio's approach to people who don't know what a design process is
- whether the way feedback gets gathered could be gentler without getting vaguer
- what a good ending to a project feels like, and how to make endings less abrupt
- how to keep a sense of play when the work is mostly careful and professional
- the difference between being known for a style and being known for a way of thinking
