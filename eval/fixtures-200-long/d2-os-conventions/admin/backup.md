general working notes

the way a project tends to go for me: a messy conversation, a pile of references, a lot of squinting at things that almost work, and then a moment where the idea clicks and the rest is carrying it through. the trick is not mistaking the pile for progress. collecting references feels productive and mostly isn't. what counts is whether I can say, in a sentence a stranger would follow, what the thing is supposed to do for the person using it.

**approach**

- start with the problem the client thinks they have, then poke at it until the real problem shows up. they are rarely identical, and the gap between them is where the interesting work lives.
- ask dumb questions early. the questions that feel too basic are usually the ones nobody has answered out loud, and the answers tend to reshape the whole brief.
- keep the early sketches ugly on purpose. if a sketch looks finished, people critique the polish instead of the idea.
- make the smallest version of the idea that can be judged. a rough layout, a crude mockup, a paragraph of placeholder copy. enough to react to, not enough to get attached to.
- when stuck, change the medium. if the screen isn't giving me anything, pencil and paper often will. if the layout feels dead, try describing it in words and see which words feel alive.
- write down why a choice was made while it's still fresh. future me will not remember, and future me will be annoyed.

**process thoughts**

working alone means nobody is going to interrupt a bad direction, so I have to build in my own friction. a habit of stepping away from a piece and coming back with fresh eyes catches more problems than any checklist. squint at the work, flip it upside down, shrink it until it's a smudge. if the hierarchy still reads, it's probably sound. if it falls apart, the details were hiding a structural problem.

feedback is a skill on both ends. giving it to myself means separating "I don't like this" from "this doesn't work." getting it from clients means translating. when someone says "make it pop," they usually mean something specific about contrast or attention, and the job is to dig out what. a vague note is a symptom, not an instruction.

it helps to keep a running list of questions I haven't answered yet, kept apart from the list of things to make. the first list is where the quality comes from. the second is just labor.

**things to keep in mind**

- restraint is a design decision. every element added is something the viewer has to process. ask what happens if it's simply removed.
- consistency beats cleverness more often than it should. a system that behaves predictably earns trust, and a clever exception spends it.
- type does most of the heavy lifting. spacing and rhythm do the rest. color is seasoning, and it's easy to oversalt.
- accessibility isn't a layer on top. contrast, legibility, and clear structure are part of the work from the start, and retrofitting them is always worse.
- the client's audience is not the client. it's easy to design for the person signing off instead of the person using the thing.
- good files are a courtesy to everyone downstream, including me. clear naming, tidy layers, sensible organization. boring and valuable.
- scope creep rarely announces itself. it arrives as a small favor, a quick tweak, a "while you're in there." noticing the pattern early makes the conversation about it much easier.
- not every project deserves the same level of care, but every project deserves honesty about what level it's getting.
- rest is part of the workflow. tired eyes make confident bad decisions.

**working with clients**

a lot of the job is translation and expectation management. people hire a studio to make something look right, but what they often need is help figuring out what they want to say. the visual work comes easier once the message is clear, so it's worth protecting space for that conversation even when everyone wants to jump to the pretty part.

some habits that seem to help:

- show the thinking along with the result. a layout with no reasoning attached invites opinions. a layout with reasoning invites a discussion.
- present fewer options than feels natural. too many choices turns a design review into a taste contest.
- be upfront about trade-offs. faster, cheaper, better is an old joke because it's true, and clients respect honesty more than they resent limits.
- keep a record of what was agreed in plain language, so there's something to point back to when memories drift. (this is a general habit, not a record of any particular agreement.)
- let silence sit. after presenting something, resist the urge to fill the gap with apologies or qualifications.

**the solo studio condition**

being the designer, the project manager, the bookkeeper, the support desk, and the person who takes out the trash is both the appeal and the hazard. context-switching is expensive. the days that feel scattered usually are, and the work from those days shows it. grouping similar tasks together, so that the administrative stuff doesn't leak into the creative stretches, seems to protect the quality of both.

there's also a loneliness to it that's easy to underrate. no colleague to say "that's not working" across the desk. it's worth cultivating a few trusted outside eyes, people whose taste is different from mine and who are willing to be blunt. the goal isn't approval, it's friction.

**open-ended ideas**

- a small library of reusable starting points: spacing scales, type pairings, grid structures. not templates to fill in, more like warmed-up tools that save the boring setup.
- a habit of keeping a "found things" folder: odd signage, good packaging, a page from an old manual that has lovely proportions. the best references often come from outside design entirely.
- writing short post-mortems for projects, even the ones that went fine. what surprised me, what dragged, what I'd do differently. patterns show up only when there's a record.
- exploring how to make the studio's process more visible to clients without turning it into a performance. maybe a loose working-notes page they can peek at.
- experimenting with constraints as a creative engine: only one typeface family, only a limited palette, only shapes that can be drawn with a ruler and compass. constraints often generate more than freedom does.
- thinking about what a good handoff looks like. the work doesn't end when the files go out; it ends when the client can maintain and extend it without calling for help.
- curiosity about how other small studios handle the unglamorous parts: contracts, intake, archiving old work. there's probably a lot to steal.
- the question of how to say no gracefully to projects that are wrong-shaped for the studio. a clear sense of what the studio is good at makes this easier, and it's worth sharpening that description over time.

**small reminders**

look at the work at actual size, and then far away. read the copy out loud. check the thing on a bad screen, in bad light, from a bad angle. assume the viewer is distracted and a little impatient, because they are. make something that still holds up under those conditions, and then make it a bit kinder than it needs to be.

and keep things moving without rushing. the work usually improves when there's room to wander, and it usually suffers when wandering turns into avoidance. learning to tell the difference is most of the craft.
