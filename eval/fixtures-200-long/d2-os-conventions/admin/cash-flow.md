general working notes

**approach**

- start from the problem, not the look. most projects that go sideways went sideways because the brief was answering a slightly different question than the real problem
- keep early work properly rough. when it's polished too soon, people defend it instead of reacting to it
- fewer, stronger directions beat a wide spread. a big spread lets the reviewer pick by gut, and the reasoning gets lost along the way
- say what each direction is trying to solve, in plain words, before showing how it looks
- constraints are friendly. a tight brief usually produces better work than an open canvas, so when the brief is loose, tighten it by asking what must not happen
- get the work into the medium it will live in as early as possible. a layout that looks great in a mockup can fall apart on a real screen or a real printed page
- leave room for the odd detail that makes a piece feel made by a person rather than assembled

**process**

the shape of most projects seems to be: listen hard, wander, narrow, refine, hand over. the trouble is that the wandering part feels unproductive, so the temptation is to skip ahead to narrowing. the work that comes out of a skipped wander tends to look competent and forgettable.

for discovery, the best questions are the boring ones. who is this for, what do they already believe, what would make them trust it, what would make them leave. the answers to those shape everything downstream, and clients often haven't asked themselves in so many words. sometimes the conversation itself is the most useful deliverable of the whole early phase.

for the middle of a project, work in loops that are small enough to survive being wrong. a rough pass, a reaction, a revised pass. the smaller the loop, the cheaper the mistakes, and the less emotionally loaded each round feels for everybody involved.

for review, it helps to frame feedback requests narrowly. "does this feel right for the audience" gets better answers than "what do you think". open questions invite taste-based opinions, which are hard to act on. specific questions invite reasons.

for handover, assume the person receiving the files will be stressed, in a hurry, and missing context. label everything, explain the system behind the choices, and write the notes as if for a stranger. a project that can't be picked up by somebody else is only half finished.

**things to keep in mind**

- being solo means every role is mine: designer, bookkeeper, account manager, the person who answers the contact form. the switching between roles is the hidden cost, not the roles themselves
- scope creep rarely arrives as a big request. it arrives as "while you're in there" and "could you just". worth noticing the pattern early and naming it kindly
- clients rarely mean what they say about taste. "make it pop" is a feeling, not an instruction. ask what they'd point at as an example, then ask what they like about that example
- good work gets quiet reactions. a loud reaction usually means surprise, which is not always the same as approval
- keep a record of why choices were made, not just what was chosen. the reasoning is what gets lost, and it's what saves an argument later
- rest is part of the work. the best solutions tend to show up away from the desk, usually while doing something dull
- not every project deserves full effort. some deserve a clean, sensible, efficient job and no more, and recognizing which is which is a real skill
- be wary of designing for the portfolio instead of the client. the pretty case study and the useful result are not always the same object
- taste drifts. looking at old work now and then is a decent calibration check, both for how far things have come and for what got lost

**open-ended ideas**

- a running file of "things that annoyed me today as a user", turned into prompts for future projects. small irritations are often the seed of better design
- a habit of writing a plain-language summary of every project right after it wraps, while the details are still fresh. might make a useful library over a long stretch
- try presenting work with the rationale first and visuals second, and see whether reactions change
- build a small kit of reusable starting points for the repetitive parts of projects: intake questions, feedback prompts, handover checklists. the aim is less reinvention, not less care
- explore offering a lightweight advisory format for people who don't need a full build but would benefit from an outside eye on what they already have
- experiment with constraint games: design something using only a limited palette, or only a single typeface family, purely to stretch the muscles. no client attached, no stakes
- think about what a "quiet" studio identity looks like. less self-promotion, more demonstration through work that circulates on its own
- consider writing up process notes in public form. teaching what's known has a way of exposing what isn't
- keep a list of people whose work is worth studying, sorted by what each does well rather than by style. useful when stuck on a specific problem like hierarchy, rhythm, or restraint
- look at how other small creative practices handle the unglamorous parts: contracts, intake, boundaries, saying no. there's probably a lot to borrow
- wonder whether there's a better way to hold early conversations so clients feel heard without the conversation turning into design-by-committee
- sketch more on paper. screens flatten thinking in a way that's hard to notice until it's absent

**loose reminders**

- clarity beats cleverness nearly every round
- if a design needs a paragraph of explanation, the design isn't finished
- ask the obvious question out loud. usually somebody else in the room was wondering too
- save the good rejected ideas somewhere. they often fit a different problem
- write things down while they're fresh, especially the offhand comments from conversations. offhand comments are where the real brief tends to hide
- protect the quiet stretches. they're where the actual thinking gets done
