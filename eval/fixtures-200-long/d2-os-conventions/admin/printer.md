# Printer

general working notes

Keep coming back to this: the work improves when the question gets sharper. Before touching anything visual, try saying what the thing is for in plain words, as if explaining it to a friend who doesn't care about design. If the explanation wobbles, the work will wobble too.

**approach**

- start loose, ideally by hand. Rough shapes are cheaper to throw away than polished ones, and they stay honest longer
- let the brief breathe. Read it, leave it, read it again after a walk. Different words jump out each time
- ask what the client is afraid of, not just what they want. Fear shapes feedback more than taste does
- find the awkward constraint and lean on it. Tight limits tend to produce the more memorable answer
- collect references widely and not only from design: signage, packaging in a corner shop, old manuals, textiles, handwriting on a shopping list
- name the feeling before the look. Calm, brisk, warm, dry, slightly cheeky. Then hunt for forms that carry it
- resist the urge to solve everything at the start. Some questions only answer themselves when the work is partly made

**process**

Work moves in loops more than straight lines. Explore, narrow, test, then widen again when it starts feeling too safe. The skill is noticing when a loop is productive and when I'm just fussing.

- early on: volume over quality. Piles of small thumbnails, quick type experiments, color swatches dropped on a page without much thought
- midway: push the promising directions until they break. See where an idea runs out of road, because that edge is informative
- late: details. Spacing, alignment, rhythm, consistency, the stuff nobody notices until it's wrong
- leave room for fresh eyes. Looking at something after real time away is basically free critique
- when stuck, change the medium. Sketch what's meant to be digital, build digitally what's meant to be physical, or describe it out loud to an empty room
- keep a log of dead ends. They're rarely dead; they're usually parked ideas waiting for the right project

**things to keep in mind**

- hierarchy comes early. If everything shouts, nothing is heard. Work out what leads, what supports, what whispers
- white space is a material, not leftover
- type does most of the heavy lifting. Respect size relationships, line length, contrast between weights, how it behaves tiny and how it behaves huge
- color behaves differently on a screen, on a surface, beside other colors, in bad light. Test it where it will actually live
- accessibility isn't a bonus layer. Contrast, legibility, generous targets, plain language. Building it in from the start costs far less than retrofitting
- consistency is a kindness to whoever inherits the work later, including future me
- file hygiene matters: clear names, tidy layers, notes beside anything clever. Past me has been unkind to present me
- keep source files and exports separate and obvious
- backups that are boring and automatic beat clever systems every time

**working with clients**

Most friction comes from mismatched expectations, not bad work. A lot of the craft is in the conversation around the design.

- show the thinking, not just the result. People accept a bold move more readily when they can see the path to it
- frame feedback requests. "Does this feel right for your audience?" gets better answers than "Do you like it?"
- separate taste from function. A preference is valid, but it's different from a problem
- be wary of presenting a wide spread of options. Too much choice invites mushy compromise
- when a request would weaken the work, say so gently and offer an alternative that serves the same goal
- scope drift is sneaky. It arrives as small friendly asks. Notice the pattern and name it kindly
- write things down after conversations while they're fresh, so memory doesn't quietly rewrite what was meant
- silence from a client isn't automatically approval or rejection. Sometimes they're just busy

**solo-studio realities**

Being designer, admin, bookkeeper, courier, and support desk is a lot of hats. The trick is keeping the making time from getting eaten by everything around it.

- batch the dull tasks. Context switching is a hidden tax
- isolation is real. Seek out critique from peers and swap work in progress with trusted folks
- rest is part of the process, not a reward for finishing it
- keep a side project alive that has no client. It's where odd ideas get to survive
- confidence wobbles. Keep a folder of kind words and work that still feels good, for those moods
- being small is a feature: quick turns, direct conversations, no layers of sign-off. Use that

**critique, taste, and judgment**

Taste is a muscle. It grows by looking at lots of things critically and asking why something works, not only whether it's attractive. A useful habit is to take a piece of work I admire and write down what it's actually doing: where the eye enters, what it ignores, what it trusts the viewer to understand.

Same in reverse. When something feels off but I can't say why, slow down and interrogate it. Usually it's spacing, a mismatch in tone, or a detail that belongs to a different idea. Naming the problem is most of the fix.

Be suspicious of work that's merely clever. Cleverness impresses the designer; clarity serves the audience. The best ideas tend to feel obvious afterward, which is the hardest effect to engineer.

Also worth remembering: fashionable choices age quickly. Ask whether a choice serves the subject or just signals that the work is current.

**open-ended ideas**

- a small library of texture scans and hand-drawn marks to pull from, so work feels less templated
- try designing backwards: start from how a thing will be worn out, reused, or thrown away
- constrain a project to a plain typeface family and see how far spacing and scale alone can go
- a restricted palette exercise: only what's already lying around the desk
- explore sound, motion, or touch as design material, not just the visual
- what would a brand look like if it were built to be quietly boring in the best way?
- write a short manifesto for the studio's taste, then argue with it
- practice describing work with no jargon at all
- turn everyday annoyances into prompts: bad door handles, confusing forms, unreadable receipts
- study how things age: wear on a doorplate, fading on a sign, patina on metal. Design for graceful aging
- keep a swipe file of great questions, not great visuals
- check how work reads at a glance, from across a room, and up close
- invite a non-designer to describe what they see before explaining anything
- make something purely for the pleasure of it and refuse to justify it

**loose threads**

Some things I don't have answers to yet. How much process should be visible to a client before it becomes noise? Is there a better way to keep a visual memory of past projects than folders and thumbnails? What's the right balance between a recognizable studio voice and serving each subject on its own terms?

Maybe the answer to the last is that voice lives in the choices about restraint, not in the surface style. Worth testing against old work to see if that holds.

Also curious about slower methods: working with fewer digital tools for stretches, to see how the thinking changes. Less automation, more deliberate hands-on making, and a closer look at what gets lost or gained.

The point of all this is to make things that feel considered. Everything else is scaffolding.
