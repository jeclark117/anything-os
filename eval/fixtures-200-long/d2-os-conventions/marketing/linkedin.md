working notes, general

this is the stuff that sits under the specifics. reminders about how the studio runs when it's running well, plus loose ideas.

**approach**

start with the problem, not the look. clients usually arrive with a solution already in their head (a mark that "pops", a site that feels "clean") and the real work is backing up to find what they're trying to change. ask why until the answers get boring, then ask what would be different if it worked. the brief usually lives there.

keep the early stage messy on purpose. paper, markers, bad thumbnails, collages of things that annoy me next to things that don't. the screen makes everything look finished too soon, and finished-looking work is hard to throw away. rough work is cheap to kill, which is the point.

stay curious about the boring parts of a business. how orders get packed, what the front desk says on the phone, what the invoice looks like. the best ideas tend to come from the unglamorous corners nobody thought to brief.

**things to keep in mind**

- the client isn't the audience. their customers are. when feedback gets personal ("i just don't like that green"), translate it back into a question about who the work is for.
- restraint is a skill. when in doubt, take something out and see whether anyone misses it.
- consistency beats cleverness over time. a clever idea that can't survive a menu, a form, an email footer, and a packaging flap is a poster, not a system.
- write down why a decision was made at the moment it's made. future me forgets, and future clients ask.
- test at the ugly sizes. tiny, enormous, grayscale, squinted at, on a bad screen, printed on cheap paper.
- words are design material. placeholder text hides problems. use real copy as early as possible, even if it's clumsy.
- leave breathing room in layouts and in the calendar. both get cramped when ignored.
- be suspicious of work that everybody likes immediately. it's either great or safe, and it's worth working out which.
- say what's not working in plain language, kindly. vague praise and vague criticism are equally useless.

**process, roughly**

there's no rigid sequence, but the shape tends to go like this:

*listen.* long conversations, few slides. record what people say in their own words, because their phrasing often becomes the seed of the language the work uses later. notice what they apologize for, what they brag about, and what they change the subject away from.

*gather.* look at the category, then deliberately look away from it. a bakery's identity can learn more from a hardware store or a record sleeve than from other bakeries. keep a loose pile of references and sort them only after the pile gets unwieldy, because the sorting reveals what actually matters.

*sketch wide.* make many bad directions quickly, then look for the ones that make me slightly uncomfortable. discomfort often means it's different from the default. the safe directions can be built later if needed.

*narrow with reasons.* pick a couple of routes and be able to explain each in a sentence a stranger would follow. if the explanation takes a paragraph, the idea isn't clear yet.

*build the system.* type, color, spacing, a handful of rules for how things behave when they meet each other. then try to break it with awkward real content: a long name, a missing photo, an ugly legal line.

*present with a story.* show the work in context, not floating on a white void. mock up the actual places it will live. walk through the thinking in the order the client will experience it, not the order it happened.

*hand off cleanly.* files named sensibly, a short guide that a stranger could follow, and a note about what's flexible and what isn't. the handoff is part of the design. a good system that nobody can use correctly turns into a bad one within a season.

**working with clients**

- early honesty about scope saves a lot of awkwardness later. vague scope grows quietly, and then everybody is annoyed.
- people respond better to choices framed as tradeoffs than to open questions. "this is bolder, this is calmer, here's what each costs you" beats "which do you like?"
- let clients be experts in their own business. i'm the expert in making it legible.
- silence from a client isn't always disinterest. sometimes they're just stuck. a short, specific nudge works better than a long, apologetic one.
- the best clients treat feedback as a conversation. worth noticing early who those are, and worth noticing what made them that way.
- don't over-explain the work when presenting. if it needs a long defense, it may need another pass.

**solo-studio realities**

being the only person means every role is mine: designer, bookkeeper, receptionist, janitor, sales. the risk is that the loudest role wins, usually whatever feels most urgent rather than most important. worth checking now and then which hat has been on the longest.

energy is the real resource. creative work done tired looks tired. protect the hours when thinking is sharp, and give admin the leftovers instead of the other way around.

isolation is a quiet cost. no colleague to say "that's not working" or "that's actually good." worth building in outside eyes: trusted peers, a friend who knows nothing about design, even a printout pinned to the wall and viewed from across the room.

boundaries are a design problem too. what's the default response time, what counts as an emergency, what's a favor versus a service. unspoken rules cause the most friction.

**open-ended ideas**

- a small library of "failed directions" that taught something. not for sharing necessarily, just to stop repeating mistakes.
- a plain-language explainer that clients could read before the project begins, covering what design is for, what feedback is useful, and what to expect from the process.
- an experiment where a project starts from constraints only: limited palette, a single typeface, no imagery. see how far the idea can carry.
- collaborating with a writer, photographer, or developer on a self-initiated piece, just to stretch the range and meet people.
- teardown notes: take a well-known identity and write down, without judging, every decision that seems to have been made on purpose.
- a habit of making something purely useless, to keep the hands loose between jobs.
- a better way to track how past work ages. return to old projects to see what held up and what didn't, and why.
- write more about process in a way that's useful to other people, not just promotional. generosity tends to travel further than polish.
- a reading list of books on things that aren't design: logistics, linguistics, gardening, bridge-building. borrow structure from elsewhere.
- a standing question to ask at the end of every project: what would i do differently, and what did i avoid because it felt risky?

**small reminders**

- good enough and finished beats perfect and unseen.
- taste is trained by looking, and also by making a lot of mediocre things.
- the work should be able to explain itself to a tired person who has never heard of the client.
- curiosity is cheaper than inspiration and more reliable.
- a clear no protects the yes.
- not every idea needs to be pursued. noting it down is already a kind of respect for it.
