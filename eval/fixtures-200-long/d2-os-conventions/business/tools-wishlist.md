# Tools wishlist

general working notes

**approach**

- start loose. rough thumbnails, ugly blobs, words scribbled in the margins. the aim at the beginning is plenty of raw ideas, not good ones, and that's easy to forget when a project feels important.
- try to say what the thing is for in plain language before touching a layout. if the purpose can't be explained out loud without jargon, the design usually wanders.
- look at what already exists in the space and notice what everybody is doing the same. the gap tends to live right there.
- keep a pile of references, but treat them like seasoning rather than a recipe. too much and everything tastes like somebody else's work.
- let the constraints do some of the thinking. a tight brief is often kinder than a blank canvas.

**process thoughts**

working solo means there's nobody across the table to push back, so the process has to supply its own friction. some ways of faking an outside opinion: sleep on it, flip the work upside down, squint at it from across the room, shrink it until only the big shapes survive, print it and tape it to a wall. problems that hide on a screen tend to jump out in the physical world.

the middle of a project is where most of the doubt shows up. everything looks half-baked and the early excitement has worn off. it helps to remember that this feeling is normal and mostly says nothing about the quality of the work. the rough patch is usually just the stretch where the idea is turning into an object.

separate making from judging. when both happen together, the inner critic wins and nothing gets made. produce a pile, walk away, then come back and sort it with a cold eye. the version of me that sorts is a different person from the version of me that makes, and they work better when they don't share the same sitting.

also worth noting: the best ideas rarely arrive at the desk. they show up while washing dishes, walking, staring out a window, riding a bus. keeping a scrap of paper close by costs nothing and saves a lot of lost thoughts.

**things to keep in mind**

- clarity beats cleverness. a clever idea that takes effort to decode is a tax on the audience.
- whitespace is a material, not leftover space. give it the same care as the shapes.
- type does most of the heavy lifting. if the typography is wrong, no amount of decoration rescues it.
- color is mood first and brand second. pick the feeling, then find the hue.
- check everything in grayscale. if the hierarchy collapses without color, it was never really there.
- squint test, thumbnail test, distance test. all cheap, all revealing.
- name files like a stranger will have to open them. that stranger is usually me, later, tired, and confused.
- keep the source material organized as the work grows, because tidying a mess afterward is miserable and tidying as it grows is almost invisible.
- an honest "i don't know yet" is better than a confident guess. clients can usually tell the difference.

**working with the people who hire me**

a lot of design trouble is really communication trouble in disguise. the feedback "make it pop" or "something feels off" is a clue, not an instruction, and the useful move is asking what they were hoping to feel. most people can't name a layout fix but they can describe a reaction.

it helps to show the reasoning along with the work. a design presented with no story invites everybody to substitute their own taste. a design presented with a short, honest account of why it looks the way it does invites a conversation about goals instead.

boundaries matter too, though they're hard to keep when the work is personal and the inbox never closes. a studio of a single person has no front desk, no receptionist, no buffer. so the habits have to act as the buffer: clear expectations about how revisions work, plain language about what's included, and a polite way of saying a request is outside the original scope.

feedback that stings is worth sitting with before replying. the first reaction is rarely the useful one. usually there's a real observation hiding inside the clumsy phrasing, and it's worth digging out.

**energy and rhythm**

creative energy isn't constant, and pretending it is leads to burnout. some stretches call for deep focus and long silence; others are better for admin, tidying, and shallow tasks. matching the type of work to the type of energy seems to matter more than total effort.

breaks aren't a reward, they're part of the mechanism. eyes need distance, wrists need rest, and the brain needs a chance to chew in the background. the posture and comfort side of working is easy to ignore until the body complains loudly, and by then it's already costing something.

variety helps. switching between screen work and paper work, between making and reading, between loud music and silence. sameness dulls the senses, and a dull sense is a poor design instrument.

**open-ended ideas**

- what would a project look like if it were designed backward, starting from the last impression and working toward the opening?
- a small personal archive of failed experiments, kept deliberately, to mine later. dead ends often hold good textures.
- trying a week-long constraint on purpose, like only one typeface or only two colors. no wait, that's just a thought; the idea is that tight rules can make strange things happen.
- building a library of physical textures, scanned or photographed: torn paper, rust, fabric, water stains. digital work can feel sterile without something real underneath.
- writing short descriptions of imaginary clients and imaginary briefs as practice, just to keep the thinking muscles warm between real projects.
- a regular habit of redrawing a favorite piece of old design from memory, then comparing. the differences reveal what actually stuck.
- exploring how sound could shape visual choices. what does a rough, grainy layout sound like? what does a calm, spacious one sound like?
- collecting examples of good error messages, signs, labels, and packaging. everyday design is the best teacher because nobody is trying to impress anybody.
- thinking about how to document process in a way that is useful to a future self rather than performative for an audience.
- asking what a studio of a single person can do that a larger shop can't: be faster to change direction, be more personal, take odd risks, keep the work human.

**small reminders**

stay curious about unrelated fields. the best visual ideas often come from biology, architecture, cooking, music, maps, textiles. the closer the reference is to design, the more likely the result is derivative.

protect the quiet. attention is the actual raw material of this job, and every notification is a withdrawal from it.

be kind to the early drafts. they're allowed to be bad. that's their whole job.

and remember that taste grows by looking, making, and being willing to be wrong in private before being right in public.
