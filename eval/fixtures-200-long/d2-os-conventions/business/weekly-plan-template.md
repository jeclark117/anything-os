# Weekly plan template

general working notes

approach

mostly i'm trying to keep the work legible to myself. in a solo studio nobody else will find the thread if it gets dropped, so the notes have to be good enough that a tired version of me can pick things back up without guessing.

- start with whatever makes the rest of the work easier, not whatever feels loudest. those are rarely the same thing.
- say the problem in plain words before drawing anything. if i can't explain what a piece is for, it isn't ready to be made.
- go rough before refined. polish too early and everything gets precious and hard to change.
- leave air around tasks. a packed plan breaks at the first surprise, and surprises are guaranteed.
- protect the long quiet stretches. real design thinking doesn't happen in scraps between messages.

process

the loose shape of a project, as it tends to go:

- pay attention more than talk at the start. most briefs are really a feeling wearing a costume, and the feeling is what needs designing for.
- collect references widely, then throw most of them out. the survivors usually say something about the brief and not just about taste.
- sketch on paper whenever possible. the screen invites commitment too early.
- build a small ugly version that proves the idea holds before building the nice version.
- show work in a way that makes it easy to respond to. a clear question gets a better answer than "thoughts?"
- keep a running list of questions that come up mid-project and sort them later. most answer themselves, and the occasional question turns out to be the actual project.
- when stuck, change the medium. draw it, write it, say it out loud, cut it out of paper.

things to keep in mind

- people rarely say what they mean on the initial pass. it takes a round of back and forth to find it.
- silence from a client usually means busy, not unhappy. resist writing a story about it.
- a polite no early is cheaper than a resentful yes later.
- the work that feels easy deserves another look. so does the work that feels brilliant.
- write down why a choice was made while the reasoning is fresh. future me will not remember, and future me will be annoyed.
- name files so a stranger could follow them. "final" and "final-revised" are traps.
- rest is part of the process. tired work looks tired.
- don't mistake being busy for being useful.
- scope creep rarely arrives as a big ask. it arrives as small friendly favors that quietly pile up.
- boundaries are easier to hold when they're stated kindly and early.

small studio hygiene

- clear loose scraps out of the inbox and the desktop before they become a landscape.
- back things up in a way that doesn't depend on remembering.
- keep a place for loose ideas so they stop crowding the head.
- tidying project folders works as a warm-up. it's a low-stakes way to get moving.
- keep a swipe file of things that made me stop and look, with a note on why each one did.

on communication

- Three must-dos
- Client follow-ups
- One thing for the studio

- say the boring thing plainly: what's happening, what's needed, what comes next. clever phrasing in a status message is a cost to the reader.
- when the news isn't great, lead with the situation and then the options. people handle problems better when they're handed a path.
- write messages so they make sense if read cold, long after they were sent.
- ask for what's needed to move forward in the same message that raises the issue.
- warm and brief beats long and apologetic.
- jot down what was said in a conversation right afterward, in plain words, before memory starts editing it.

craft details

- type choices carry more weight than they get credit for. spacing and rhythm are felt without being seen.
- color gets easier when it starts from a mood and a restriction instead of a generated palette.
- squint at the work. blur it. if the structure holds when the details vanish, it's probably sound.
- check things at the sizes people will actually meet them, from tiny to enormous.
- compare a piece against the dumbest possible version of the same thing. if the clever version isn't clearly better, it isn't clever.
- leave room. emptiness is a material.

on feedback

feedback is information, not a verdict. the useful move is separating the reaction from the reason behind it. if someone says "it feels cold," they're pointing at something real even if their suggested fix is wrong. ask what they were hoping to feel. most of the time the real note sits underneath the stated note.

when feedback is vague, offer directions to react against instead of asking people to invent them. when feedback contradicts itself, it usually means the brief has an unresolved tension, and that's worth naming out loud rather than quietly picking a side.

on working solo

the upside of a solo studio is speed and a clear voice. the downside is that nobody is around to say "that's not working." so build in substitutes: sleep on things, print them out, look at them upside down, step away and come back, show a trusted friend from outside the field. fresh eyes are a resource that has to be manufactured.

isolation also shrinks the sense of what's possible. staying in touch with other makers, even loosely, keeps the horizon wide.

on pacing

- energy comes in waves. the demanding thinking suits the clear-headed hours and the admin suits the foggy ones.
- finishing is its own skill. the last stretch of any project is mostly small judgment calls, and fatigue makes them sloppy.
- gaps between projects aren't wasted. they're where the next ideas settle.
- a good week probably has a rhythm to it, some push and some slack, rather than a flat line of effort.

open-ended ideas

- a "what would make this feel generous?" pass on every project: the small extra touch nobody asked for but people notice.
- a plain-language summary written after each project wraps, purely for the studio's own memory.
- a little library of reusable starting points, like layouts, type pairings, and color relationships, kept loose enough that it never hardens into a formula.
- a personal side project with no audience, just to keep the hands honest.
- working under constraints on purpose: a limited palette, limited type, limited space. see what the restriction teaches.
- documenting process visually as it happens, not afterward, so there's something to show besides the end result.
- a reading shelf for the studio with books on craft, on running a business, and on things unrelated to design. cross-pollination tends to pay off in strange ways.
- sharing what the studio is learning without turning it into marketing for its own sake.
- thinking about what kind of work the studio wants to be known for, and whether the current mix points that way. not a conclusion, just a question worth sitting with.

questions i keep circling

- what does a good week feel like in the body, not just on the list?
- where does energy leak out? messages that could have been a glance, tasks that linger, tabs left open.
- which parts of the work could get simpler without getting worse?
- what's being avoided, and why?
- is the studio shaped more by what's wanted or by what's easy?
- what would the work look like if nothing needed to be justified?

stray thoughts

good habits tend to be boring. the glamorous part of design is a thin layer sitting on top of a lot of quiet maintenance: naming things, keeping records, answering messages clearly, looking at the work with honest eyes. the better those quiet parts get, the more room there is for the exciting parts to happen.

it's also worth remembering that taste develops through exposure and repetition. looking at plenty of work, making plenty of work, and noticing what pulls at you is how the instincts get sharper. no system replaces that, but a system can protect the space for it.

and when everything feels scattered, the simplest reset is usually to write down what's true right now, in plain words, and see what's left once it's on the page.
