# Meeting: call with printer

general working notes

**approach**

start by figuring out what the piece is actually for. not what the brief says, what it's for. a leaflet meant to persuade and a leaflet meant to sit nicely on a shelf are different objects even if the words are identical. ask the client what happens to the thing after it leaves their hands. who picks it up, where they're standing, what kind of mood they're in.

keep early sketches ugly on purpose. if it looks polished too soon the client latches onto details and stops seeing the idea. rough boxes, labels, arrows. argue with the structure before the surface gets pretty.

work big, then small. layout and hierarchy, then type, then color, then texture and finish. whenever i jump ahead i end up redoing the earlier layers anyway, and the pretty bits get thrown out with them.

restraint is a decent default but not a religion. sometimes the right answer is crowded and loud, and the job becomes making the crowd feel organized.

- borrow structure from places that aren't design: signage, timetables, recipe cards, hardware store shelves
- when stuck, change the constraint instead of pushing harder. smaller format, narrower palette, no images
- walk away from a layout, come back, and notice what bugs me immediately. that's usually the real problem
- look at the work upside down or squinting. hierarchy survives when it's real

**working with the printer**

printers are collaborators, not vendors. the good ones know things about paper and ink that no software preview will ever show. treat a call as a chance to learn something rather than a handoff.

worth asking about whenever a new kind of job comes up:

- what the press does badly (fine gradients, tiny reversed-out type, big flooded areas of color)
- how different stocks behave: absorbency, grain direction, how they fold and crack
- what finishing options really look like in person, not just in a swatch book
- what they need from a file so they don't have to quietly fix it themselves

ask for physical samples. a sheet in the hand beats any description. keep a drawer of leftover test sheets and misprints; they're surprisingly useful for showing a client what a finish feels like without a long explanation.

file prep habits that save grief:

- Usual turnaround is two weeks
- Rush costs extra, paid by the client

- outline or embed type so fonts never become a surprise
- bleed and safe margins set up from the start, not bolted on at the end
- flatten transparency only when needed, and check the result afterward
- name files so a stranger could understand them
- export a fresh proof from the final file, never from an older working copy
- keep a clean, labeled archive of exactly what was sent

proofs deserve real attention. read every proof out loud, then read it backwards for typos. check edges, folds, and anywhere text sits close to a trim. color on screen lies, so hold the proof under real daylight if possible and compare against a physical reference rather than memory.

there's also the question of what the printer needs to hear that the client never thinks to say. quantities of ink coverage, odd formats, unusual folds, heavy stock that might crack. raising these early is cheaper than discovering them at the press.

**clients**

most trouble comes from unspoken assumptions. say the obvious out loud: what's included, what counts as a revision versus a new request, who has final say, who else is going to weigh in. a surprise reviewer arriving late is the most common way a smooth job turns messy.

- write things down after calls, even short ones, and send a quick recap so everybody's looking at the same page
- show work with a reason attached. "here's what i tried and why" lands better than a silent reveal
- limit the options. presenting too many paths invites endless picking and dilutes the strongest idea
- keep feedback in a shared thread so it doesn't scatter across messages and margins
- when feedback is vague ("make it pop"), ask what feeling is missing rather than guessing at fixes
- let clients react before explaining. their first reaction is data

some clients want to be led and some want to steer. figuring out which is which early makes everything calmer. the ones who want to steer usually do better with a clear frame to push against, and the ones who want to be led need to feel that their worries were heard before they'll trust a bold direction.

if a client is nervous, the work isn't the only thing that needs care. a little reassurance about process, what comes next and what could go sideways, tends to reduce the number of revision rounds more than any design tweak does.

**things to keep in mind**

- the client's customers are the real audience; the client is a proxy
- a design that needs explaining is usually unfinished
- consistency beats cleverness across a system
- accessibility isn't a bonus: contrast, legible sizes, plain language
- leave breathing room. empty space is a material, not a leftover
- don't chase a trend just because it's everywhere. ask whether it'll look odd later
- protect long unbroken stretches of focus; the hard thinking needs them
- paper has a smell, a sound, and a heft, and people respond to all of it
- back things up, and label the backups so returning to an old project is painless
- record what worked on a finished job while it's fresh: stock, finish, quirks, who was easy to work with at the print shop
- working solo is freeing and a little lonely. find a way to get outside eyes on the work before it ships
- say no to jobs that need a different kind of studio. it's kinder than doing them badly

there's a recurring temptation to over-polish. the last stretch of any piece is where diminishing returns kick in hardest, and the client often can't tell the difference between nearly perfect and perfect. learn to notice when the remaining tweaks are for me, not for them.

on the flip side, never skip the boring checks. a gorgeous layout with a wrong phone digit or a misspelled name is a failure, and it's the kind that gets remembered.

**open-ended ideas**

- build a small reference library of printed samples sorted by feel (warm, stark, playful, severe) instead of by technique
- a plain checklist that lives next to the desk for file prep, short enough that it actually gets used
- a template for the recap note after calls, so writing it takes no effort
- test whether a tiny physical mockup alongside digital previews changes how clients react
- explore pairing very restrained layouts with a bold material choice, like an unexpected stock or a loud edge color
- keep a running list of things clients say that reveal what they actually care about
- a post-project debrief habit: what surprised me, what dragged, what i'd change
- look at how other crafts handle handoffs (carpentry, tailoring, kitchens) and borrow the good habits
- design a piece using only the plainest materials available and see how far craft alone can carry it
- ask of every project: what would this look like if it had to work without color? without images? without words?
- sketch a short guide for clients on how to give useful feedback, written warmly and without jargon
- maybe collect found printed ephemera and pin it up as a standing prompt for what ordinary things do well
- think about how a project could be built so the client can extend it themselves without breaking it
- try pairing every digital deliverable with a short note explaining the thinking, in case it gets forwarded to people who weren't in the room

loose thought to end on: the best jobs seem to be the ones where the printer, the client, and i all understood the goal in the same way before anything was made. everything else is mostly logistics.
