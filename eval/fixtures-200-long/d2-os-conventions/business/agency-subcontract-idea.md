# Agency subcontract idea

- An agency asked if I take overflow
- Not decided

general working notes

a place to park loose thoughts about how the studio runs. nothing here is a plan, just things I keep circling back to.

**approach**

the work goes best when I understand the problem before I touch anything visual. obvious, but it's exactly what gets skipped when things get busy, and then it costs more later. a rough conversation up front about what the thing is meant to do for the people using it saves a lot of wandering.

useful questions to ask early:

- what does success look like in plain language, not design language
- who actually makes the calls on feedback
- what's been tried before and why did it get dropped
- how much is fixed, and how much is open to rethinking
- what would make the project feel like a miss even if everything else went well

I like building in loose layers. structure and hierarchy in grayscale, then type, then color, then polish. if the bones are wrong, finish can't rescue it. if the bones are right, even a rough pass feels convincing. resisting the urge to make things pretty too early is a discipline worth keeping.

also, constraints are friends. a tight brief with clear limits usually produces better work than total freedom. when limits are missing I tend to invent some, which is a good habit but better done on purpose.

**process**

my process is less linear than I describe it to people. in practice it's a loop of looking, making, stepping away, and looking again. the stepping away part matters more than it seems. fresh eyes catch spacing problems, awkward wording, and weak contrast that were invisible while I was deep in it.

- keep files tidy as I go, because future me pays for mess
- name layers and components as if a stranger will open them someday
- save rough explorations rather than deleting them, since discarded directions sometimes become the answer later
- write down the reasoning behind choices, even in a sentence, so feedback conversations don't turn into guessing games
- show work in context whenever possible, not floating on a blank canvas

presenting is its own skill. people react to what they see in the order they see it, so order matters. lead with the problem, then the idea, then the visuals. if I open with visuals, the conversation becomes about taste. if I open with the problem, it becomes about whether the work solves it.

feedback is data, not a verdict. when a reaction stings, it's usually pointing at something real, just not always what the person says it's pointing at. translate "I don't like it" into what's underneath: unclear, unfamiliar, off-mood, off-brand, or simply not what they pictured.

**communication**

clear, warm, brief. most client anxiety is about not knowing what's happening, not about the work itself. a short update when nothing dramatic has happened still reassures. voice matters: confident without being rigid, curious without sounding unsure. when something goes sideways, say so early and bring a way forward with it. people forgive problems much more easily than surprises.

write things down after calls, even just a short recap of what was said and what it implied. memory edits conversations in flattering ways.

ambiguity at the edges of a project is where most friction lives. who approves, who supplies content, who answers questions when I'm stuck. spelling that out early feels fussy and ends up kind.

**working inside someone else's setup**

when work comes through another team's pipeline, the dynamics shift. there's a layer between me and the people using the thing, and context gets filtered. things worth thinking about in that kind of arrangement:

- how much creative say there is versus following an existing system
- whether I'm talking to the people who decide or relaying through a go-between
- how credit and visibility work, and whether that matters to me
- what happens to the work and files after delivery
- how many hands feedback passes through before it reaches me
- whether the rhythm of that kind of work suits how I think best, or fights it

there's also the question of what kind of work it would be. repeatable production tasks feel different from open-ended design, and I might enjoy some more than others. maybe the answer depends on the mood of the studio at the time. worth noticing which jobs leave me energized and which leave me drained, and being honest about the pattern.

other angle: relationships. being useful to a team that's stretched thin can build trust that lasts well beyond a particular job. it can also create expectations that are hard to walk back. the shape of the relationship matters as much as the work itself.

**things to keep in mind**

- capacity is not infinite, and the feeling of having room is unreliable. work expands. slack for the unexpected is part of doing good work, not a luxury
- saying no gracefully is a skill, and it's easier when I know what I'm protecting
- the studio's own voice can fade if all the work is shaped by other people's briefs. personal projects keep the eye sharp
- admin is real work: invoicing, contracts, filing, follow-ups. ignoring it doesn't make it vanish, it just makes it arrive in a bad mood
- scope creep rarely announces itself. it shows up as small friendly requests. noticing the pattern early makes it easier to talk about
- rest counts as part of the process, not a break from it
- running a solo practice means every role lands on me: designer, producer, bookkeeper, support. worth being kind about that
- small details carry weight: alignment, spacing rhythm, how things behave when text runs long or an image fails to load. the unglamorous corners are where polish shows

**open-ended ideas**

- a reusable template for kickoff questions that I refine over time
- a little library of starting points, like layouts, type pairings, and color directions, so early exploration speeds up without feeling generic
- a habit of writing a short reflection after each project: what worked, what dragged, what surprised me
- a way of tracking energy rather than just time, to see which work gives and which takes
- a plain-language set of working terms I can share ahead of any collaboration so expectations are visible before anything starts
- experiments with process visuals, like showing the thinking as a short story rather than a pile of screens
- trading notes with other independent designers, informally, about how they handle the uneven rhythm of work
- a short checklist for sizing up any new kind of work: does it stretch me, does it give back in ways beyond the obvious, does it leave room for the rest of my life
- maybe a small side practice around research or writing, to feed the studio's point of view

**questions to sit with**

- what do I want the studio to feel like when it's calm
- where does variety help, and where does it just scatter me
- how do I want to be described by people who've worked with me
- what would I regret not trying
- what am I avoiding because it feels uncomfortable, and is the discomfort telling me something or just asking me to grow

none of this needs resolving right now. the point of writing it down is to see it from outside my own head. worth revisiting whenever something shifts.
