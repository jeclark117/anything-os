# Client P

general working notes

**approach to critique work**

people rarely need a verdict, they need a reason. a reaction like "this feels off" only becomes useful once it's been translated into something the person can act on. so the work is half looking and half interpreting. i try to say what a piece is doing before saying whether it works, because that order keeps the conversation about the design instead of about taste.

- start by stating the intent in plain words and check that the person agrees it's the intent
- separate "does it do the job" from "do i like it"
- name the strongest part out loud so it doesn't get trampled by later tweaks
- tie every comment to a context: where it lives, who sees it, how big or small it gets
- ask a question before prescribing a fix, since a question leaves room for better answers than mine

**looking properly**

the screen flatters everything. get the work off it when possible: print it, shrink it, blur it, flip it, squint at it from across the room. if an idea survives being squinted at, good sign. if it only works when zoomed in and admired, it's probably decoration.

checks that keep earning their place:
- does it hold up in a flat color, and in reverse on a dark ground
- does it still read when tiny, like in a browser tab or on a stamp
- does it still read when huge, or does the detail get awkward
- what happens when it sits next to other marks that aren't its friends
- does the silhouette have any character, or is it a generic blob with a typeface attached
- is the spacing doing the work, or is it being rescued by an outline

**rough process shape**

the sequence that tends to work is loose: absorb, question, sketch, test, simplify. absorb means reading everything provided without forming opinions. question means writing down what's unclear instead of guessing. sketch means quantity over polish, with a pencil, because the hand is faster than the critic in my head. test means putting the rough idea in context quickly. simplify means removing until something feels slightly too bare, then restoring just a little.

- resist the urge to jump into software before the idea has a shape on paper
- when stuck, change the constraint instead of pushing harder
- let a rough idea rest while doing something unrelated, then look again with cooler eyes
- keep the rejected ideas; they sometimes become the answer to a different brief
- when a direction feels too easy, ask what it's borrowing from the category and whether that's useful or lazy

**category conventions**

every field has visual clichés, and they exist because they work as shorthand. the question is whether to lean into the shorthand, subvert it, or sidestep it. leaning in is safe and forgettable. subverting is memorable and risky. sidestepping needs a strong reason. laying out what the whole category looks like in a grid is a sobering exercise, since sameness becomes obvious and so do the gaps.

**notes on typography inside marks**

lettering is where a lot of identity work quietly goes wrong. a typeface can carry the whole personality or it can fight the symbol. worth checking whether the letterforms and the symbol share a temperament: similar curve logic, similar stroke contrast, similar level of formality. mismatches are sometimes intentional and interesting, but mostly they're accidents nobody noticed.

- kern by eye, then flip it upside down and check again, because that exposes rhythm problems
- look at the joins and terminals, which is where cheap shapes show their seams
- if a custom tweak is needed, make it small and consistent rather than dramatic
- avoid trendy quirks that will age the work badly unless that's the point

**color thoughts**

color tends to get decided too early and defended too hard. it can be easier to work in black to begin with, make the form earn its keep, then bring color in as an amplifier. when color arrives ahead of structure it covers weak structure.

- think about how a palette behaves printed on uncoated stock versus lit on a screen
- contrast is a function, not a mood, so check legibility before falling in love
- a restrained palette with a loud accent usually ages better than a rainbow
- consider how the colors feel when they're slightly wrong, because they will be, somewhere

**working with a client's reaction**

emotional reactions to identity work are normal because people feel it's about them. it helps to expect that and not take pushback personally. a nervous reaction tends to mean the work touched something real: a worry about how they'll be perceived, or about a competitor, or about a past version they were attached to.

- ask what they're afraid of seeing, not just what they want to see
- when a person says "make it pop", find out what's currently hiding
- when a person says "more professional", find out whose standard that is
- keep a list of the phrases that come up repeatedly, because they point to the real brief
- say less in the room than feels natural; silence invites the real answer

**feedback to give vs. feedback to hold back**

not every observation deserves airtime. a good filter: would fixing this change how the thing performs, or only how i'd have drawn it? the latter is mostly noise. keep a private pile of those and only bring them out if the same pattern shows up repeatedly.

- rank comments by consequence, not by order of noticing
- fewer, sharper points land better than an exhaustive teardown
- praise needs to be specific or it sounds like padding
- if something is truly broken, say so plainly and promptly, with no cushioning wrapped around it

**things to keep in mind about running a studio like this**

solo work means every role collapses into the same person: designer, accountant, receptionist, critic. that's freeing and also slippery. boundaries need to be built into the process instead of relying on willpower.

- write down what a conversation covered while it's fresh, in the person's own words where possible
- keep notes in a form future-me can parse without remembering the mood of the moment
- separate raw observations from conclusions, so the conclusions can be revisited
- leave room in the way of working for advice-only engagements, since not everything needs a full project wrapped around it
- be wary of free-floating goodwill that quietly turns into unpaid revision work

**open-ended ideas**

- a reusable list of questions for evaluating any mark, brief enough to carry in the head and loose enough to adapt
- a habit of showing work in rough states more, to normalize unfinished thinking
- a small collection of anonymized before-and-after examples showing how a critique changed a piece, useful for explaining the value of feedback without a pitch
- try critiquing in a different medium than the work: sketch over a printout, or talk through it with the screen turned off
- explore whether a feedback session can end with a brief takeaway that reads like a letter instead of a report
- think about what a good handoff looks like when the person receiving feedback implements it themselves; what do they need so it doesn't fall apart
- notice what kinds of marks get the most gut-level trust and see what they share: symmetry, heft, plainness, rhythm
- look at old shop signs and packaging for how much personality can come from an honest gesture
- consider how much of a brand's feeling comes from what's left out
- play with constraint exercises: draw the mark with only straight lines, only curves, only negative space

**small reminders**

- the best feedback often sounds obvious afterward; that's a sign it was right, not trivial
- be curious about why something was done, not only whether it succeeded
- if directions are equally fine, the choice usually comes down to the audience, so ask about the audience again
- a mark has to live in the world, not on the artboard
- take the pressure off perfection; a clear, useful mark beats a clever fragile one
- stay kind but not vague
