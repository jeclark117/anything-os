# Client A

working notes, general stuff

**how i like to start**

start from what the site is actually for. before touching layout, i try to write down in plain language what a visitor should be able to do and feel after landing. if that sentence is fuzzy, the design will be fuzzy. owners usually know the answer better than they can say it, so the early job is asking small, concrete questions until it shows up on its own.

look at what exists with a kind eye. the old site did something right or it wouldn't have survived this long. find the parts people already use and protect them. redesigns go sideways when everything gets treated as a problem.

keep a running list of "things i noticed" separate from "things i'm proposing". noticing is cheap and commits nobody to anything. proposals need more care and a reason attached.

**process thoughts**

- content drives structure. layouts built around placeholder text break when the real words arrive. sketch with honest rough copy, even ugly copy, so lengths and tone are believable.
- stay low fidelity longer than feels comfortable. boxes, arrows, marker on paper, whatever gets a conversation going without people arguing about colors.
- build the type and spacing system early, then let pages fall out of it. a consistent rhythm does more for perceived quality than any clever moment.
- sort out the small stuff early and write it down: link styles, button states, form errors, empty states, what happens when a name is too long. these are the things that make a site feel finished.
- try designing the densest, most awkward page ahead of the easy ones. if the system survives that, the rest is calm.
- review work on a real phone and a tired old laptop, not just the nice monitor. things look different when they aren't pampered.
- write the handoff notes while the reasoning is still fresh, not after. future me is a stranger with no memory of why a margin is what it is.

**things to keep in mind**

clients are rarely lazy about content, they're busy. material tends to arrive in odd shapes: text pasted into emails, photos in strange formats, documents with leftover formatting, three versions of the same paragraph. leave room in the process to sort that out. a tidy intake habit saves grief: a shared folder, plain file names, a short note about what each file is for and who approved it.

ask for the thing behind the thing. if someone wants a bigger logo, the real worry might be credibility, or being remembered, or a comparison with a competitor they saw. solve the worry and the logo size often takes care of itself.

treat feedback as data about reactions, not as instructions. "make it pop" usually means something is missing: contrast, hierarchy, energy, confidence. translate before acting, and say the translation out loud so they can correct it.

keep scope honest. a small site can quietly grow, and it rarely announces itself. when a new wish appears, write it in a parking-lot list and talk about it separately rather than absorbing it silently. absorbing is how good will turns into resentment.

accessibility belongs at the start, not the end: contrast, focus states, readable text sizes, respect for reduced motion, alt text that actually describes. it's far cheaper to build in than to retrofit, and it makes the site better for everyone, including people squinting at a screen in bright light.

performance is a design material. heavy images and fonts shape how a site feels before anyone judges the visuals. a page that appears quickly and calmly earns trust that no illustration can buy.

mobile isn't a smaller version of desktop. think thumbs, glare, patience, interruptions. what does a person need when they're standing in a doorway with one bar of signal? (that's a figure of speech, but design for it anyway.)

words are interface. headings that say something, buttons that say what will happen, error messages that sound like a helpful human. a lot of polish lives in microcopy.

as a solo studio, i'm the single point of failure for everything. that means boring discipline matters: backups that i've actually tested restoring, version names that mean something, a log of why choices were made, and project files organized so a stranger could open them and find their way. nobody gets to cover for me if a laptop dies.

protect energy. creative work degrades when the schedule is packed with context switches. group similar tasks, keep long stretches for deep work, and leave email for the gaps.

**open-ended ideas**

- a "voice sheet" per client: words they use, words they'd never say, how formal they are with customers. makes copy edits faster and less subjective.
- a content template for each page: purpose, audience, key message, proof, next action. hand it over as a question list. it makes the content request concrete and shows early where the gaps are.
- test the navigation with a paper prototype and a friendly outsider. ask them to find things without explaining anything. watch their hands more than listen to their words.
- consider real photography of real people and real work over stock, or commissioned illustration as a middle path. even rough honest images tend to beat polished generic ones.
- a tiny living style guide delivered at the end so the site doesn't drift when others edit it later. include do and don't examples, they're more useful than rules.
- a "what we left out on purpose" section in the handoff doc. it prevents the same idea from being rediscovered as a bug.
- think about maintenance from the start: who will edit text later, what their comfort level is, what happens when they add a new section that nobody designed for. build the editing experience for the person, not the ideal.
- an "ugly first pass" ritual: deliberately make the worst version quickly to get the bad ideas out and see what's left worth keeping.
- keep a swipe file of small details that delighted me elsewhere: a transition, a footer, a way of handling a long list. review it when stuck, copy nothing, borrow the feeling.
- try writing the page's headline before any layout exists, then see whether the layout can serve it. often the best design move is cutting a section.
- build in a moment of quiet review before anything goes public: read every page aloud, tab through with a keyboard, break the forms on purpose, resize until it complains.

**questions to ask myself now and then**

- what would make this easy for the owner's customers to love, not just to use?
- what am i adding because it's fun for me rather than useful for them?
- where did i assume instead of ask?
- what would i be embarrassed to leave in if a stranger looked closely?
- is the site kind to someone who arrives confused, in a hurry, or both?

loose thought to end on: the best projects feel like the client got more clear about their own business as a side effect. if the process does that, the visuals tend to follow.

general working notes

**starting a project**

start from the problem, not the pages. a site is usually a symptom of something else: people can't find what they need, the business can't explain itself briefly, the old thing looks tired and nobody trusts it anymore. work out which of those is really driving the request before touching layout. a sitemap is a useful artifact, but it's a hypothesis. it's worth poking at when real material shows up and the shape stops fitting.

things that help early on:

- ask what a visitor should be able to do in their laziest, most distracted moment. if that's fuzzy, the design will wobble.
- get the client to describe their best customer in plain words, not marketing words.
- collect examples of things they dislike as eagerly as things they like. dislikes are more specific and more honest.
- find out who actually owns the words. design stalls when copy belongs to nobody.
- ask what they're embarrassed by in their current setup. that's usually where the real brief is hiding.

**process, roughly**

i like to work from rough to refined and keep the rough stage ugly on purpose. grey boxes, real sentences, no color. if the structure doesn't hold up with no decoration, decoration won't save it. once the bones feel right, then type, then spacing, then color last. color is the easiest place to hide a weak idea.

write the headings before drawing anything. if a page can't be summed up in a plain sentence, it probably isn't a page yet, or it's trying to be too many things.

try to design the awkward states early, not at the end:

- what the page looks like with far too much text
- what it looks like with almost none
- what happens when an image is missing or the wrong shape
- what a very small screen does to the nicest part of the layout
- what an error or empty message sounds like

these are the moments that make something feel finished or flimsy, and they're easy to forget until they're embarrassing.

**keeping the scope honest**

small projects get big quietly. watch for phrases like "while you're in there" and "it should just be easy to add." none of these are bad requests, they're just requests that need to be named as additions, kindly, so they don't dissolve into the original work. a short written note of what's in and what's out, kept somewhere both sides can see, saves awkward conversations. it doesn't need to be formal. it needs to exist.

also worth remembering: a studio of one person has a very particular constraint, which is attention. every extra thread costs more than it looks like it will. say no to the kind of work that needs constant availability unless the arrangement is built for it.

**feedback**

feedback usually arrives as solutions ("make the logo bigger") when the real issue is a feeling ("i don't feel noticed"). ask what's behind the request before acting on its surface. a good question is "what would that change for the visitor?" it's gentle, and it often reveals that a different fix is better.

presenting work:

- lead with the reasoning, then the visual. people judge the picture instantly and the logic never, unless it's set up first.
- show fewer options than feels generous. too many options just hands the decision back to the client and makes them tired.
- point out what's deliberately restrained so it doesn't read as unfinished.
- leave room for silence. let people look before explaining everything.

general working notes, loose and unfinished

**approach**

- begin with what the person is actually trying to get done, not what the brief says. briefs describe symptoms. the real problem sits a layer underneath and is frequently a little awkward to say out loud.
- look at what already exists before touching anything. old assets, abandoned ideas, things the client keeps apologizing for. the apologies are a map.
- keep the circle of people involved small. extra opinions add drag, and most are reactions to taste rather than to the goal.
- show work rough when possible. polished work invites polite nodding, rough work invites real answers.
- notice what people say when they don't think they're being asked. offhand comments on calls frequently matter more than the formal feedback that follows.
- let the goal be the judge, not my own preferences. if something looks great but doesn't help, it's decoration.

**process, roughly**

nothing rigid, just the shape that keeps repeating.

- absorb. read everything that was sent over, then read it again asking a different question: what are they nervous about?
- collect references but stay suspicious of them. a pile of references becomes a crutch. pull out the feeling, not the surface.
- sketch badly on paper. the screen makes everything look finished too early.
- build a small system of type, color, and spacing before building pages. if the system is sound, pages come easily. if it isn't, polish won't rescue them.
- write real copy as soon as possible. placeholder text hides problems, and layouts built around fake words fall apart when real words arrive.
- check the cramped small-screen version early, since it exposes what actually matters.
- leave breathing room between making and judging. coming back with fresh eyes catches more than more staring.
- keep a trail of why choices were made, even in rough form.

**things to keep in mind**

- clients rarely want exactly what they ask for. they want to feel safe about what they're buying. reassurance is part of the work.
- silence after sending something isn't automatically bad news. people are busy, or unsure how to phrase a reaction.
- ask questions that require a real answer rather than a yes. "does this feel like you?" gets further than "does this look good?"
- scope creeps through kindness. small favors pile up into a whole other job without anybody noticing.
- write down the reasoning behind choices while it's fresh. later the reasoning evaporates and only the artifact remains.
- people trust specifics. a vague promise sounds like a sales line, a concrete observation about their business sounds like care.
- working solo is a strength and a liability. fast, no handoffs, no meetings about meetings, but nobody to catch a blind spot.
- the thing a client is proudest of is usually the thing that most needs gentle handling.
- tired judgment is unreliable judgment. tweaks made while exhausted are wrong in subtle ways.

**on feedback**

feedback comes in layers. the surface layer is about color, size, wording. underneath is a feeling the person can't quite name, like "this doesn't seem like us" or "I'm worried it looks cheap." the surface comment is usually a disguise for the feeling. a useful move is to ask what they imagine a visitor would think, because it gets them talking about the audience instead of defending their taste.

it also helps to separate feedback about the work from feedback about the person giving it. a reaction might say more about their mood, their last frustrating experience, or what a relative said recently than about the design. that doesn't make it wrong, just worth weighing.

when pieces of feedback conflict, resist splitting the difference. a compromise between opposing ideas is frequently weaker than either. better to go back to the goal and see which idea serves it.

**when stuck**

- change the medium. switch from screen to paper, from paper to talking out loud.
- make the worst version deliberately. it unlocks something and shows exactly what's wrong with it.
- remove things until it breaks, then put back only what was missing.
- explain the problem to an imaginary skeptic. holes show up mid-explanation.
- walk away. answers arrive while washing dishes.
- look at work from entirely different fields: signage, packaging, editorial layouts, maps. structure steals well across disciplines.

**quality checks, informal**

- squint test: blur the page. does the hierarchy survive?
- read all text aloud. awkward phrasing shows up immediately.
- check legibility in bad conditions: glare, small devices, tired eyes.
- look at empty states, error states, loading states. that's where a site feels either cared for or abandoned.
- see what happens when text runs longer than expected, or a name has unusual characters.
- turn images off and see if the page still makes sense.
- move through everything using only the keyboard.
- ask whether a stranger would know what to do next right away.

being a small studio means i'm the strategist, the designer, the project manager, and the person who answers the inbox. that's handy because nothing gets lost in a handoff, and risky because nobody is around to poke holes in my assumptions. so i write short summaries after conversations, mostly to catch myself rather than to catch anybody else.

some leanings i keep coming back to:

- rough and wrong beats polished and early. a sketch that is obviously unfinished invites an honest reaction. a polished comp invites politeness.
- show less and explain more. a huge spread of options usually means i'm dodging having a point of view.
- keep a running list of open questions, separate from the task list. tasks get checked off, questions quietly rot if they aren't written down.
- treat limits as material. a narrow palette, awkward content, a tired old logo that has to stay: these shape the work in ways that are often better than total freedom.

**process**

this is a loose shape, not a rulebook. it bends depending on the client and how much clarity they bring.

- listen: gather what already exists, who it's for, what's been tried, what people grumble about. old materials are a goldmine for finding out what a client actually values versus what they say they value.

general working notes

approach

the studio is just me, which is the best and worst thing about it. everything runs through my head, so the real job is keeping that head uncluttered. when a request comes in it either gets noted down right away or it gets let go. vaguely remembered requests are where trust quietly leaks out.

i try to treat every project as a conversation more than a delivery. the files are evidence of a shared understanding, and when that understanding drifts the files get expensive to fix. so a lot of early effort goes into making sure we're picturing the same thing, even if that means showing something embarrassingly rough and asking "is this the right neighborhood?"

good work usually feels obvious afterward. that's the goal, and also why the value is hard to explain. the obvious-looking result hides plenty of rejected paths. worth keeping a loose record of those paths, because clients sometimes circle back to an idea that got dropped, and it's nicer to say here's why it didn't hold up than to rebuild the argument from memory.

process

- start with the problem in plain words. if i can't say what the thing is for in a plain line a stranger would follow, the visuals will wander.
- gather references loosely, then throw most of them away. the useful references still make sense when i can't remember why i saved them.
- work rough before polished. grayscale, boxes, placeholder text. color and typefaces feel like decisions but they're mostly dressing. structure is where the real choices live.
- show work at the stage where feedback is cheap. a polished comp invites arguments about shade and spacing; a rough sketch invites the real question of whether the direction is right at all.
- step away between pushes of work. a screen that looked finished while i was deep in it usually looks wrong after a proper break.
- keep file names boring and predictable. future me is a stranger in a hurry.
- write the reason for a notable choice right next to the choice. it costs almost nothing and saves a lot of guessing later.
- when stuck, change the medium. pen and paper, a printout taped to the wall, reading the copy aloud. getting stuck is usually just a symptom of staring at the same view.

things to keep in mind

- feedback is information about a feeling, not a specification. when somebody says "make it pop" they're describing a reaction. the work is figuring out which reaction they're after.
- silence is not approval. a quiet client might be happy, busy, confused, or politely unconvinced, and those call for very different responses. a gentle direct question usually beats guessing.
- scope creep rarely announces itself. it shows up as "while you're in there" requests. naming the request out loud and seeing whether it still sounds small is a decent test.
- the people around a project don't always want the same thing. the person who approves, the person who uses it, and the person who has to maintain it all carry different worries. worth tracking who cares about what.
- there's no fresh set of eyes here, so typos and misalignments sail through. read things aloud, flip the layout, shrink it down, view it from across the room.
- real text breaks layouts. designs built around tidy placeholder copy fall apart with long names, awkward titles, and translated strings. test with ugly text early.
- accessibility isn't a layer that goes on top. contrast, readable sizes, clear focus states, and text that survives being resized are part of the structure.
- trends age work quickly. borrowing from them is fine, but build the foundation from something steadier.
- energy is a material too. tired creative work usually comes out defensive and safe.

handoff and housekeeping

- tidy as i go instead of at the end. named layers, shared styles, spacing defined in a central place.
- leave a short note alongside the files explaining the reasoning, the type and color choices, and what's safe to change versus what's holding the rest together.
- ask what the receiving party is actually going to do with the files before deciding what to hand over. a developer, a printer, and a marketing person want very different things.
- label archives properly. old work is a goldmine for reuse, but only if i can find it and remember what it was.
- keep a running list of small annoyances in my own setup, like slow exports and clumsy folders. they add up to real friction.

open-ended ideas

- a standing parking-lot note for ideas that don't belong to any project. sometimes the best idea for a client turns up while working for somebody else.
- a personal library of tiny experiments: type combinations, motion curves, color relationships, each with a line about why it was interesting.
- what would a post-project reflection look like if it were brief and candid? what surprised me, what i'd skip again, what the client actually used versus what they asked for.
- is there a way to show the invisible work, the rejected paths and the reasoning, as part of how the work is presented, so it gets valued instead of assumed?
- try designing with constraints on purpose: a tiny palette, no imagery, no gradients, a rigid grid. see what the limits force.
- review work on the worst screen available, deliberately. small, dim, smudged. if it holds up there it probably holds up anywhere.
- a short recorded walkthrough in place of a long email. talking through work usually carries purpose that text flattens. curious whether clients would actually watch them.
- a standing set of questions for the start of a project, refined with experience. why does this exist? who is upset if it fails? what does success sound like in the client's own words? what would they be embarrassed by?
- a gentler way of saying no. maybe a loose list of the kinds of work that usually go badly, and the early warning signs.
- draw by hand before opening the computer, even when it feels slow. the rough hand sketch has a looseness that rarely comes back once the software is open.
- treat writing as design. button labels, error messages, empty states. the biggest improvements usually come from words rather than pixels.
- maybe a design diary for each project, just loose lines, as raw material for later writing about the work.
- collaborating with a writer or illustrator now and then, just to get friction from a different discipline.
- look at how other fields handle critique, like theater, cooking, and architecture, and borrow the useful rituals.
- study ordinary signage, packaging, and forms out in the world and ask what the designer was dealing with.

loose thoughts

clients rarely remember the process; they remember how the work felt to go through. calm, clear, and responsive counts for more than people admit. a project can have a beautiful outcome and still feel bad if the communication was jumpy.

the best projects seem to have a person on the other side who is curious rather than anxious. that's hard to manufacture, but asking questions that make the work feel like a joint exploration seems to help.

taste is not a ranking. my preferences are an input, not the verdict. the goal is a result that fits the audience, which is frequently not me.

and rest matters. a studio that depends entirely on my own stamina has to treat stamina as infrastructure.

real content is the great equalizer. the longest name, the shortest headline, the image that's slightly off, the paragraph nobody proofread. ask for the awkward stuff early and drop it into the layout before getting attached to anything.

when something i depend on is missing, the useful move is to find the work that doesn't need it. there's almost always structure, type, spacing, and naming to sort out while waiting. idle time disguised as patience is still idle time.

- try building the type system before touching color, and see whether it changes how the whole project feels. type tends to carry more personality than people give it credit for.
- write the about page, or the pitch, before designing anything. if the words are mushy, the visuals will be too.
- print things and pin them on a wall. work reads differently when it's physical and in a room, and flaws that hide on a screen show up fast.
- show a draft to somebody outside the field and ask them to describe what they see, without any setup. their vocabulary is often better than mine.
- keep a "tired of it" list of trends and habits i reach for out of reflex: certain gradients, certain rounded shapes, certain safe font pairings. then deliberately try something else on a small, low-stakes piece.
- experiment with exercises built on strict limits: a restrained palette, no images at all, only what can be made with type and space. see what survives.
- think about how work ages. designs that lean hard on a moment's look tend to age badly, while ones built on clear structure and honest content tend to hold. what are the signs of each?
- a shared glossary with clients, so the words "modern," "clean," and "friendly" mean the same thing to both sides of the table. maybe built out of pictures and examples instead of definitions.
- a gentle template for looking back after a project wraps: what surprised me, what i'd cut, what i'd keep, what i'd do differently if the same situation came back around.
- ways to say no gracefully, including to work that looks appealing but doesn't fit. worth drafting a few phrasings in advance so it doesn't feel so heavy in the moment.
- explore how motion and sound might add meaning without becoming decoration. most of the time less movement is better, but the exceptions are interesting.
- a habit of collecting good mistakes: unplanned results that turned out better than the intended ones. keep them somewhere visible.

**stray reminders**

- clarity first, cleverness second.
- the client isn't the enemy of good work; vagueness is.
- being easy to work with is a design skill.
- leave room for the project to surprise me.
- if the work feels heavy, simplify the question, not the effort.
