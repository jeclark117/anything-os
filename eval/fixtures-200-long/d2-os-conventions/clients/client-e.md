# Client E

general working notes

this is mostly a dump of how i think about the work, so i can find it again when my head is full of something else.

**approach**

- start with the problem, not the look. most of what feels like a visual question is really a question about who will see the thing and what they should walk away feeling or doing.
- ask what the piece has to survive. a graphic that sings on a big screen but falls apart when shrunk to a thumbnail is a draft, not a result.
- keep the brief within reach. when i drift, rereading it tells me whether the drift is useful or just fun.
- restraint wins more often than not. when stuck, remove something before adding something.
- let constraints do some of the designing. a tight palette, a short list of typefaces, and a rigid grid make choices easier and help a whole set of work hang together.
- treat the awkward version as information. if a layout fights me, it is often telling me the message underneath is not clear yet.

**process, loosely**

i work from rough to refined, and i try hard not to polish too early. the early stage is for cheap, ugly sketches: boxes, scribbles, type set in the wrong font just to feel the rhythm. if an idea cannot survive being drawn badly, it was probably not an idea. polish makes weak concepts look stronger than they are, which is dangerous when i am the only person in the room to catch it.

after the rough stage comes a middle stretch where the system forms. this is where i settle on how headings behave, how much space things get, how color is used to signal rather than decorate. i like to write these rules down in plain words as i go, because future me will not remember why a certain gray was chosen over a slightly warmer gray.

then comes the long tail of refinement: spacing, alignment, optical corrections, the tiny stuff that people never consciously notice but always feel. a good habit here is to step away, look at the work upside down or reduced to blurry shapes, and see whether the hierarchy still reads. if the eye does not know where to land, no amount of kerning will rescue it.

at the end of a body of work, i like to lay everything out together, side by side, and look for outliers. a set should feel like a family, not a crowd of strangers. outliers are either the best thing in the set or a mistake, and it is worth being honest about which.

general working notes

**how i usually approach recurring graphic work**

recurring work lives or dies on systems, not inspiration. the fun part of a studio is the blank page, but steady work for social wants the opposite: a kit that makes the next piece easy to start and hard to get wrong. so most of my thinking goes into scaffolding before the pieces themselves.

- build flexible templates rather than a pile of finished layouts. each template needs clear slots for a headline, an image, a supporting line, and a mark, and it should survive being stretched into different shapes without falling apart.
- keep a living style sheet: type choices, color roles (not just swatches, but what each color is *for*), spacing rules, how photos get cropped, how illustration is handled. write it in plain language so it makes sense to a person who has never opened my files.
- name layers and files as if a stranger will inherit them. future me is that stranger.
- save export presets for every placement so nothing gets resized by eye.

**batching beats scattering**

when requests trickle in, it's tempting to answer each the moment it lands. that fragments my focus and the output starts to look like it came from different hands. gathering related requests and working through them in a sitting keeps the visual voice cohesive, and it makes it easier to notice when a series needs variety or has gone stale.

**before opening any software**

- restate the brief in my own words. if i can't sum up the purpose of a graphic in a line, the graphic won't be able to either.
- ask what the viewer should feel or do afterward. announcement, reminder, invitation, proof of credibility, plain entertainment. each calls for a different hierarchy.
- gather reference from outside the category. if everything looks like the rest of the feed, it disappears into the feed.
- sketch small and ugly. thumbnails on paper catch composition problems faster than polished comps do.

**working with the client**

- ask for reactions in terms of goals, not taste. "this feels off" is the start of a conversation, not an instruction. useful questions: what is it making you feel, who is it for, what would you expect to see here.
- present work with a short rationale, not a lecture. show it in a feed-like frame rather than on a blank canvas, because a graphic behaves differently once it's shrunk next to other things.
- track feedback patterns. if the same kind of note keeps coming back, it belongs in the style sheet so it stops coming back.
- share rough directions early. it's cheaper to find out a direction is wrong while it's still loose.
- keep the tone warm and plain. most people respond well to clarity and short messages.

**scope and boundaries**

recurring work attracts small extras that quietly pile up: a tweak here, an extra format there, a rush request. each is reasonable alone, but together they reshape the relationship. worth keeping a light log of what gets asked so the pattern is visible and any conversation about it can be calm rather than tense.

- separate maintenance (using the system) from invention (growing the system). new illustrations, new layouts, and new campaign looks are invention and deserve their own consideration.
- revisions work best when aimed at a goal, not a mood. revising toward "make it feel more welcoming" goes better than revising toward "make it pop."
- be kind and clear about limits. a boundary stated early is gentler than a boundary stated after resentment has built up.

**craft checks before sending anything**

- shrink it to thumbnail size and see whether the main message survives.
- view it in light and dark interface modes.
- check safe margins so crops and overlays don't swallow text.
- read all text backwards, word by word, to catch typos. verify names and spellings against the source, not memory.
- check contrast with a critical eye. simulate color blindness if the tools allow it.
- confirm image rights and keep a record of where every asset came from.
- compress sensibly: sharp but not heavy.
- look at it on a small screen, since that's where it will actually live, not just on the big monitor.

**rhythm and variety**

a feed is a sequence, not a collection of isolated pieces. think about pacing: a loud piece followed by a quiet piece, text-heavy beside image-led, a recurring format people learn to recognize. anchor formats build familiarity; wildcards earn a second look. too many anchors and it gets dull, too many wildcards and nothing feels like a brand.

**open-ended ideas**

- a library of small decorative elements (shapes, textures, doodles, hand-drawn marks) that can be recombined so pieces feel handmade but stay on-brand.
- motion as a light layer: subtle loops derived from static pieces, so a still can be reused in a livelier form.
- a mood-based palette shift that stays inside the brand's rules, for variety without drifting off-system.
- pull quotes from customers as graphic material, treating real words typographically.
- behind-the-scenes pieces: process shots, rough sketches, material samples. people like seeing how things get made.
- data as graphic: simple charts and diagrams that make dry information friendly.
- a starter pack of editable files the client's team could use for tiny changes, so small edits don't need to route through me. might reduce friction; might also dilute uniformity. worth thinking about, not settled.
- alt text suggestions delivered alongside graphics, as a quiet accessibility habit.
- a swipe file of work i admire, annotated with why it works rather than just that it does.
- a recurring "wildcard" format reserved for experiments, so risk-taking has a home and doesn't leak into everything.

**things to keep in mind**

- the audience is not me. my taste is a tool, not a target.
- steady work can slide into autopilot. protect some room for play, even if it never ships.
- notice when a piece is portfolio-worthy, and remember that sharing it publicly is a permission question, not an assumption.
- responsiveness is a feature, but constant availability is a trap. a calm pace produces better work than a jumpy one.
- if the client ever shares how posts performed, write down which formats landed and which fell flat. results turn taste arguments into evidence.
- assets arriving late (photos, logos, copy) are the most common source of stalls. a lighter way to collect them might save more effort than any design trick.
- keep source files tidy and backed up in more than a single place, even when it feels like overkill.

**open questions**

- how much of the system should the client own versus me?
- what's the least intrusive way to ask how things are working?
- how do i keep a long-running visual language from calcifying?
- where does a refresh become necessary, and who notices the need first?
- is there a way to make the style sheet something the client actually reads, rather than something that sits in a folder?
- what would a quiet, low-effort check-in look like, one that surfaces problems before they become frustrations?

**small habits worth noticing**

keep a scrap file of half-ideas from finished work, since a rejected direction for one piece is often the right direction for another. leave a short note on the last file of a batch about what i'd try next, so picking the thread back up doesn't start cold. step away from a layout before judging it; fresh eyes catch spacing problems that tired eyes forgive. and when something works unusually well, take a moment to figure out why, so it can be repeated on purpose rather than by luck.

**things to keep in mind**

- clients rarely know what they want, but they almost always know what they do not want. use that. showing a range of directions early, including a couple of deliberately wrong ones, is a cheap way to find the edges of their taste.
- feedback is data about a feeling, not an instruction about a solution. when a reaction comes in as "make the logo bigger," the real message is often "i do not feel seen." dig for the feeling.
- keep files tidy as if a stranger will open them. layers named, groups sensible, colors stored as swatches rather than scattered values. the stranger is usually me, after a long break.
- naming matters. a consistent naming habit saves more grief than any clever shortcut. say what the thing is, say what state it is in, and keep the pattern the same everywhere.
- accessibility is not a layer on top. contrast, legibility at small sizes, and sensible reading order are part of the foundation. check them while building, not afterward.
- keep source material and exports separate. nothing is worse than discovering the only editable copy got overwritten by a flattened one.
- back up in more than a single place. dull advice, still true.
- be careful with borrowed imagery and borrowed fonts. licensing is boring right up until it isn't. keep a running note of where assets came from.
- watch for scope creep dressed up as a small favor. small favors compound. a polite, clear boundary protects the relationship better than quiet resentment.
- write handoff notes in plain language. what was made, how it is meant to be used, what should never be done to it (stretched, recolored, crowded). people follow instructions that are short and kind.

**working alone**

being a one-person studio means every role lives in my head: designer, producer, accountant, critic, cheerleader. the roles argue. the critic is useful but needs a leash. the producer is useful but can flatten the fun out of a project. it helps to notice which hat i am wearing and say it out loud, even if only to the empty room.

isolation can quietly narrow the work. to counter it, i try to keep a steady diet of things that have nothing to do with design: old signage, packaging from other countries, hand-painted shop lettering, textbook diagrams, the way a handwritten label peels at the corner. none of it is directly useful, and that is the point. ideas show up when the inputs are weird.

rest is part of the process, not a reward after it. tired eyes make muddy decisions. a walk, a swim, a long stare out a window: all of it counts as work if the mind is left free to rearrange things.

**open-ended ideas**

- build a personal library of small, reusable pieces: a handful of grid setups, some well-tested type pairings, a few color relationships that always behave. not templates exactly, more like warm-up exercises that start a project from a stronger place.
- keep a visual glossary of words clients use and what those words have turned out to mean in practice. "clean," "bold," "friendly," and "premium" each seem to mean different things to different people, and a map of those translations could speed up early conversations.
- try a practice of annotating finished work with what went wrong along the way. not as self-criticism, just as a record. patterns would likely show up, like the recurring places where i overcomplicate or under-explain.
- explore a set of experiments with no client at all: posters about everyday objects, imaginary brands for imaginary shops, alphabets built from a single repeated shape. low stakes, high curiosity. some of it might feed back into paid work in unexpected ways.
- think about a plain-spoken process document that could be shared with new clients, explaining how the work unfolds and where their input matters most. it might head off confusion before it starts.
- consider how motion, sound, or print finishing might extend static pieces. a graphic can have a second life if it is designed with that in mind from the start.
- try designing with the weakest screen and the cheapest printer in mind, then enriching from there. a lot of work gets judged in lousy conditions.
- look for ways to make the review stage more of a conversation and less of a verdict. maybe short recorded walkthroughs, maybe annotated boards, maybe simply asking better questions.
- keep a small list of questions that open up a brief: what should this make someone do, what should it never feel like, what would be embarrassing if it showed up in the wrong place?

**loose reminders**

good work usually comes from clarity, patience, and a willingness to throw away the clever thing. keep the margins generous. keep the language human. keep asking what a piece is for. and when something feels almost right but not quite, trust that feeling and keep nudging until it settles.

Working notes, loose and unordered

**Approach**

Most of the work is listening before drawing. The brief a client hands over is rarely the real problem; it's the version they could put into words. So I try to ask what would make the project feel like it worked, and then ask what would make it feel like a waste. The second answer is usually more honest and more useful.

I like to start rough and ugly. Marker on paper, boxes and arrows, words where images should go. Polish too early makes everyone defend the surface instead of questioning the structure. If a layout survives being sketched badly, it's probably sound.

Keep the thinking visible. Clients trust work more when they can see how it got there, even if the path was messy. A short note about why something looks the way it does saves a lot of back and forth later.

**Process**

- Gather references, but sort them by what they teach rather than what they look like. A reference is only useful if I can say what it's doing and why it works.
- Write the problem in plain language before touching any design software. If the problem statement is mushy, the design will be too.
- Build the smallest version that could be tested by a real person, then react to what confuses them. Confusion is data. Compliments mostly aren't.
- Leave gaps between phases. Work that sits untouched for a while looks different when I come back, and the flaws show up on their own.
- Keep a running list of "things I'm unsure about" separate from the to-do list. Uncertainty deserves its own space so it doesn't get quietly buried under tasks.
- Save versions with honest labels. "Final" is a lie I've told myself too often; better to describe what's actually different about each file.

**Things to keep in mind**

Working alone means there's nobody to catch drift. A project can slide away from its goal for a long stretch before I notice. A habit that helps: periodically reread the original brief with fresh eyes and ask whether the current work would still make sense to the person who wrote it.

Scope creep is rarely dramatic. It arrives as small, friendly requests that each seem reasonable. The trick is noticing the pattern, not any individual request. Worth keeping a gentle vocabulary for it: "that sounds like a new piece of work, let's look at it separately."

Clients often communicate in feelings: "it needs more energy," "it feels cold." Translating those into concrete moves is half the craft. Energy might mean tighter spacing, stronger contrast, a sharper typographic voice, or just a more confident crop. Ask what they were looking at when they felt it.

Pay attention to the person who isn't in the room. Usually there's a decision-maker behind the contact I talk to, and the work has to make sense to them without me narrating it. A design that needs an explanation to land is a design that hasn't finished.

Constraints are friends. A limited palette, a rigid grid, a fixed set of typefaces. They reduce the decision load and make the work coherent. When a project feels shapeless, it's often because the constraints were never set.

Don't confuse taste with judgment. Taste is what I like. Judgment is what serves the people using the thing. They overlap, but not as much as I'd like to believe.

Rest matters more than it feels like it should. Some of the best fixes arrive during a walk, in the shower, or while washing dishes. Staring harder at the screen rarely helps.

**Communication habits**

- Say what I'm doing and why, in plain words, before sharing anything visual.
- When sending work for review, frame the kind of feedback I need: structure, tone, or detail. Otherwise people comment on whatever is nearest, which is usually the color.
- Write down what was said in a conversation soon after it ends, while the nuance is still there. Memory edits itself.
- Bad news travels better when it comes early and comes with options. Silence makes small problems look like big ones.
- Be careful with jargon. A word that feels precise to me can feel like a wall to someone else.
- Thank people for good feedback specifically, not generically. It encourages more of it.

**Quality checks**

Before anything leaves the studio, a few passes with different eyes:

- Squint test: blur the layout and see whether the hierarchy still reads.
- Grayscale test: remove color and check that meaning still holds.
- Small-screen test: does it survive the least flattering viewing conditions?
- Read-aloud test: copy that sounds clumsy out loud is clumsy in the head too.
- Stranger test: could someone with no context tell what this is for?
- Print or export check: the file that looks right on my screen is not the file that looks right in the world.

**Open-ended ideas**

- A tiny library of "why this works" notes, written in my own words, drawn from work I admire. Not a mood board, more like annotated margins.
- A reusable intake questionnaire that feels like a conversation rather than a form. Questions about what the client is tired of, what they're proud of, what they're afraid people will think.
- A habit of ending each project with a short reflection: what surprised me, what I'd do differently, what I avoided because it was uncomfortable.
- Experiment with giving clients a "rejected ideas" page, showing roads not taken and why. It might build trust, or it might invite second-guessing. Worth trying carefully with someone relaxed.
- Think about how to make the studio's working process itself a kind of portfolio piece. People hire for how it feels to work with someone, not just for the finished artifacts.
- Explore collaborations with writers, photographers, or developers whose instincts differ from mine. Friction between disciplines tends to produce better ideas than agreement.
- Consider a slow personal project that has no client at all, just for the sake of keeping the muscles loose. The risk is that it becomes another obligation, so it should stay playful.
- Look for ways to document the studio's visual language so it stays consistent even when moods shift.

**Small reminders**

Names matter; spell them correctly. Files should be named so a stranger could find them. Backups are boring until they're everything. Defaults are decisions someone else made, so check them. A project that feels easy may be hiding a misunderstanding. A project that feels hard may just need a better question.

When stuck, change the medium. Draw it, write it, say it to an empty room, cut it out of paper. The problem often rearranges itself.

Keep curiosity pointed outward. The best work usually comes from caring about the client's world more than about my own portfolio.

And leave room for mistakes. They're part of the process, and the useful ones tend to point somewhere I wouldn't have gone on purpose.
