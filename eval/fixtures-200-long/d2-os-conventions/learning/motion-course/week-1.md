# Motion course: week 1

- Easing matters more than speed
- Nothing moves linearly in real life

general working notes

**approach**

- watch real things move before opening anything. a door settling after a push, a cat deciding to jump, a leaf nudged off a table by a draft. notice where the movement feels hesitant and where it feels committed. most of what sells a motion lives in those small hesitations
- describe the movement in plain words before building it. "it winds up, hangs for a breath, drops, settles." if the sentence is dull the animation will probably be dull too, and the sentence is much cheaper to rewrite
- work rough at the start. grey boxes, placeholder shapes, ugly on purpose. judge the feel before judging the look. a pretty thing moving badly is worse than a plain thing moving well
- let weight dictate behavior. heavy things resist starting and resist stopping. light things start eagerly and get pushed around by everything. if an object has no weight, viewers sense it even when they can't say why
- think about intent. what is this thing trying to do, and what is in its way? motion without a reason reads as decoration

**process**

the loop is basically: block it out, play it at full tempo, play it slowed down, adjust, repeat. slowed playback exposes the mechanical problems, like a pop, a stall, or a collision that doesn't land. full tempo tells you whether it feels right. they answer different questions, so don't trust either by itself.

step away from a piece for a while and then come back to it cold. the stuff that bothers you on return is usually the stuff that would bother a stranger. the stuff you've gotten used to is usually the stuff you've stopped seeing.

keep a scrap file of motion experiments that have nothing to do with a project. little bouncing, swinging, settling studies. they build a feel for the vocabulary the same way sketching builds a feel for form. none of it needs to be good, it needs to be frequent.

when something feels off and the cause isn't obvious, check the boring stuff before the clever stuff:

- is the start of the movement abrupt?
- is the end of the movement abrupt?
- is anything arriving at the same moment as something else when it shouldn't?
- is there a pause that's too tidy, a hold that feels like a freeze instead of a breath?
- is the scale of the movement right for the size of the object?

usually it's something in that list. rarely is it the fancy thing I suspected.

**things to keep in mind**

- motion is communication before it's style. it tells people what changed, what matters, where to look, and what they can touch. if a movement doesn't say anything, it's noise
- restraint reads as confidence. a small, well-judged movement beats a big, busy one almost every time
- repetition changes the math. something charming on a first viewing can be exhausting when it plays constantly. think about who sees it over and over and how they'll feel by then
- context eats details. a subtle shift in feel that looks great on a big monitor may vanish on a small screen, and the reverse can happen too. check at the size it will actually live at
- not everyone enjoys movement. some people find it disorienting or sickening. always have a calm version in mind, and treat that as part of the design instead of an afterthought
- consistency matters across a whole system. if some elements glide and others snap, it feels like different people made them. a shared personality for motion is worth defining early, even loosely
- feel is subjective but not random. people agree more than you'd expect about what feels natural, which is why observing real behavior pays off
- sound changes how motion is perceived. a soft thud can make a soft landing feel heavier, silence can make a fast movement feel sharper. worth remembering even when no sound is planned
- don't confuse polish with progress. an hour spent perfecting a transition that sits on the wrong idea is an hour wasted

**open-ended ideas**

- build a personal library of "characters" for motion: nervous, lazy, precise, playful, stubborn. then apply each to the same simple shape and see how the same path becomes a different story. could be a useful way to talk with clients about tone, since people often can't describe what they want but can point at what they like
- try animating with a physical constraint in mind, like pretending the object is attached to a spring, a rope, or a rubber band, and see what the constraint forces you to discover
- record a quick voice memo of me making noises that match a movement, whooshes and thumps and sighs, and use it as a timing guide. the mouth is surprisingly good at rhythm
- study how movement is handled in places where it has to be understood instantly, like signage, vehicle indicators, or safety demonstrations. what do they strip away?
- look at motion that fails on purpose. stiff puppets, glitchy video, awkward stop-motion. what exactly makes it charming instead of broken? maybe there's a usable idea about imperfection in there
- explore whether a studio identity could be carried mostly by how things move rather than how they look. a logo that's forgettable standing still but memorable in motion
- sketch the path of a movement on paper as a trail, a curve with dots showing spacing, before animating. the spacing of those dots is the whole story, and seeing it drawn might make it easier to edit by eye
- what would a motion style based on friction look like? things that drag, catch, release. most digital movement feels frictionless, which might be why it often feels slippery
- think about anticipation and aftermath as separate design problems. the build-up before a movement and the settling after it carry plenty of the emotion, and they're easy to skip
- could there be a way to teach clients to read motion? a short vocabulary they can use in feedback instead of "make it pop" or "make it smoother"

**loose questions**

- how much of what feels natural is learned from growing up around screens versus learned from the physical world?
- where's the line between expressive and distracting, and does it move depending on the mood of the viewer?
- is there a good habit for knowing when a piece is finished, or is that always going to be a gut call?
- what's the smallest possible movement that still communicates something?

random reminder to self: the best reference is usually sitting a few steps away, in the room or out the window. look there before looking anywhere else.
