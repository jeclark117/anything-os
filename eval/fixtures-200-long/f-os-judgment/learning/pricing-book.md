# Pricing book

general working notes

approach

the work is mostly about relief. a muddled brand, a site that loses people partway through, a deck that goes flat in the room. people want that feeling lifted and something calmer in its place. the files are just the delivery vehicle. when a conversation drifts toward file formats and revision rounds, it has usually drifted to the wrong altitude.

a brief usually hides a different brief underneath. the stated ask is frequently a symptom. so the early conversation is mostly hearing what keeps them up, what they've already tried, what embarrassed them in front of their own customers. the more specific the pain, the easier it gets to describe what a fix is worth to them.

keep an eye on the story i tell myself about my own worth. undervaluing shows up quietly, as apologetic phrasing and unasked-for favors and a habit of explaining too much.

things worth asking early:
- what does success look like for the people who will see this work, not just the person paying for it
- what happens if nothing changes
- who else gets a say, and when do they usually show up
- what have they seen elsewhere that they loved, and what made them wince
- what would make this a quiet win versus a loud win

process

start wide, then narrow on purpose. early sketches should be cheap and a bit embarrassing. if every early idea looks polished, nobody can tell which ideas are good, and the polish makes people attached to the wrong things.

show rough thinking in a way that invites argument. a rough direction with a clear rationale gets better feedback than a finished-looking comp with no story. people react to what they can see, so make what they see honest about its stage.

keep a running trail of why. every choice about type, color, spacing, structure should have a sentence behind it, even a short sentence. when feedback arrives sideways ("it just feels off"), the trail makes it possible to separate taste from a real problem.

sort feedback by kind:
- reactions about feeling
- reactions about function
- reactions about politics inside their organization
- reactions that are really about something unrelated to the work

each deserves a different kind of answer. mixing them up is how projects get stuck.

protect the middle of a project. the beginning is exciting and the end has momentum, but the middle is where scope quietly grows. a small request that sounds harmless can carry a whole new problem inside it. name the shape of the work out loud when it changes, kindly and early, rather than absorbing it silently and resenting it afterward.

the end of a project deserves as much design as the start. a tidy handover, clear files, a short guide for using what was made, and a real moment of celebration. people remember endings, and they tell stories about them to the next person who needs a designer.

things to keep in mind

- being small is an advantage in conversation and a risk in logistics. nobody is covering when something goes wrong, so the way of working has to be forgiving of bad days
- a calm voice sells more than a clever pitch. confidence in how a proposal is worded carries into how it's received
- people compare to what they know. if all they've seen is timesheet-style billing, a different structure needs a little extra explanation, delivered without defensiveness
- the options laid out in a proposal are also a conversation about what matters. watch which option they linger on, because it says more than what they claim to want
- scope language matters as much as the creative. be specific about what is included and what sits outside, in plain words, so nobody has to interpret later
- the cheapest-feeling option is rarely the path that gets the best work out of me. leave room for the version of the project i'd be proud of
- say no to poor-fit work with some warmth, and maybe a pointer elsewhere. a graceful no travels further than a reluctant yes
- rest is part of the process. tired work looks tired

how the structure of an offer can feel

an offer that feels like a menu invites comparison shopping. an offer that feels like a path invites a conversation about where they're headed. there's a difference between "here are the parts" and "here's the journey, with places to step off along the way."

wondering about bundling versus unbundling. bundled offers feel simpler to say yes to but hide what's valuable. unbundled offers show the value but invite haggling over parts. maybe the answer depends on how nervous the buyer is.

language that helps: results, clarity, confidence, momentum, readiness. language that hurts: anything that sounds like a timesheet.

there's also a difference between a fee and a price. a fee feels like a charge for effort, a price feels like a statement of worth. small wording shift, big emotional shift.

open-ended ideas

- a small library of before-and-after stories, each told from the customer's side of the table, to make the value of results tangible without leaning on jargon
- a plain-language glossary that explains what each kind of deliverable actually does for a business, so the conversation stays about purpose
- a pre-mortem exercise at the start of a project: imagine it went badly, then ask why
- a gentle check-in ritual at the midpoint where both sides say what's working and what's quietly worrying them
- a way to record what a project taught me, whether or not it went well, in a few honest sentences while it's still fresh
- trial conversations with friendly strangers: describe the studio's offer out loud and watch their face
- a visual way to show risk and reward in a proposal, maybe a simple diagram instead of paragraphs
- a short note to self before every proposal: who is this person, what are they afraid of, what would make them exhale
- an archive of proposals that landed well and proposals that didn't, annotated with guesses about why
- treating the proposal itself as a design object. it is, after all, the earliest piece of work they see from the studio

questions that stay open

how much explanation is too much when presenting a choice? at what point does helpful clarity turn into pressure?

what is the right amount of room to leave for surprises? some work reveals itself only partway in, and the structure has to flex without feeling like a bait and switch.

how do you talk about value with people who've been burned by vague creative work before? probably with proof, patience, and specifics, but the balance isn't obvious.

is there a way to make generosity part of the offer without it hardening into an expectation?

what does a healthy relationship with a returning customer look like, and how does the offer change when trust is already there?

when in doubt, make the work easier to understand and the reasoning easier to follow. clarity is persuasive, and it's kind.

- Price the outcome, not the hours
- Anchor with the biggest option first
