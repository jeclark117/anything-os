# Phone plan

- Business line $25/mo

Loose working notes, mostly for me. Being solo means nobody catches sloppy habits, so the habits have to live inside the way the work moves. None of this is a rule. It's a pile of tendencies that have held up, plus questions I keep circling back to.

**Approach**

Start from what the work is supposed to do for the person looking at it. Pretty is a side effect of clear. When a project feels stuck, it's usually because the purpose got fuzzy, not because the layout is wrong.

Listen more than feels natural in early conversations. People tend to mention the real constraint almost as an aside, right after describing the thing they think they want. Write down the aside.

Prefer rough, honest drafts over polished guesses. A sketch that gets reacted to teaches more than a gorgeous mockup that gets politely approved and quietly disliked.

Keep the vocabulary of a project narrow: a tight set of shapes, a restrained palette, a limited range of type choices. Constraint does most of the creative heavy lifting, and it makes the result feel intentional instead of decorated.

Treat references as ingredients, not recipes. Collect widely, then ask what exactly is appealing about each piece. Is it the spacing, the tone, the confidence? Borrow the quality, not the surface.

**Process**

- Begin with a messy dump of everything: client words, images, annoyances, constraints, half-formed hunches. Don't judge yet.
- Sort the dump into themes. The themes become the spine of the work, and anything that doesn't connect to a theme is a candidate for the bin.
- Explore wide and ugly before narrowing. The early ideas are rarely the ones that survive, but they clear the pipes.
- Step away from the screen. Walks, dishes, staring out a window. Fresh eyes catch what staring can't.
- Present work with a short story about why, not just the artifact. People respond better to reasoning they can follow.
- When feedback arrives, separate the reaction from the proposed fix. "Make it bigger" often means "I can't find the thing I care about." Solve the reaction, not the instruction.
- Finish properly: tidy files, sensible names, a handoff note written as if for a stranger. Future me counts as a stranger.

**Things to keep in mind**

- Admin is part of the work, not a tax on it. Contracts, invoices, backups, accounts, and recurring costs deserve a look now and then so they don't ambush the creative hours. Recurring costs in particular drift upward quietly. It's worth asking whether each still earns its place.
- Being reachable is not the same as being available. Tools that make contact easy also make interruption easy, so decide what deserves an instant reply and what can wait.
- Scope creep rarely arrives as a big ask. It arrives as small extras that each seem reasonable. Stating plainly at the start what's included makes later conversations kinder.
- Protect energy. The best thinking happens when rested, so put hard problems where the mind is sharpest and routine production where it's duller.
- Be clear about usage and credit early. Awkward conversations about rights are much easier before the work exists than after it's out in the world.
- Say what you don't know. Admitting a gap builds more trust than bluffing through it, and it usually leads to a better question.
- Avoid hiding behind jargon. If an idea can't be explained in plain words, it probably isn't finished.
- Saying no politely is a skill, and it's part of keeping the studio healthy. A project that's a poor fit costs more than it pays, even when it looks good on paper.
- Keep a version history that makes sense at a glance. Names like "final" and "final-really" are a tell that something upstream is broken.
- Rest isn't a reward for finishing. It's an input.

**Working alone: small observations**

Solitude is good for depth and bad for perspective. The fix isn't to avoid solitude but to build in outside views on purpose: a peer who'll be honest, a stranger who'll look at the work cold, a printed copy on the wall that looks different from the screen version.

Momentum matters more than mood. Starting something small and ugly beats waiting to feel inspired. Inspiration tends to show up after the hands are already moving.

Notes are only useful if they can be found again. A habit of writing things in the same place, in plain language, beats an elaborate system that needs maintenance. The system should be boring enough to survive a bad week.

Expect the middle of every project to feel bad. There's a stretch where the early excitement has faded and the finish line isn't visible, and the work looks worse than it is. Knowing that ahead of time makes it easier to push through without redesigning everything out of panic.

**Open-ended ideas**

- A small library of reusable starting points, written as principles instead of files: spacing relationships, type pairings, colour moods, rhythm. Something to think with, not something to paste in.
- A short, blameless "what went sideways" log after each project. Not for guilt, just so the same mistake doesn't keep wearing new clothes.
- A visible process page that shows clients how decisions get made, which might cut down on mystery and on surprise.
- More hands-on experiments before going digital: paper, ink, scissors, tape, photocopies of photocopies. Mess that can't be undone does something to the thinking.
- Self-initiated projects with no client and no brief, purely to stretch. They might feed back into paid work in ways that are hard to predict.
- A loose circle of peers who trade critique, so the studio feels bigger than a person without actually becoming bigger.
- Writing more publicly about the work. Explaining a decision sharpens it, and the half-embarrassing drafts are often the useful ones.
- What would a deliberately slow studio look like? Fewer projects, deeper attention, longer gaps between. Is that sustainable, or just a nice story?
- Is there a way to make the early listening stage more structured without making it feel like an interrogation?
- What would it look like to design the studio's own routines with the same care given to client work?

**Questions that don't have answers yet**

How much of the process should clients see? Too little feels opaque, too much feels like homework for them.

What does "enough" look like? Enough work, enough polish, enough income, enough visibility. Not having a picture of it makes every goal feel like a moving target.

Where is the line between a distinctive style and a rut? A recognisable hand is useful until it starts dictating the answers before the questions are asked.

How do you keep curiosity alive when the work becomes familiar? Maybe by deliberately picking a skill that's a little out of reach and letting it be clumsy for a while.

**Loose closing thought**

The best work seems to come from a mix of rigour and play. Rigour keeps it useful, play keeps it alive. When one side is missing, it shows. These notes are mostly an attempt to remember that balance when the schedule gets loud and the easy thing is to rush.
