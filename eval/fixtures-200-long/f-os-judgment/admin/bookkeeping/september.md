# Books: september

general working notes

approach

start with the problem, not the look. projects go sideways mostly because the real question never got stated plainly. before touching any layout, write down what the thing is supposed to help a person do. if that statement is mushy, the visuals will be mushy too.

working solo has a real upside: no handoffs. what's in my head is what lands on the screen. the downside is that nobody pushes back, so i have to build my own friction. step away, come back cold, and ask what a stranger would notice before anything else. if the answer isn't the thing that matters, the hierarchy is off.

keep early work cheap and ugly. rough sketches, boxes and arrows, scrappy type. the moment something looks polished, people start defending it instead of questioning it, me included. polish should be a reward for surviving questions, not a starting point.

i like to treat every project as a conversation with a slightly skeptical audience. what would they push on? where would they get lost? what would they misread? imagining that audience out loud, even talking to an empty room, surfaces weak spots faster than staring at the screen does.

process

- gather raw material up front: existing assets, past attempts, whatever the client already loves or hates. the dislikes are usually more informative than the likes
- collect references widely, then sort them by what they actually do rather than how they look. a reference that solves a similar problem in a totally different field is worth more than a pretty one
- explore wide before narrowing. aim for directions that are genuinely unlike each other, not variations on a theme wearing different colors
- test in context early. a logo on a blank canvas lies. put it on a cramped small screen, a dark background, a tiny browser tab icon, a crumpled printout
- expect an awkward middle stretch where nothing feels right and it's tempting to abandon the direction. usually that's just where the details need work, not the idea
- keep a running log of why things look the way they do. future me won't remember, and clients will ask

the rough shape of a project usually goes: absorb everything, sketch badly, narrow gradually, refine obsessively, then explain clearly. the explaining part gets underrated. work that can't be explained in plain words is hard for anybody else to champion, and a design nobody champions rarely survives contact with a larger organization.

things to keep in mind

- clarity beats cleverness nearly every time. a clever idea that needs explaining has already lost some of its charm
- constraints are friends. a tight palette, a limited type system, a strict grid. total freedom is where work goes generic
- white space is a material, not leftovers. treat it as something placed on purpose
- accessibility isn't a layer for the end. contrast, legibility, motion sensitivity, and tap targets shape the structure from the start
- type does most of the heavy lifting. get the typography right and the rest forgives a lot
- feedback is data about a reaction, not an instruction. when a client says "make it pop," the real problem is somewhere underneath. ask what they felt when they looked at it
- be wary of the loudest opinion in the room. it's rarely the opinion of the person who'll actually use the thing
- people skim. design for the skimmer, reward the reader
- defaults are choices that somebody else made for you, so question them
- a good brief is a large share of the work already finished

working with clients

it helps to write up what was heard after any conversation, in plain words, and send it back so misunderstandings surface early rather than at the reveal. people are usually relieved to see their own vague wishes turned into something concrete, even when they end up revising it.

show work with a story attached. not a sales pitch, just the reasoning: here's what we were trying to solve, here's what this does about it, here's what it trades away. every design trades something away, and naming the trade keeps the conversation straight.

separate taste from function in feedback. taste is negotiable and personal; function is about whether the thing does its job. they matter, but they deserve different kinds of discussion.

when feedback contradicts itself, that's not a failure on anybody's part. it usually means the underlying goal isn't shared yet. go back to the goal statement from the start of the project and see whether it still holds.

keeping a solo studio sustainable

- admin eats creative energy when it piles up. small regular tidying beats big rescue sessions
- protect deep-work time ferociously. interruptions fragment focus in ways that are hard to recover from
- rest is part of the process. the best ideas show up while walking, washing dishes, or staring at a wall
- keep a side project with no client. it's where new techniques get tried with nobody's expectations attached
- saying no to a poor fit is a skill. the wrong project costs more than the empty space it would have filled
- stay curious about things unrelated to design: cooking, geology, bad sci-fi, how bridges are built. unrelated input is where fresh connections come from
- keep a shelf of physical objects that are well made. a good hinge, a nicely printed box, a well-balanced knife. they recalibrate taste

open-ended ideas

- what would a studio look like where every project ended with a small public write-up of what went wrong and why? could be useful, could be terrifying
- a personal library of tiny interaction studies (buttons, toggles, transitions) kept as thinking aids rather than reusable code
- design in reverse: start from the final moment of the user's experience and work backward to the entry point
- a reusable set of opening questions for any project, refined over time, kept short enough to actually use
- make work with deliberately limited tools: a restricted set of shapes, a palette of black plus a spot color, a typeface chosen blind
- sound as identity: what does a brand sound like when nobody can see it?
- design for the return visit, not just the arrival. what does the thing feel like after the novelty wears off?
- what if a deliverable included a guide to its own undoing, so clients can evolve it without breaking it?
- study how old signage ages: patina, fading, repairs. design things to wear well rather than to stay pristine
- write the microcopy before any layout, then build the design around the words
- cheap print experiments: photocopier textures, misregistered color, rubbed-off ink. bring that looseness back into digital work
- invite a writer, illustrator, or photographer into a speculative exercise just to see what collides
- an archive of rejected directions, revisited later. some of them were just early
- explore how a visual system could degrade gracefully when pieces are missing, like a brand that still works with the logo removed

loose thoughts

taste is built by looking at lots of things critically, not just admiring them. when something works, try to name why. when something annoys, name that too. a vocabulary for reactions turns instinct into something that can be taught, repeated, and defended.

there's also value in leaving some work slightly unresolved. a finished feeling isn't always the goal; sometimes a quiet gap leaves room for whoever lives with the design to make it their own.

and a reminder to self: most of this is easy to say and hard to practice. the useful part isn't having the principles, it's noticing in the moment when i'm drifting from them.
