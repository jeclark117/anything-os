# Quarterly taxes 2026

general working notes

**approach**

Start with the problem as the person would say it out loud, not as the brief words it. Briefs get polished until the interesting part is gone. Ask what they're afraid of getting wrong. That answer shapes the work more than the stated goal does.

Paper before software, nearly always. Bad drawings are fine. The point is to clear out the obvious ideas so the odd ones have room. If every sketch looks like a cousin of the others, push sideways: change the medium, flip a constraint, remove something that seems essential and see what's left.

Keep a loose pile of references, but sort it by feeling rather than by style or industry. A folder called "makes me lean in" beats a folder called "inspiration" every time.

Good design is often a string of small refusals. No extra flourish, no extra colour, no extra explanation. When stuck, look for what can come out rather than what can go in.

**process, roughly**

- listen more than feels natural at the start. People say the real thing near the end of a conversation, usually while packing up.
- write the problem in plain sentences before drawing anything. If it can't be said plainly, it isn't understood yet.
- make the ugly version early. An ugly version invites honest reactions, a polished one invites politeness.
- show work in context whenever possible. A mark floating on white tells nobody anything. The same mark on a door, a receipt, a cracked phone screen, a shipping box tells a lot.
- keep the options narrow enough that choosing is a real choice. A wide spread just hands the decision back to the person who hired a designer to make it easier.
- leave breathing room between rounds so the work can be seen with fresh eyes. Stepping away is part of the job, not a break from it.
- name files like a stranger will have to find them. Future me is a stranger.

**things to keep in mind**

Taste is a habit, not a gift. It gets built by looking at a lot of things and being honest about why some of them work. Worth keeping a running note of small reactions: "this spacing feels generous," "this typeface sounds like it's clearing its throat." Those fragments add up to a point of view.

Clients rarely want what they ask for in the literal sense. They want to feel confident, to look credible to their own customers, to stop worrying about the thing. Design is the vehicle, not the destination. When a request seems oddly specific (make the logo bigger, change the green), it's usually carrying a feeling that hasn't been named. Try naming it back to them gently.

Feedback comes in layers. The surface layer is "I don't like it." Underneath is "it doesn't feel like us," and under that is often "I'm not sure who we are yet." Working at the right layer saves a lot of circling. Sometimes the useful move is to stop revising and have a different conversation entirely.

Constraints are friends. A tight budget of attention, space, colour, or patience tends to produce clearer work than open freedom. When a project has no constraints, invent some and treat them as real.

Working alone has a particular failure mode: nobody pushes back, so weak ideas survive too long. Counter it by explaining the work out loud to an empty room, or to a patient friend who doesn't care about design. If the explanation falls apart, the work probably does too.

The reverse failure also exists. Working alone means every opinion is mine, and that can curdle into rigidity. Worth asking regularly whether a position is a principle or just a habit that got comfortable.

Protect the boring parts of the studio. Files, backups, templates, a tidy way of handing work over. They never feel urgent and they always matter eventually. A calm handoff is part of the design, because it's the last thing the client experiences and often what they remember.

Rest is input. Some of the best solutions arrive while walking, washing dishes, or staring out a window. A studio that never goes quiet stops producing surprises.

**on communicating**

- say what the work is trying to do before showing it. Framing changes how it's received.
- be specific when praising and when disagreeing. "The rhythm of the headline works" teaches more than "nice."
- write short emails. Put the one thing that needs an answer where it can't be missed. (avoid "one")

Fix: "Put the thing that needs an answer where it can't be missed."

- when something goes wrong, say so plainly and bring a way forward in the same message. Silence makes small problems look like big ones.
- keep a tone that sounds like a person, not a brochure.

**open-ended ideas**

- a habit of tiny self-assigned briefs: redesign a ticket stub, a seed packet, a bus stop sign, purely to keep the eye sharp and to have something to try new ideas on without anybody else's stakes attached.
- a library of questions rather than answers. A deck of prompts to pull from when a project starts to feel flat: what would this look like if it were quieter? If it were older? If it had to work in the dark?
- a practice of writing a short retrospective after a project wraps, not to judge it but to catch what surprised me. Surprises are where learning hides.
- a shelf of physical samples: paper stocks, offcuts, fabric swatches, found packaging. Touching things changes how screens get designed.
- a way of recording the reasoning behind choices so that when someone asks why a colour was picked, there's an answer better than "it felt right."
- more collaboration with people outside design: writers, makers, a carpenter, a sound person. Their instincts rhyme with mine in unexpected places.
- experiment with making work that is deliberately incomplete, leaving gaps that the audience fills in. Interesting question: how little is enough?
- think about what a studio like this looks like when it's quiet and sustainable rather than busy and impressive. What would be left if the performance of being busy were stripped out?
- explore teaching, or at least writing publicly about the craft, as a way to sharpen my own thinking. Explaining forces honesty.

**loose thoughts to end on**

Most of the craft is attention. Noticing that a margin is slightly off, that a word is doing no work, that a person in the meeting went quiet. The tools change constantly and the attention is what carries over.

Be kind to the early versions of ideas. They're clumsy because they're new. Give them room, then edit hard.

And remember that the work exists for somebody who will meet it tired, distracted, and busy with their own life. If it can make that moment slightly easier or slightly more pleasant, it did its job.
