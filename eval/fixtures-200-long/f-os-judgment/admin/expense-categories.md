# Expense categories

working notes

things i keep coming back to when the work is going well, and things i forget when it isn't.

**approach**

Start with the problem, not the look. When a project begins with a mood board and nothing else, the work drifts. The better habit is to write down in plain sentences what the thing is supposed to do for the person who will use it, and only then start drawing. If the sentences are hard to write, that's useful information about the brief, not a reason to skip ahead.

A studio this size has an advantage in staying close to the work. There's no handoff where meaning gets lost between the idea and the result. The flip side is that nobody pushes back, so I have to build in my own friction: step away, come back with fresh eyes, and look at the work as if a stranger made it.

Keep the loop between idea and something visible as short as possible. A rough sketch on paper beats a perfect concept that lives in my head. Ugly early drafts are cheap to throw away, and that's the whole point of them.

Prefer constraints. A blank canvas is the hardest place to design from. Limiting the palette, the type, the grid, or the tone for a while often opens up more than it closes off.

Treat taste as something that needs feeding. Looking at good work isn't a distraction from the job. It's maintenance.

**process, loosely**

- listen carefully in the early conversations; they're mostly for hearing how a client describes their own problem, including the words they use without noticing
- gather references widely, then be ruthless about which ones actually say something
- sketch loosely before opening anything digital
- explore a wide spread of directions early, then narrow; resist polishing too soon
- show work in context whenever possible, because a mark or a layout on a blank white page lies about how it will feel in use
- write short rationale notes alongside the work so the reasoning survives after the details fade
- leave room at the end for a real review pass with a checklist, rather than trusting memory
- look at work on the actual surfaces and devices it'll live on, not just the screen it was made on

Feedback deserves its own thinking. When a client says they don't like something, the stated reason is rarely the real reason. Ask what they were hoping to feel, what they were worried about, what it reminded them of. Translate reactions into goals before translating them into changes. When feedback is vague, offering concrete alternatives lets them point instead of describe.

Presenting is part of the work. Walk through the thinking in the order the client will experience it, not the order it was made. Lead with the problem, show how the design answers it, and only then get into details like color and type. Address the obvious objections calmly up front rather than waiting to be cornered.

**things to keep in mind**

- scope creeps quietly; it helps to name it gently when a request is really a new project
- saying what isn't included is as important as saying what is
- file hygiene pays off: clear layer names, consistent naming, tidy folders, sensible versions. Future me is always a stranger to past me
- keep backups in separate places, and check once in a while that they actually open
- accessibility isn't an add-on; contrast, legibility, and clear hierarchy belong in from the beginning
- keep a running log of what worked and what didn't after a project wraps, even if it's just a few honest sentences
- tired eyes make confident bad choices, so rest counts as part of the process
- running the whole studio means being the designer, the writer, the bookkeeper, the support desk, and the tidy-up crew. Switching hats costs energy, so grouping similar tasks together helps
- warmth and clarity can coexist; being kind doesn't require being vague
- a good brief is a gift. A bad brief is an invitation to ask better questions
- don't confuse busy with productive
- people remember how a process felt long after they forget the specifics of the deliverable

**craft reminders**

Type does most of the heavy lifting. Spacing, rhythm, and alignment tend to matter more than whatever clever idea sits on top. White space is a material, not leftover room. If a layout feels off, the fix is often removing something rather than adding something.

Test at the extremes: very small and very large, high contrast and low, crisp and degraded. A design that only works in perfect conditions isn't finished.

Consistency builds trust, but too much of it turns into sameness. The interesting work usually lives in the tension between a clear system and a moment where it deliberately bends.

Edges and details are where care shows. Optical alignment, how a curve meets a line, how a button reads when pressed. Most people won't consciously notice, and they'll feel it anyway.

Print and screen are different animals. Color shifts, texture matters, scale changes the whole reading. Never assume one will translate cleanly into the other.

**open-ended ideas**

- build a small library of reusable prompts for discovery conversations, the kind that get people talking about what they actually want
- keep a visual diary of things noticed out in the world: signage, packaging, hand lettering, worn and patched objects, accidental beauty
- make self-initiated projects with no client, just to chase a question; keep them loose and let them fail
- explore how sound or motion could carry personality in a brand, not just still images
- write about process in plain language; explaining the work sharpens it
- draft a reflection template for the end of each project so the lessons don't evaporate
- imagine what a studio would look like if it deliberately worked slowly, with fewer projects and deeper attention
- collaborate occasionally with other independent makers, such as illustrators, writers, photographers, and developers, and see what a loose network could do that a solo practice can't
- revisit old work now and then to see how taste has shifted, and what stayed stubbornly the same
- try designing with only found materials or only a limited toolset as a creative exercise
- think about what a client would want to hand down to the next person who takes over their brand, and whether the studio could produce that kind of lasting guide
- consider how to make the invisible parts of the work, the thinking and the research, more visible to clients without overwhelming them

**questions worth sitting with**

How much of a personal style is habit, and how much is choice? What's the difference between a client's taste and their needs, and who gets to arbitrate when they diverge? When is a project truly finished, and how can I tell the difference between done and merely tired of it? What would it mean to make work that ages well instead of work that merely looks current?

And a looser one: what would the studio's work look like if it had no portfolio to impress, only a problem to solve?

**a note on pace**

Good work usually has a rhythm: a period of wandering, a period of narrowing, a period of finishing. Trying to rush the wandering produces thin ideas. Trying to extend it forever produces nothing. Learning to feel which phase I'm in is probably the most useful skill to keep sharpening, and it's one that no checklist can fully capture.
