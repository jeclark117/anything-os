# Rates

general working notes

**how i like to start**

- get the problem said out loud in plain words before opening any software. if i can't explain what the thing is for without leaning on design jargon, i don't understand it yet
- ask what the work is meant to change for the person looking at it. a logo, a site, a poster, packaging, it's almost always trying to make somebody feel something or do something
- gather whatever already exists: old materials, photos, scraps of copy, stuff the client can't stand. the stuff they can't stand is weirdly useful because it shows where the edges are
- write down what's off the table early so nobody falls in love with an idea that can't happen
- keep the brief short. if it takes pages to explain, the project probably has a fuzzy center, and that's worth pointing out gently

**process, roughly**

my process is messy and i've stopped apologizing for it. mostly it goes: soak in the material, sketch badly and quickly, step away, then come back and see which sketches still make sense with fresh eyes. the ideas that survive a break are usually the real directions. the ones i'm still defending after stepping away are usually ego.

paper before screen whenever possible. screens make everything look finished too early, and finished-looking things are hard to criticize honestly. rough marks keep the conversation about the idea instead of the kerning.

go wide, then narrow. push the range far enough that some options feel wrong on purpose, because otherwise the safe option wins by default and nobody knows why. then narrow with reasons i can say out loud.

name files and layers like a stranger will have to open them. that stranger is me, later, tired and confused.

**showing work**

- present fewer directions than feels natural. a big pile of options reads as indecision and hands the deciding back to people who came to me so they wouldn't have to
- always say why. "i chose this because..." beats "here's what i made" every time
- show work in context, on the actual thing, not floating on a white page
- leave room for the reaction i didn't expect. the offhand comment from the quiet person in the room is often the real brief
- separate "i don't like it" from "it doesn't work." feelings are valid input, they just need translating

**feedback**

vague feedback is a puzzle, not an insult. when someone says "make it pop," ask what they'd point at if they had to show me something that already pops. usually they produce a reference that looks nothing like what the words suggested. the reference is the real information. the adjective is just the best they could do on the spot.

it also helps to remember that people react to work the way they react to everything else in their day. a bad mood, a rushed glance, a cramped screen. if a reaction seems out of proportion, it might be about something other than the work. not always, but often enough to keep in mind before reworking something that was fine.

worth asking, when feedback is contradictory: what's the thing everyone agrees on underneath? contradictions usually sit on top of a shared worry.

loose thoughts on how the studio runs. nothing in here is final, it's just a place to think out loud.

**approach**

- listen more than I talk at the start of a project. the brief people hand over is rarely the real problem. there's usually a worry sitting underneath it, and the worry is what the work should answer.
- ask what a good outcome would feel like, not just what it would look like. people describe feelings more honestly than they describe specs.
- say the problem out loud in plain words before opening any design software. if I can't explain it plainly, I don't understand it yet.
- gather references widely, then toss the obvious ones. the obvious ones are what the whole field already collected, and they pull the work toward sameness.
- stay curious about the client's world. the best ideas tend to come from an odd detail about how their customers actually behave, not from whatever is trending.
- treat constraints as friends. a tight brief is easier to be inventive inside than a blank page.
- remember that taste is a tool, not a verdict. my preferences are useful for making choices, but they aren't the measure of whether something works for the people it's meant for.

**process**

being a solo studio means every stage runs through the same head. that's the advantage and the hazard. nothing gets lost in a handoff, but there's nobody across the table to say "that's not quite right" before it's too late.

**being a studio of me**

- there's nobody to bounce ideas off, so build substitutes. talk to the wall. write the rationale as if explaining to a skeptical friend. show rough work to a trusted outsider before it goes out
- every bit of admin, email, bookkeeping, and sulking is also me. protect actual design time because nothing else will
- "let me think about that" is a complete sentence. answers given on the spot in a call tend to be worse than considered ones
- scope creep arrives in friendly clothes. "while you're in there, could you just..." is how a small project quietly turns into a large one. notice it, name it kindly, and let people choose with open eyes
- keep a running file of lessons learned the hard way. cheaper than relearning them
- rest matters more than it looks. tired work has a particular dullness that i can usually spot afterward but almost never during

**taste and craft**

taste is mostly exposure plus honesty. look at lots of things, including bad ones, and ask why they're bad. keep a folder of stuff that stopped me in my tracks, organized by feeling rather than medium. a menu from a tiny diner and a museum identity can be teaching the same lesson about restraint.

craft is the unglamorous part: spacing, alignment, the choice between two nearly identical grays. nobody will praise it directly, but everybody feels it when it's missing. the details that nobody notices are the ones holding the whole thing up.

copy matters more than designers like to admit. a layout with lazy words is a nice frame around nothing. when possible, nudge the words toward being better, or at least ask what the headline is trying to say before choosing its typeface.

constraints are a gift. a tight palette, a stubborn format, a bad photo that has to be used. some of the best solutions come from not having the option to do the obvious thing.

**things to keep in mind**

- clarity first, cleverness second. clever is a seasoning
- the person using the thing doesn't care about my process. they care whether it works and whether it feels right
- consistency is a kindness. a system that behaves predictably lets people relax
- accessibility isn't a bonus round. contrast, size, and readable structure are part of the job, not decoration on top of it
- when stuck, change the medium. draw it, print it, cut it up, say it aloud, squint at it from across the room
- say no to the project that would make me worse at the work, even if it looks shiny
- credit the people whose ideas helped, even the small ones
- the best thing i can do for a rough idea is give it a bit of time before judging

**open-ended ideas**

- a small public journal of process, as a way to think out loud and let curious people see how i work. unsure whether it would be useful or just a distraction
- skill swaps with other independent creatives: writers, photographers, developers. a way to get fresh eyes without adding overhead
- a kickoff questionnaire that's short and slightly odd, built to surface what people actually care about instead of what they think they're supposed to say
- an "unfinished ideas" shelf for personal work with no client attached. what comes out when nobody's paying for it tends to feed everything else
- constraint experiments: limited palette, limited typefaces, limited everything. see what breaks and what gets better
- a short reflection written after each project, for myself, to catch patterns i miss in the moment
- maybe teaching or workshops someday. explaining things is a good way to find out which parts i only partly understand
- a reference library of before-and-after stories, even imaginary ones, to practice describing why a change mattered
- a habit of reviewing old work with a cold eye, to see what aged well and what only felt good at the time

**loose questions**

- how much of this work is craft and how much is persuasion? probably more persuasion than i'd like to admit
- what does a good working relationship feel like from the inside, and how early can i tell if it's heading there?
- where's the line between a distinctive style and a rut?
- is it better to be known for a specific thing or to stay flexible and risk being forgettable?
- what would the studio look like if it allowed itself to be a little more ambitious?
- how do i tell the difference between a project that's hard because it's good and one that's hard because it's wrong?

**small reminders**

the work is the point. everything else is scaffolding, and scaffolding should be taken down when it stops helping. be curious, be kind about other people's constraints, and leave things a little tidier than they were found. when it's going badly, go for a walk. when it's going well, notice why.

- rough before refined. paper, marker, bad thumbnails. the point is volume and speed, with judging saved for afterward. the temptation is to polish an idea before it has earned it.
- let ideas rest away from the screen for a while. they look different after a walk, a meal, a night of sleep. weak ones get weaker and strong ones get clearer.
- show work in stages that match the question being asked. early on, show direction and mood, not details. showing fine detail too soon invites feedback on the wrong layer, and then the whole conversation drifts to kerning when the real issue is tone.
- keep a running log of why choices were made. future me won't remember, and clients will ask "why this" at the least convenient moment.
- build in a pass that's purely about removing things. most work improves when something comes out.
- check work in the context where it will live, not on a blank canvas. a mark that looks great alone can vanish on a crowded shelf or a tiny screen.
- test with a squint, test at small size, test in grayscale, test upside down. if the idea survives all that, it's probably sturdy.
- keep files tidy as I go. naming and structure feel like a chore in the moment and a gift later.
- end each work session by leaving a note to myself about where I stopped and what I was thinking. picking up a cold project without that is miserable.

**things to keep in mind**

- feedback is information about a reaction, not an instruction. when a person says "make it pop," the useful part is what felt flat to them.
- being the only person here means being the bottleneck. rested attention produces good work, and tired attention produces work that is plausible but dull.
- scope drifts quietly. small additions tend to arrive wrapped in "while you're at it." they're easier to talk about kindly when noticed early, and awkward when noticed late.
- don't mistake busyness for progress. a crowded inbox feels like momentum and often isn't.
- be honest about what I'm not good at. there are areas where a collaborator would make the result better for the client too, and pretending otherwise helps nobody.
- write things down while they're fresh. partly remembered ideas turn into vague guilt instead of useful material.
- the portfolio isn't the same as the work. what looks good in a case study isn't always what solved the problem, and the reverse is true as well.
- the thinking is often the invisible part of a project. a clean final result can hide how much wrestling went into it, which is a good sign and a small frustration together.
- stay kind to the earlier versions of the work. the clumsy sketches are where the real discoveries tend to hide.
- clients are usually experts in something I'm not. their knowledge of their own field is a resource, and treating it that way makes the collaboration lighter.
- clarity beats cleverness nearly every time. a clever idea that needs explaining is often a clear idea that hasn't been found yet.

- a library of starter questions, grouped by type of project. maybe it turns out to be mostly the same questions wearing different clothes, which would be interesting in itself.
- experiments done purely for fun, with no client attached, as a way to keep the eye sharp. possibly share these publicly, possibly keep them private.
- studying work from outside design altogether: architecture, cooking, signage, packaging, old manuals, hand-painted shop fronts. all of those solve clarity problems in ways that feel fresh when borrowed carefully.
- a way to explain the process to newcomers with a picture instead of a paragraph. what would the diagram look like? probably not a straight line. more of a loop with detours and dead ends.

- trying a project with a deliberately severe constraint: a restricted palette, a restricted typeface, restricted shapes. see what creative pressure does to the ideas.
- exploring how to bring a client into the process so they feel like they're discovering alongside me instead of waiting for a reveal. reveals are dramatic, but they put an enormous amount of weight on a sudden moment.
- collecting a shelf of physical things: paper samples, printed scraps, odd labels, worn packaging. touch is underrated when most work happens behind glass.
- asking what the studio would look like if it stayed small on purpose, and what it would look like if it grew. not a question to answer now, just to hold onto and see how it feels over time.
- keeping an "unfinished" box for ideas that didn't fit any project. they might want a home more than a purpose.
- wondering about the role of boredom. some of the best connections seem to arrive during repetitive, low-stakes tasks, so maybe those tasks deserve a bit more respect than they get.
- sketching a map of the kinds of work that give energy versus the kinds that drain it, and seeing whether the pattern says something about where the studio is headed.

**a few loose reflections**

the work seems to go best when curiosity leads and anxiety follows at a distance. when it's the other way around, everything shrinks: ideas get safer, conversations get more defensive, and the end result looks like it was made by committee even though it wasn't.

there's also a quiet pleasure in small craft details that nobody will consciously notice: a well-judged margin, a color that sits a touch warmer than expected, a transition that feels inevitable. they probably won't be mentioned in any feedback, and they're a big part of why something feels considered rather than assembled.

lastly, a reminder that a one-person studio has a voice whether it wants one or not. the way I write emails, the way I frame a presentation, the way I handle a disagreement all become part of the product. worth paying attention to that voice, and worth letting it stay human.
