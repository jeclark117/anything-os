# Review: june

general working notes

**Approach**

Most of the good work comes from slowing down at the start. When a brief lands, the pull is to open the canvas right away and push shapes around, and that usually produces something that looks fine and says nothing. It's better to sit with the problem in plain words before touching anything visual. What is this for, who will be looking at it, what should they feel or do when they see it? If that can't be said in a sentence, the design isn't ready to begin.

Constraints are friends. A tight palette, a limited type system, a narrow set of shapes: these make decisions easier and the result more coherent. When a project feels loose and shapeless, the fix is usually to add a rule rather than more options.

Reference material is useful, but it's easy to drown in it. Collecting inspiration feels like progress and mostly isn't. A small pile of things that genuinely resonate beats a giant board of everything vaguely nice. It's worth asking why each piece is there. If the answer is "it looks cool", it probably doesn't belong.

Good design is mostly editing. The first instinct is rarely wrong, but it's usually overdressed. Taking things away until the idea can stand without help tends to be the move that separates decent work from memorable work.

**Process**

- Start rough. Paper, scribbles, crude blocks. Ugly early work is cheap to throw away, and that freedom matters.
- Go wide before deep. Explore lots of directions quickly, then pick what deserves polish.
- Step away from a layout before judging it. Fresh eyes catch weak hierarchy, awkward spacing, and clumsy alignment that were invisible while immersed.
- Squint test: blur the screen or stand back from it. If the hierarchy survives, it's working.
- Look at work in the context where it will live, not floating on a blank artboard. A mark on a shopfront behaves differently from the same mark on a slide.
- Keep files tidy as they go. Named layers, sensible grouping, consistent styles. Future-me is always a stranger who needs help.
- Write down the rationale while it's fresh. Explaining why a choice was made is far easier in the moment than reconstructing it afterward, and it makes presenting smoother.
- Test with real content whenever possible. Placeholder text flatters everything, and real words and real images expose problems fast.

**Working with clients**

Being a studio of just myself means the whole relationship runs through me, which is both a strength and a risk. The strength is that nothing gets lost in translation, and clients get a direct line to the person actually making the work. The risk is that nobody is around to sanity-check tone, scope, or energy. Small habits can stand in for a team: re-reading an email before sending it, or imagining how a stranger would read a proposal.

Feedback is data, not a verdict. When a client says "make it pop" or "it feels off", the useful move is to ask what they were picturing, or what the work reminded them of. Vague comments usually hide a real concern that's easier to solve once it has a name. The best feedback conversations happen when the client understands the reasoning, so showing the thinking alongside the visuals pays off.

Scope creep is rarely malicious. It grows out of enthusiasm. Clear language at the start about what's included, plus a calm way of pointing at the edge when it's approached, keeps goodwill intact. Healthy relationships make it easy to say "that sounds like a new piece of work" without friction.

Finishing well matters as much as the work itself. A tidy handover, organized files, a short guide to using the assets, and a genuine thank-you leave a lasting impression and often lead to referrals. Clients remember how the ending felt.

- Energy is the real resource. Hours can be tracked, but the quality of those hours varies enormously, and the hard thinking deserves the best of them.
- Protect long stretches of uninterrupted focus. Notifications off, messages parked. Design needs depth, and depth doesn't survive constant poking.
- Rest is part of the work. Walks and time away from screens solve problems that staring never will.
- Isolation creeps in quietly. Without colleagues it's easy to lose perspective, so stay in touch with other designers, share work in progress, and swap honest opinions.
- Admin is unglamorous but it keeps everything running: invoicing, records, contracts, backups. Systems that are boring and reliable beat clever ones.
- Keep a running list of what went well and what snagged. Patterns only show up once they're written down.
- The workspace matters more than it seems. Good light, a comfortable chair, a clear desk, and a screen that's calibrated properly all add up.

**Things to keep in mind**

- Learn the difference between good enough to ship and perfect. Perfect is often a hiding place.
- Taste develops by looking at a lot and making a lot. Neither works alone.
- Work that drains and doesn't teach anything is worth questioning, even when it pays the bills.
- Don't compare a messy middle to someone else's polished highlight reel.
- Momentum is a good moment to stay curious rather than coast. Success can make a person careless in subtle ways, like reusing old solutions because they worked before.
- Accessibility is part of quality, not an extra. Contrast, legibility, and clarity help everyone.
- Honest craft shows. People may not be able to name what's right about careful kerning or considered spacing, but they feel it.
- Not every problem is a design problem. Sometimes the real issue is the offer, the message, or the process behind the thing being designed, and saying so is part of the job.

**Open-ended ideas**

- A library of reusable starting points: grids, type pairings, spacing scales, color relationships. Projects could begin from a stronger base instead of a blank slate.
- Case studies framed around the problem and the thinking rather than pretty screenshots. What was hard, what was tried, what didn't work.
- A small self-initiated project with no client at all, purely for experimentation: posters, type specimens, generative patterns, odd little tools.
- A journal or newsletter about process, mostly as a way of clarifying thoughts rather than building an audience.
- A light diagnostic format where someone's existing brand gets a candid review, which could be useful for people not ready for a full engagement.
- A loose network of trusted illustrators, writers, and developers for projects that outgrow a solo studio.
- Revisiting old work purely to see what aged well and what didn't, and why.
- A talk or workshop on how a small studio approaches problems.
- A better way of capturing client conversations so insights don't vanish into the fog of memory.

**Questions worth sitting with**

- What kind of work is the most energizing, and how could more of it find its way in?
- What would a more sustainable rhythm look like, one that allows for deep work and real rest?
- Which parts of the process could be made lighter without losing quality?
- Where does the studio want to be known, and for what? Being broadly capable is different from being the obvious choice for something specific.
- What's being avoided because it feels uncomfortable, and is it actually important?

None of this needs resolving right away. These are things to turn over, notice, and return to as the work keeps teaching.
