# Kickoff questions

general working notes

approach

- listen more than I produce at the start. people tend to arrive with a fix already in mind, and the real problem is usually hiding behind it
- turn vague adjectives into things I can look at. "clean" and "warm" and "modern" mean wildly different things depending on who says them, so ask for examples they love and examples they can't stand
- keep my own taste in check early. the goal isn't to make something I'd hang on my wall, it's to make something that works for the people who have to live with it
- rough studies beat polished comps while direction is still soft. polish makes people reluctant to criticize, and I want the criticism
- collect the odd quirks nobody thinks to mention until they bump into them: an inherited material, a superstition about a color, a printer that can't hold fine lines

being a studio of just me means the whole process lives in my head unless I put it on paper. that's most of the reason for keeping notes at all.

process

most projects move through a loose rhythm of absorbing, scattering, narrowing, and refining, though it almost never runs in a clean line. absorbing is reading everything, looking at what neighbors and competitors are doing, and gathering the stray language people use when they talk about their own work. the stray language matters a lot. the way someone describes their customers in a casual moment is often better material than anything in the formal brief.

scattering is where I make plenty of bad things on purpose. the point is to find the edges of the space, not to find the answer. if everything I make looks related, I probably haven't pushed far enough. it helps to work fast and ugly here, with thick pens, cut paper, wrong fonts, whatever gets an idea out of my head before I can talk myself out of it.

narrowing is where judgment comes in, and where I have to be honest about which directions are only interesting to me. a useful test is to imagine explaining a direction to a skeptical stranger in a sentence. if I can't, it's probably decoration rather than an idea.

refining is the long tail of spacing, alignment, edge cases, and tiny choices that nobody notices unless they're wrong. this is the part that separates work that feels trustworthy from work that feels almost right. it's also the part that's easiest to rush, because the exciting bit is over.

working with feedback

- feedback usually arrives as a solution ("make the logo bigger") when the underlying note is a feeling ("I don't feel seen"). ask what's behind the request before touching anything
- silence isn't agreement. a slow reply can mean confusion, worry, or just a busy inbox
- show work with context, not naked. a short framing of what I was trying to do, and what kind of response I'm looking for, gets much better reactions than dropping an image into a thread
- separate "I don't like it" from "it doesn't work". both are valid but they call for very different responses
- people are kinder to work in person than on a screen, and harsher in writing than they intend. keep that in mind when reading a blunt message
- when feedback contradicts itself, that's information. usually it means the people involved haven't talked to each other yet

things to keep in mind

- the real audience is rarely the person in the meeting. keep asking who actually encounters this work, and in what mood, and in what hurry
- good design often looks like nothing happened. if the work is doing its job, people may never mention it
- taste is not a substitute for fit. something beautiful that confuses its audience has failed
- constraints are a gift more often than a burden. a tight situation produces sharper ideas than an open field
- save the rejected directions. they're often the seed of something later, and sometimes the better answer for a different project entirely
- be wary of trends that arrive with a lot of enthusiasm and no reasoning. borrowing a look without its logic produces work that ages badly
- accessibility isn't a late pass. contrast, legibility, and clear structure are cheaper to build in than to retrofit
- reading the room matters as much as reading the brief. the same proposal can land well or badly depending on what people are worried about that week
- keep a habit of looking at things that have nothing to do with design: signage in odd places, hand-painted shop fronts, instruction leaflets, old packaging, handwriting on notes left on doors
- my own energy is a material too. work made when I'm depleted tends to show it

open-ended ideas

- a running file of overheard phrases that might become names, taglines, or tone references, kept separate from any particular project
- a small library of "failed on purpose" experiments, where I deliberately break a rule to see what it was protecting
- a habit of writing the explanation of a piece before making it, then checking whether the finished work still matches the explanation
- a recurring exercise of redrawing something old from memory and noticing what my memory kept and what it dropped
- some way to capture how a project felt in the middle, not just how it ended up. the muddle is where most of the learning is, and it evaporates fast
- a plain-language glossary of the terms I use with people outside the field, so I stop slipping into jargon without noticing
- an informal practice of swapping critique with other solo workers, partly for the feedback and partly for the company
- what would a handoff look like if the receiving person had never met me and couldn't ask questions? worth imagining even when it isn't needed
- is there a way to make the early listening visible, so people can see their own words reflected back and correct them before I build on a misunderstanding?
- a standing question to ask of any piece: what would make this feel inevitable rather than merely acceptable?

loose thoughts

some projects want to be loud and some want to be nearly invisible, and the skill is in telling the difference early. it's tempting to bring the same energy to everything, but restraint is a legitimate answer. so is excess, when the situation calls for it.

I also want to stay curious about the parts of the work that feel like administration. how I name files, how I keep versions straight, how I write a message that makes a request easy to answer. those habits quietly shape the experience of working with me, probably more than any single piece of design does.

and it's worth remembering that being small is an advantage as much as a limit. I can be direct, adjust quickly, and care about details that a larger operation would smooth over. the notes are there to protect that, not to turn it into a system.
