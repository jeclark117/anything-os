# Website

Needs a refresh. Low priority.

general working notes

**approach**

- start with the question behind the question. a request for a logo is often a request to be taken seriously. a request for a new layout is often "people can't find what they came for." extra listening here saves a lot of redoing later.
- keep early work rough on purpose. rough things invite honest reactions, polished things invite polite ones. if something looks finished too soon, people stop reacting to the idea and start fussing over spacing.
- words before visuals, or at least alongside them. layouts built around placeholder text tend to fall apart the moment real copy arrives. even a messy draft of headings and sentences changes how the structure wants to behave.
- work from constraints outward. what has to be true, what can't move, what's flexible. the flexible parts are where the interesting choices live.
- prefer fewer moving parts. every extra typeface, color, or effect is something to maintain and something that can clash later.

**process, loosely**

the shape is usually the same even when the work isn't. gather material, make a mess, narrow down, refine, hand over, tidy up. the trap is treating those as a straight line. in practice it loops, and the loops are fine as long as the narrowing step doesn't get skipped. skipping it is how a project ends up with everything in it and nothing in focus.

when gathering, collect things that aren't design at all: signage, packaging, old manuals, handwriting, the way a shop organizes its shelves. reference boards made only of other designers' work produce work that looks like other designers' work.

when making a mess, don't judge anything yet. put down bad ideas fast so they're out of the way. the good ones are often hiding behind the obvious ones, and the obvious ones need to be exhausted first.

when narrowing, write down why something is staying or going. "feels right" isn't a reason that survives a conversation with a client. "it holds up at small sizes and still has character" is.

when refining, zoom out as often as zooming in. it's easy to spend ages on a curve that nobody will ever notice while the overall hierarchy is still muddy. squint at things. look at them upside down. look at them on a bad screen.

when handing over, assume the person receiving the files has no context. name things clearly, leave short notes about intent, and explain what's safe to change and what isn't. a good handover means the work keeps behaving after it leaves the studio.

when tidying up, capture what was learned while it's fresh: what worked, what dragged, what would be done differently. a loose note beats a perfect memory.

**things to keep in mind**

- being a studio of a single person is an advantage in some ways (fast, flexible, coherent) and a risk in others (blind spots, no sounding board). worth building habits that substitute for a second opinion: stepping away, reading work aloud, showing rough drafts to a trusted friend outside the field.
- scope creeps quietly. it rarely arrives as a big request; it arrives as "while you're in there." a polite habit of naming what's inside the work and what's outside it keeps both sides happy.
- feedback is data about a reaction, not an instruction. when somebody says "make the logo bigger," the underlying issue is probably that it doesn't feel confident. solving for the feeling often beats following the literal note.
- accessibility is part of quality, not an add-on. contrast, legibility, sensible reading order, and clear labels make things better for everybody, and they're much cheaper to consider from the start than to retrofit.
- good defaults beat clever options. if a system needs a long explanation, it's probably too clever.
- say no gracefully. not every project fits, and a poor fit costs more than it earns in goodwill, stress, and portfolio clutter.
- protect thinking space. creative work needs stretches without messages and notifications. batch the admin so it doesn't leak into everything.
- keep the business side boring and reliable. contracts, invoices, file backups, and naming conventions are unglamorous, but they're what let the creative side stay relaxed.
- be wary of trends. borrowing a style because everybody has it makes work age badly. borrowing a principle usually doesn't.
- rest matters. tired eyes make worse decisions about color and spacing, and it shows.

**open-ended ideas**

some things worth turning over when there's mental room, none of them settled:

- a small library of reusable layout skeletons, built from past projects, stripped of anything specific. a starting point that still feels custom once content goes in.
- a habit of writing short case studies that focus on the problem and the reasoning, with the final visuals playing a supporting role. people hiring a designer are often buying judgment more than polish.
- a collection of failed experiments, kept deliberately. looking back at what didn't work can be as useful as a highlight reel, and some of the rejects might be seeds for something later.
- more writing in public about process, even in a plain, unpolished way. it clarifies thinking, and it gives prospective collaborators a sense of how conversations would feel.
- a more playful corner for personal work with no brief, no audience, and no review. a place for odd type experiments, bad color combinations, and half-ideas. it tends to feed the paid work in ways that are hard to predict.
- a better system for capturing stray inspiration: a photo of a hand-painted sign, a phrase overheard, a texture on a wall. the trick is to make capturing effortless and revisiting it a pleasure rather than a chore.
- thinking about how a presence online could behave more like a studio space than a brochure: somewhere visitors can sense how the work is approached, how the place sounds, what it would be like to sit down and talk there.
- exploring slower, more tactile methods alongside the screen: printing things out, cutting and pasting, drawing letterforms by hand. the physical step often unlocks decisions the screen keeps hidden.
- a short personal checklist for the end of any project: is it clear, is it kind, is it consistent, is it something that will still make sense when nobody is around to explain it?

**small reminders**

- clarity first, charm second, cleverness only if it earns its place.
- the best projects have a point of view. if the work could belong to anybody, it probably isn't finished.
- leave white space on purpose, in layouts and in the working day.
- keep curiosity switched on. the most useful skill is noticing.
- when stuck, change the medium, the scale, or the question.
- write things down before they evaporate.
