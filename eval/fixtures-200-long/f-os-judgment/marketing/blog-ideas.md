# Blog ideas

- Why I don't do spec work
- What a small studio is good at

**general working notes**

Start with the problem, not the look. Most of what makes a piece of design land is whether it solves what the person actually needed, and that is rarely what they typed into the brief. So the early work is mostly listening and asking dumb questions until the real shape of the problem shows up. I keep a running list of questions that have unlocked projects before: what does success look like for the people on the receiving end, what would make this embarrassing, what do you already hate about what exists.

**approach**

- Talk in plain language. If a concept can't be explained to a stranger without jargon, it probably isn't finished.
- Show the reasoning along with the work. People trust a direction more when they can see the trail that led to it.
- Keep early sketches rough on purpose. Polished things invite polite approval, rough things invite real opinions.
- Make the constraint the feature. Limited palette, limited type, limited space often produce the most distinctive results.
- Write down what isn't being solved, so scope doesn't creep in quietly.

**process thoughts**

I tend to work in loops rather than a straight line: gather, sketch, step away, come back, throw out the best-looking thing because it was only best-looking. The step away matters more than it feels like it should. Ideas ferment. Something that seemed clever when I was deep in it often looks thin from across the room, and something that felt dull turns out to be sturdy.

Keep a folder of things that made me stop and look. Not for copying, for calibrating. When I can't say why I like something, that's the interesting part to chase.

Notes on feedback:

- Separate taste from function when reading comments. "I don't like it" and "it doesn't work" need completely different responses.
- Ask what a reaction is reacting to before changing anything. Often the fix is smaller than the complaint sounds.
- Silence isn't agreement. Check in even when it's quiet.
- Defend the work with reasons, not feelings, and be ready to let go when the reasons run out.

**on materials and medium**

Different media push the thinking in different directions. Paper makes me decisive because erasing is a hassle. Screens make me endlessly tweak because nothing costs anything. Worth switching deliberately depending on whether the problem needs commitment or exploration. Physical prototypes, even crude cardboard ones, expose problems that no mockup ever does. Hands find what eyes skip.

Type deserves patience. Most of the quality of a layout hides in spacing and rhythm, and nobody notices when it's right, only when it's wrong. Same with color: restraint tends to read as confidence.

**on pacing and focus**

Not all stretches of work are equal. Deep, protected stretches are for making, and shallow, scattered stretches are for admin. Mixing them wrecks both. Notifications are the enemy of anything that needs sustained attention. Boredom is useful too, since a lot of ideas show up in the gap when nothing is asking for me.

Momentum is fragile. Leaving a task mid-thought, with a scribbled note about what comes after, makes restarting painless. Leave the sentence unfinished on purpose.

**on making things legible**

A design that needs explaining has usually missed something. Good hierarchy lets a stranger find the point without help. When reviewing, imagine a person who is distracted, in a hurry, and a bit annoyed. If it works for them, it works for everybody. Accessibility isn't an add-on. It's a quality check that happens to improve everything else: contrast, size, plain wording, clear order.

**things to keep in mind**

Running everything alone means being the designer, the bookkeeper, the sales department, and the person who answers email at odd moments. The risk is letting the admin side quietly eat the creative side. Signs worth watching for: sketchbook gathering dust, creative software opened only to fix things, a constant feeling of just reacting.

Then there's the opposite risk, disappearing into craft and neglecting the relationships that keep the work coming. Good work nobody hears about is a hobby.

Rest is part of the process. Tired eyes make muddy decisions about spacing and color, and tired judgment makes muddy decisions about people. Notice when the quality of attention drops and stop pretending otherwise.

Keep the archive tidy. Future me is a stranger who has to find files, understand naming, and reconstruct why something was done. Be kind to that stranger. Write things down while they're fresh, because a reason that's obvious now will be gone soon.

**open-ended ideas**

- A running collection of before-and-after stories that focus on the thinking rather than the visuals.
- Something about the awkward middle of a project, when the exciting beginning is over and the finish line isn't visible.
- How to explain design value to people who've never hired a designer, without sounding defensive.
- Why constraints help, maybe with a small experiment using an absurdly tight constraint just to see what falls out.
- What makes a good brief, and what a good brief looks like from the other side of the table.
- A checklist of questions to ask when a project feels off but nobody can say why.
- Notes on saying "I don't know yet" with confidence.
- Collecting small examples of overlooked design: signage, forms, labels, error messages. The unglamorous stuff does so much work.
- How to make a handoff that doesn't leave the other side stranded.
- The emotional side of showing work: nerves, defensiveness, the odd grief when a favorite direction gets set aside.
- Reading a room, noticing who in a meeting actually holds the decision and who is just loud.
- Sketching with the wrong hand, wrong tool, wrong scale, just to get unstuck.

**questions that keep coming back**

Where does craft end and fussiness begin? There's a point where more polish stops helping the person using the thing and starts helping only my own comfort. Hard to see from inside.

How much of design is persuasion? A decent amount, probably. The idea has to survive contact with other people, and that means telling the story of it well.

What does it mean for work to age well? Some things look dated quickly because they were chasing a moment. Others stay calm. Worth studying the calm ones for what they refused to do.

How do I stay curious when familiarity sets in? Switching mediums, reading outside the field, borrowing process from cooking or carpentry or music. Cross-pollination keeps the hands honest.

**small habits worth keeping**

- Squint at things. Blur the page and see what still reads.
- Print it, or look at it on a different screen, or turn it upside down.
- Read all copy out loud.
- Leave slack in the schedule for things going sideways, because they will.
- Ask for the context behind a request, not just the request.
- Thank people who give honest feedback, especially when it stings.
- Keep a list of phrases clients use to describe what they want, since the vocabulary reveals more than mood boards do.
- Look at the work next to the competition, but only after forming an opinion without it.

**loose thoughts**

Taste is a muscle, built by looking critically at lots of things, including bad ones. Bad examples teach faster because the flaws are loud.

Clarity beats cleverness nearly every time. Cleverness is a bonus when it serves clarity and a tax when it doesn't.

People remember how a thing made them feel before they remember what it looked like. Worth designing for the feeling on purpose.

A good relationship with a collaborator is worth more than a good portfolio piece. Protect those.

Not everything needs a system. But repeated problems usually deserve a system eventually, and spotting that moment is a skill.
