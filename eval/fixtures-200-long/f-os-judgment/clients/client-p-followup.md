# Client P followup

working notes, general stuff

**approach**

the work starts with listening more than drawing. before any marks go on a page, it helps to understand what the client wants people to feel when they meet the brand, and what they're scared people might feel instead. most briefs describe a look. the real brief is usually a mood plus a worry, and the worry is where the interesting work lives.

keep early conversations loose. questions like "what would a stranger say after walking past the front window" get better answers than "describe your values." people struggle with abstractions, but they're great at stories about customers who loved them or got annoyed with them.

being small means there are no layers between the thinking and the making. that's worth protecting. don't build process for its own sake. every step should earn its place by saving effort or sharpening the result. otherwise it's ceremony, and the client ends up paying for ceremony.

work that feels inevitable in hindsight usually came out of a long stretch of feeling lost. that's normal. the lost part isn't a sign something's broken, it's where the weaker options get weeded out.

**process**

- gather before judging. collect references, competitors, old materials, odd scraps from the client's own drawers. resist forming opinions while collecting
- sketch badly and fast on paper. screens make everything look more finished than it is, and finished-looking ideas get defended too early
- put the weakest idea beside the strongest. contrast clarifies things that discussion can't
- show work in context early: on a mock storefront, a mock envelope, a phone screen. a mark floating on white tells you very little
- keep a running list of questions the work is raising, separate from the work itself
- write down why each choice was made while it's fresh. those reasons are what survive when people's memory of the process fades
- let work rest before judging it. a fresh look catches what tired eyes forgive
- when stuck, change the medium. cut paper, a marker, a photocopier, tracing over a printout

**feedback**

reactions are data about the underlying problem, not instructions about the fix. "make it pop" usually means something important is getting lost. "it feels too corporate" often means the structure is fine but the details are cold. translate before acting, and when in doubt, ask what they were hoping to feel when they looked at it.

watch for feedback that's really about something else: a worry about a rival, a board member's taste, an old logo that somebody is fond of. naming the real concern gently usually moves things along faster than defending the work.

try to separate taste from function. taste can be discussed with kindness, but function can be tested. whenever possible, test it. can it be read from across a room, does it survive a bad photocopy, does it still hold together in a tiny avatar circle.

**presenting**

say less than feels necessary. let the work breathe and let silence do some of the persuading. walk people through the problem before showing the answer, so the answer lands as relief instead of as a surprise.

show the reasoning in plain words, not design jargon. if a choice can't be explained to a smart person outside the field, it might not be a strong choice yet.

bring a clear opinion about what's best, but leave honest room for disagreement. clients trust a studio that has a point of view and can also hear a counterpoint.

**things to keep in mind**

- a good follow-up isn't a nudge. it's proof the conversation was actually heard, so it should carry something specific from the talk, a detail or a question that shows the thinking kept going after the call ended
- relationships are the long game. the work on the table matters, but so does how it feels to work together. clients remember feeling calm and well-informed more than they remember clever slides
- big possibilities are fun to daydream about, and daydreaming is fine, but they can quietly crowd out the work that's actually in front of the studio. keep the ambient excitement from leaking into how current work gets treated
- scope grows in small, polite increments. name it kindly and early, before it hardens into an expectation
- be careful with enthusiasm in writing. things said casually can get read as promises
- protect the quiet stretches meant for making. admin expands to fill whatever room it's given
- keep files tidy as if a stranger will inherit them. a stranger might be future-you after a long gap
- credit collaborators generously and specifically
- when a client is nervous, slow down and explain more. when a client is excited, slow down and explain more. it's the same move

**brand work in general**

a brand system is really a set of habits: how things are said, how they're spaced, what gets emphasized, what gets left out. the logo is just the most visible habit. when clients over-focus on the mark, steer them toward the other habits, because those are what customers meet most of their day.

consistency matters, but rigid consistency is brittle. better to define strong principles and a generous range of permitted variation, so people inside a company can make good calls without phoning the studio every time.

old brands carry equity that isn't visible on a balance sheet: familiarity, small affections, shortcuts in people's heads. before changing anything big, find out what people would miss. sometimes the answer is a color, sometimes a phrase, sometimes just how the packaging feels in the hand. evolving what exists is often more respectful and more effective than replacing it, though not always, and it's worth staying honest about when it isn't.

naming, voice, and visuals work best when built together instead of handed off in sequence. a visual identity designed without knowing how the company talks tends to feel like a costume.

**open-ended ideas**

- a brand guide that's a living little site instead of a document that goes stale in a drawer
- a private library of discarded directions, stripped of identifying details, to revisit when a new problem rhymes with an old problem
- short postmortems after a project wraps, just a paragraph on what was surprising
- design the worst-case version of a brand on purpose, then invert it. it's a quick way to find what the right version avoids
- a deck of kickoff question cards that make opening conversations feel like a game instead of an interview
- explore the non-visual: sound, texture, smell, the heft of a paper stock, the click of a closing box
- study how brands age, especially the awkward in-between stage when things are partly old and partly new, and design for that stage instead of pretending it doesn't exist
- accessibility as a starting constraint rather than a final check: contrast, legibility at tiny sizes, kindness to tired eyes
- sustainable print choices as a standard offering, framed around what's beautiful rather than what's virtuous
- trade small experiments with collaborators in other disciplines, writers, illustrators, photographers, just to see what bounces
- impose odd constraints on practice pieces: a restrained palette, only circles, only the letters of a client's name
- give the studio's own identity some care. the cobbler's children problem is real
- keep a folder of things that made a stranger smile in public: signs, labels, handmade notices. good design is often anonymous
- try explaining a whole identity to a child, or to a skeptical relative at a dinner table. whatever survives that translation is the core
- sketch what a brand's "off day" looks like, the version that appears when the budget is thin or the printer messes up, and make that version still look decent

**small reminders**

- the early idea isn't sacred, and neither is the late idea
- ask for what's needed in plain words, early
- rest is part of the process. good judgment rarely comes from exhaustion
- leave room for being wrong, and leave room for being pleasantly surprised
- the best work often has a little awkwardness left in it, a place where a human hand is visible. polish too hard and that vanishes
- good questions are cheaper than good guesses
- look at the work upside down, squinted at, reflected in a mirror, reduced to a thumbnail. it reveals problems that normal viewing hides
- when the work feels stale, go look at something unrelated: architecture, textiles, handwritten menus, hardware store shelves. then come back and see what leaks in
- keep a loose sense of what the studio is for, beyond any particular job, and let that quietly shape what kinds of work feel right and wrong to chase
