# Client K

general working notes

Loose thoughts on how I like to approach packaging work, mostly so I stop re-deriving them every time.

Packaging is a strange discipline because the thing gets judged in the hand, on a shelf, in a photo, and on a table at home long after the purchase. Each of those is a different audience. The shelf wants a quick read from a distance. The photo wants shapes that survive cropping. The hand wants texture, weight, and that small moment when the lid comes off. The table at home wants something that doesn't look embarrassing next to everything else in the room. I try to hold all of these in my head while sketching, but I don't try to solve them at the same time.

approach

- Start with the smell, not the logo. For anything scented, the words people use to describe it do more work than any mark I could draw. Warm, sharp, powdery, green, smoky, creamy. I make a loose pile of adjectives for each scent and let colour and type fall out of that.
- Look at the family before the member. Variants need to feel related but not identical. If I can only tell them apart by reading the label, the system is too timid. If they look like unrelated products, it's too loose. The sweet spot is where you could cover the name and still guess they belong together.
- Work in greyscale for longer than feels natural. Hierarchy problems hide behind colour. If the layout works in black and white, colour is a bonus rather than a rescue.
- Build the dieline early, even roughly. Flat artwork that ignores folds, glue flaps, and seams is a fantasy. Wrapping a paper mock around an actual jar tells me more than staring at a screen ever will.
- Keep the copy minimal and get the real copy early. Placeholder text lies about how much room there is. Ask for the legal bits, the safety wording, the burn guidance, the ingredient lists, and the barcode area as soon as it's polite to.

process notes

I move through loose stages, and I try to be honest about which stage I'm in so I don't polish something that hasn't earned it.

- Gathering: samples, reference piles, whatever the brand already has out in the world. Photos of competing shelves. Notes about what feels overused (the hand-drawn botanical, the muted earthy palette, the serif that whispers). The point isn't to avoid all of it, just to know when I'm choosing it on purpose.
- Rough directions: a bunch of quick, ugly, different ideas. Different in structure, not just colour. Paper sketches count. If every direction shares the same layout, it isn't a set of directions, it's a single idea in costumes.
- Narrowing: show fewer than I want to. A tidy pitch with a clear recommendation beats a wall of options that hands the decision back as homework.
- Refining: spacing, type details, optical corrections, the stuff nobody notices unless it's wrong.
- Prepping files: sensible names, labelled layers, fonts and licensing sorted, outlines made, spare versions ready for the printer.

type notes

- Wide-tracked capitals for scent names feel expected. Worth testing a quiet serif next to something blunt and mechanical, just to see what the friction does.
- Weight contrast often beats size contrast. A heavy word beside a light one can carry a label without anything needing to shout.
- Leave generous air around the name. Crowded labels read cheap.
- Candles get read in dim light, by a lamp or a window. Test legibility at arm's length with the room darkened.

things to keep in mind

- Printing is where nice ideas go to get muddy. Dark flat colours on uncoated stock soak up ink and lose detail. Fine hairlines vanish. Tiny reversed type fills in. Leave generous tolerance wherever trim and fold are involved.
- Candle vessels might be glass, ceramic, or tin, and each behaves differently with labels and sleeves. Curved surfaces distort anything that wraps. Check how a label sits across a seam in the container.
- Heat matters. Anything near a flame or a warm jar should be considered for scorching, curling, and glue that gives up. This isn't my area of expertise, so ask the maker rather than guess.
- Shipping is part of the design. A beautiful box that arrives crushed is a bad box. Ask how it travels and what it sits inside.
- Sustainability claims are slippery. Be careful with words like "eco" or "natural" on the artwork unless the client can back them up. Prefer simple, honest material choices over green-flavoured styling.
- Contrast on small type matters, especially pale ink on tinted stock. Scent names shouldn't depend on colour alone to be told apart.
- Consistent file and version naming saves me from sending the wrong thing at the worst moment.
- Keep a short list of questions and send them in a batch rather than dribbling them out. People answer a tidy list faster.
- Write down why I made a choice at the time. Future me, defending it in a review, will have forgotten.

communication habits

- Over-explain the constraints, under-explain the taste. Clients usually know what they like when they see it, but they rarely know what the printer can do.
- Show work in context: on a shelf photo, in a hand, on a table. Flat artboards flatter nothing.
- When feedback is vague ("make it pop", "more premium"), ask for an example of something they love and something they hate. Translate adjectives into concrete moves.
- Keep a calm paper trail. A short recap after calls, in plain language, so nobody's memory has to carry the load.
- Be quick to say when something is outside what I can reasonably do, and offer an alternative rather than a bare no.

open-ended ideas

Loose stuff to chew on, nothing here is settled.

- What if the scents were distinguished by texture or structure rather than only colour? A different emboss pattern, a different fold, a notch in the sleeve. Tactile coding could work for people who shop by feel.
- Could the inside of the box carry the personality? The outside often has to be safe for retail, but the inside is a private surprise. A pattern, a short line of text, a hidden illustration.
- Is there a way to make the packaging reusable that people would actually use? A tin that becomes a pencil holder is the cliché, but maybe a sleeve that doubles as a match holder, or a lid that works as a tray. Something that earns a spot on the shelf after the candle is gone.
- Letterforms as the main graphic. Skip illustration and let a custom-ish wordmark carry the mood. Stretch it, crop it, repeat it across the faces of the box.
- A pattern system built from a very simple module that rearranges itself for each scent. Cheap to produce, easy to extend if the range grows.
- Experiment with how the scent name is revealed. Hidden until the lid is lifted? Printed small and confident with lots of air around it?
- Pull colour from the raw materials of each scent: gather tones from photos of the ingredients, then desaturate until they feel like a family.
- Sound and ritual: the click of a lid, the way a sleeve slides. Unboxing is easy to over-engineer, but a satisfying friction fit is worth a test.
- A tiny printed card with burn care and a bit of personality, instead of a dry instructions slip.
- Photograph the packaging near real candlelight early, to see how glossy finishes and metallics behave around the glow.

reminders for myself

- Don't fall in love with the sketch that arrives easily. It's usually the safest, and attachment blocks everything after it.
- Breaks help. Stepping away from a layout and coming back reveals what's off in seconds.
- A good system makes the next piece easy. If extending the design feels painful, the system is wrong.
- Save rejected ideas in a folder. They're often the right answer for some other job.
- Keep the studio tidy enough that paper samples and mockups don't bury the real work, and keep the schedule loose enough to leave room to think.
- Not every idea needs to be clever. Sometimes the best candle packaging is calm, well spaced, and honest about what's inside.
