# Client L: followup

general working notes

loose thoughts on how the studio runs, jotted mostly for myself. nothing in here is a plan, just the stuff that keeps proving useful.

approach

being small is the whole appeal. there's no layer between the person asking and the person making, which keeps things quick and also means nothing gets caught by a colleague. so habits have to do the catching. the habit that pays off most is treating every project as though a client will pull the folder back up long after it ships, because they usually do. a request arrives out of nowhere, and past me needs to have left enough breadcrumbs for present me to understand what was going on.

principles that hold up:

- start from the problem the work is meant to solve, not the format it ends up in
- make the reasoning visible so clients can disagree with the thinking instead of just the surface
- keep the brief truthful. if it has drifted, say so out loud rather than quietly designing for a brief that no longer exists
- let the work be wrong early, when being wrong is cheap
- protect the quiet stretches. good ideas rarely show up in a cluttered head

process

the shape of most projects is roughly: hear them out, poke at the edges, sketch badly, show something rough, refine, finish with care. the temptation is to skip the rough stage and go straight to polish because polish feels like progress. it isn't. a polished wrong idea is harder to let go of, for the designer and for the client.

notes on the stages:

- hearing them out: ask what the thing is for before asking what it should look like. people frequently describe a solution when what they really have is a problem. the more they talk about why, the less they talk about typefaces
- poking at the edges: look at what sits next to the work. who handles it, where it lives, what it competes with, what happens to it as it ages
- sketching: keep it ugly on purpose. a rough page invites comment, a clean page invites a verdict
- showing: present the reasoning alongside the visuals, and say what kind of feedback is useful. "does this feel right" gets vague answers; "does this read as trustworthy" gets something to work with
- refining: this is where most of the craft lives. spacing, rhythm, how the eye travels, what gets left out
- finishing: check the boring stuff. file formats, bleed, how color behaves across screens and paper, whether the piece survives being photocopied or squinted at

things that keep resurfacing:

- print behaves differently from screens and it still surprises me. color shifts, paper soaks up ink, small type that looked fine on a monitor turns to mush. proofing on real stock is worth the hassle
- finished projects aren't frozen. source files, typefaces, and image origins need to stay findable. archiving carefully is a kindness to a future self
- imagery dates faster than structure. a good layout can carry fresh material without much surgery if it was built with that in mind
- sloppy file naming is a debt that compounds. a folder full of "final" and "final-really" is a trap

working with clients

- check-ins after a project wraps keep the relationship alive, and that's frequently where new work quietly starts
- write things down while the conversation is fresh. memory edits itself, usually in flattering ways
- separate "nice to have" from "needed" when talking through scope, and keep the language plain
- be upfront about what is easy and what isn't. clients usually respect a clear explanation more than a vague yes
- people float ideas casually to test the water. worth noticing the difference between a passing thought and a real itch, without pouncing on either

things to keep in mind

- scope creeps in through friendly requests. it rarely announces itself
- feedback from a group of reviewers is frequently contradictory. look for the underlying worry rather than averaging the comments
- rest helps. fresh eyes catch problems that long staring misses
- keep a swipe file of things that made a good impression, and note why, not just what
- be wary of work that pulls the studio away from what it's good at, even when it looks attractive
- admin is part of the craft. a messy back office leaks into the front

on feedback

feedback is information about the viewer's experience, not an instruction about the fix. when a client says a page feels cold, the answer might be color, might be spacing, might be the words. the job is to find the real cause and not just obey the surface suggestion. it helps to ask what they were hoping to feel, then work backward.

it's also worth separating taste from function. a preference for a different shade is a conversation; a layout that confuses a reader is a problem. each deserves respect, but they call for different responses.

and silence isn't approval. quiet can mean satisfied, busy, confused, or unsure how to say what's bothering them. a gentle nudge with a specific question draws out more than a general "thoughts?"

on words

copy and design are the same job wearing different clothes. a layout with placeholder text lies about how it will behave, so it's worth getting real wording in early, even if it's rough. headlines that run long break grids; captions that run short leave holes. reading the actual words out loud exposes awkward rhythm faster than any critique does.

short plain labels usually beat clever ones. if a reader has to decode a heading, the heading has failed, however pretty it looks.

on materials and craft

- physical things teach lessons screens can't. holding a proof changes how it's judged
- constraints are friends. a limited palette, a restricted set of typefaces, a tight format: they force clarity
- details are where trust gets built. alignment, edges, careful corners, the quiet feeling of something being cared for
- mistakes in print are permanent in a way that makes proofreading feel serious. read aloud, read backward, hand it to fresh eyes

thinking about taste

taste is mostly a record of exposure plus a willingness to be bothered by things. the more carefully i look at ordinary objects, signage, packaging, menus, forms, the more i notice why some feel effortless. frequently it comes down to restraint. less decoration, more confidence in space.

it also helps to remember the audience isn't a designer. they don't care about the clever grid. they care whether they can find what they need and whether it feels like it was made by people who cared. clarity before personality, cleverness only if it earns its place.

on pacing

busy stretches and quiet stretches teach different things. busy stretches reveal which habits are solid and which only work under no pressure. quiet stretches are for sharpening: tidying archives, refining templates, wandering through old sketches, making things nobody asked for just to keep the hand loose.

there's a risk in treating quiet as a problem to be fixed with busywork. occasionally the useful move is to look around, read, and let curiosity pick the direction.

open-ended ideas

- a small library of reusable layout skeletons that handle swapping imagery gracefully
- a habit of capturing why a design choice was made, in a line, right beside the file
- a way to show clients how a piece evolved, from early sketch to final, as a story they can keep
- what would a design look like if it were built from the start to be revised rather than replaced?
- experiments with paper, finishes, and binding just to see how they change the feeling of the same layout
- a checklist for handing over files, so clients never have to ask for the source material
- a practice of revisiting old work to see what held up and what dated badly, and why
- debrief questions worth asking: what surprised us, what felt heavy, what would we change if we could redo it
- maybe a quiet reference shelf of physical samples, so ideas have something to push against besides a screen

working habits worth protecting

- keep the desk clear enough to think
- walk away from a stuck problem and let it turn over quietly
- read widely outside design: history, fiction, instruction manuals, anything with a strong voice
- keep rough notes in a place that's easy to find again
- be kind to the version of me who opens this folder later

closing thought: the best work usually comes from caring a bit more than the situation demands, and from being easy to work with while doing it. nothing here needs resolving. just a pile of reminders to look over when a project asks for the whole toolbox.
