# Client M: signage

- Four signs, matte finish
- Sign shop has the files

general working notes

**approach**

- begin with where the piece will physically live, not with the layout. a sign is an object in a space, so the real questions are about viewing distance, lighting, what sits behind it, and how people move past it.
- type is the job. if the hierarchy doesn't read at a glance from across a room, no amount of polish rescues it.
- keep the system tight. restrained palette, restrained type, plenty of breathing room. the quieter the piece, the more the proportions have to be right.
- sketch on paper before touching the screen. the screen invites fiddling, and paper keeps the structure honest.
- design for the least flattering conditions: glare, shadow, a tired person walking past with their hands full and their mind elsewhere.
- let the content decide the format, not the other way around. if a name is long, give it room instead of squeezing it to fit a template.
- when stuck, remove things. most stuck layouts have too much in them.

**process**

- gather the practical constraints early: mounting method, substrate, where fixings can go, anything the building or landlord won't allow.
- build artwork at real scale where possible, or at a clean known ratio, so nothing gets surprised by enlargement later.
- convert text to outlines before anything goes to a fabricator, and keep a live-text version in the archive in case edits come back.
- define colors in a way the production side can actually match. screens lie. a physical swatch beats a screen value every time.
- leave generous bleed and safe margins, and label them clearly in the file so nobody has to guess what the lines mean.
- name files plainly with a consistent scheme, and add a short readme explaining anything non-obvious. future me is basically a stranger.
- proof as close to real as possible: print at scale on cheap paper, tape it up in the actual spot or somewhere similar, then stand back and squint.
- keep a running log of questions asked and answers given, in writing, so the reasoning is easy to retrace when memory fails.
- finish a project by tidying it: clean layers, clear names, final artwork separated from experiments. it costs little now and saves a headache later.

**things to keep in mind**

- surface finishes change how color reads. a deep shade on a low-sheen surface can look darker and flatter than it did on screen, and pale shades can turn chalky.
- contrast needs margin. build in more than seems necessary, because fabrication variance quietly eats it.
- fine details vanish at distance. thin strokes, tight spacing, and small enclosed shapes fill in or wash out. shrink the artwork to thumbnail size and see what survives.
- reflections matter more than expected, especially in spaces with big windows or overhead lighting that rakes across the surface.
- corner radii change the whole personality of a piece. consistent radii across a family of objects feel deliberate, while mismatched ones feel like an accident.
- think about the person who installs it. clear orientation marks, an obvious top, and hardware locations that make sense will save everybody grief.
- accessibility is part of the brief from the start: contrast, legible letterforms, sensible mounting heights, and tactile considerations where they apply.
- materials have lives after install. weather, cleaning products, fingerprints, scuffs, sun. a design that only looks good brand new isn't finished.
- clients tend to look at artwork on a phone while distracted. the design has to survive a bad preview as well as a good room.
- alignment across a family of objects matters more than any individual flourish. a consistent anchor point does a lot of quiet work.
- be wary of trends that will date quickly. signage tends to stay up far longer than anybody plans for.
- don't fall in love with a detail that only the designer will ever notice.

**talking with the client**

- show work in context whenever possible. a mockup photographed on a real wall is more persuasive than any amount of explanation.
- explain the why in plain words. people accept a lot more when they understand the reasoning, and they push back more gently too.
- ask what the space feels like to them before showing anything. their vocabulary is useful, even when it's vague.
- keep feedback rounds focused. ask for reactions to specific things instead of general approval, and gently steer away from fixing taste by committee.
- write down what was approved in plain language, so there's a shared reference when memories drift.
- when a request would hurt the work, say so kindly and offer an alternative that gets at the same goal.

**open-ended ideas**

- a small studio reference sheet showing how common materials actually look in person versus on screen, with physical samples taped in and notes about quirks.
- a handoff checklist template for fabricators, covering the stuff that always gets forgotten: orientation, finish notes, color references, hardware, tolerances.
- explore wayfinding logic: could a family of objects tell a quiet story through repetition and variation? subtle shifts in alignment, a recurring mark, a consistent rhythm of spacing.
- try designing the negative space first and letting the content fill in around it.
- photograph finished work in poor light on purpose, to learn what holds up and what falls apart.
- assemble a pocket kit for site visits: measuring tape, level, swatches, painter's tape, a small camera, a notebook that doesn't mind getting crumpled.
- think harder about how a piece ages. what would still look good after a long stretch of scuffs and sun? maybe design for patina instead of fighting it.
- play with texture contrast: a smooth mark on a grainy ground, or the reverse, and see which feels more at home in the space.
- test unusual typefaces with real words from the client's world instead of placeholder text. odd letter combinations expose weaknesses fast.
- write a short "things I wish I'd known" list for newer designers, built from mistakes made in the studio.
- build a library of annoying edge cases: long names, awkward punctuation, tall letters next to short ones, all the things that break tidy layouts.
- sketch how a design might behave in a different material altogether, just to learn which parts of it are structural and which are decorative.
- keep a folder of signage that works well out in the world, and write a sentence about why each of them does.

**working habits**

- step away from the screen regularly. fresh eyes catch spacing problems that tired eyes forgive.
- print things. look at them upside down, in a mirror, from across the room, at the edge of a bad light. each view reveals something different.
- keep a loose notebook for stray thoughts, because good ideas often arrive while doing something unrelated.
- resist polishing too early. rough work stays flexible, and flexible work gets better.
- be generous with white space and stingy with decoration.
- treat every constraint as a design input rather than an obstacle. the awkward wall, the odd bracket, the limited palette: they usually make the work more specific and therefore better.
- when in doubt, simplify, then simplify again, and then check whether anything important got lost along the way.
