**how i tend to approach a new brief**

i listen more than i talk. most of what a client says early on is a symptom, not the actual problem, so i keep asking why until the answer gets a bit embarrassing. "we need a new logo" often means "people don't understand what we do." that's a different job and it deserves a different kind of attention.

after that i write the problem down in plain words, as if explaining it to a friend who knows nothing about the field. if the explanation is clumsy, the thinking is clumsy. design that grows out of a clumsy problem statement usually looks nice and does nothing.

things i keep coming back to:
- the work is a conversation, not a delivery. show rough things early, even when it feels exposed.
- constraints are a gift. a tight palette, a limited set of typefaces, a strict grid: they make decisions easier and the result more coherent.
- look at what's around the thing being designed. context decides more than taste does.
- write the reasoning down next to the visuals. clients forget why a choice was made unless the why is attached to it.
- leave room to be wrong. the best direction sometimes shows up after a bad direction has been thrown out.

**process, roughly**

*research and soaking.* collect references that have nothing to do with design: old packaging, signage, hand-painted shop fronts, textbooks, maps, receipts. build a loose wall of them. patterns show up if you stare long enough.

*sketching badly on purpose.* paper before screens, pencil, fast, ugly. screens make everything look finished too soon, and finished-looking work is hard to criticize candidly, including by me.

*narrowing.* pick the directions that feel most alive, not the ones that feel safest. then ask what would have to be true for each to work. if the answer needs a lot of explaining, it's probably fragile.

*building out.* systems before pages. color, type, spacing, tone of voice, how things behave when they're stretched or squeezed. a good system survives being handled by people who aren't me.

*handoff.* clean files, plain naming, a short guide that a stranger could follow. i'd rather over-explain a file structure than field confused questions later.

**running the studio side**

being a solo studio means every role is mine: designer, producer, accountant, receptionist, person who forgets to eat lunch. the trick is keeping the roles from bleeding into each other. the admin voice and the creative voice want different things from a day, and when they argue the creative work usually loses.

some habits worth protecting:
- keep financial records tidy and boring. boring is the goal. a messy ledger creates a low hum of anxiety that leaks into everything.
- separate business spending from personal spending, always, no exceptions worth making.
- keep receipts somewhere obvious, in a place future-me can find without detective work.
- write down what a project actually demanded versus what was expected. over lots of jobs that gap teaches more than any book on pricing.
- leave a buffer in the schedule for the unplanned. unplanned things always arrive.
- protect long stretches of quiet for the hard thinking, and cluster the emails, calls and invoices elsewhere.

**on pricing and worth, generally**

it's easy to undervalue work that feels fun. if a job is enjoyable, that's not a reason to charge less for it. think about the value of the result for the client, the experience brought to it, and the risk absorbed, not just the effort that shows up on a tracker. also worth remembering that scope creep is rarely malicious; it's usually enthusiasm. naming it kindly and early keeps the relationship intact.

**working with clients**

- clarify who actually makes the final call. too many opinions flatten a design.
- ask for feedback in terms of goals rather than preferences. "does this feel trustworthy?" gets a better answer than "do you like it?"
- say no gracefully when something isn't a fit. a polite refusal early beats a resentful project later.
- be generous with credit and candid about limits.
- stay curious about their business. the more i understand how they earn their living, the better the work gets.

**keeping the eye sharp**

look at things that aren't design. go outside, walk without headphones, notice lettering on old buildings and the way light lands on paper. keep a running file of small observations: a color combination seen on a fruit stall, an awkward but charming kerning mistake on a hand-written sign. these become raw material later.

read widely. fiction, history, manuals, anything with a strong voice. design is a way of giving form to meaning, so more meaning in the head means more form available in the hands.

practice small, self-directed exercises with no audience: redraw a letter badly, build a poster from a mood, re-lay out a page from a book that annoyed me. no client, no brief, no stakes. this is where the odd ideas get tested safely.

**open-ended ideas to chew on**

- a small set of printed pieces that explain how the studio works, to hand to prospective clients so they arrive already understanding the process.
- an archive of rejected directions, annotated with why they didn't make it. could be useful, could be a little painful to read.
- some kind of recurring, low-pressure personal project that keeps the studio's voice visible between jobs.
- a template for project post-mortems that's short enough to actually get filled in.
- what would it look like to build a library of reusable layout skeletons that still feel bespoke? maybe the trick is making them awkward enough to need adjusting.
- a quieter way of presenting work, less polished deck and more tactile objects on a table. does it change how clients respond?
- collaborating with a writer, illustrator or developer on a loose, as-needed basis, so the studio can stretch without turning into something bigger than intended.
- a short reading list for clients who want to understand design thinking before the work starts.
- a habit of photographing work in messy, real settings rather than clean mockups, to show how it lives in the world.
- a glossary of studio jargon translated into plain language, so clients never feel talked over.

**reminders to self**

- finished is better than perfect, but not at the cost of care.
- rest is part of the process. tired eyes make muddy decisions.
- be kind to the earlier version of me who made past files. they were doing their best with what they knew.
- ask more questions. then ask a better question.
- the point is to make things that help people understand, feel, or find their way. everything else is scaffolding.
