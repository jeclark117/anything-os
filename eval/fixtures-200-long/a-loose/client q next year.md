general working notes

**approach**
- start from what a reader should feel on closing the report, not from how it looks. work backward from that and the structure mostly falls out on its own.
- keep a running scrapbook of references that have nothing to do with the project. old packaging, municipal signage, the way a cheap receipt is set. the useful moves usually come from sideways looks, not from studying other reports in the same sector.
- treat the data like characters. some are loud, some are quiet, some only matter in context. decide who gets the spotlight before touching layout.
- keep the system small. a tight type palette, a restrained set of colors, a grid that can flex. constraints make pages feel related without making them feel identical.
- say less on each page than feels safe. white space does work, it tells the reader where to breathe.
- when stuck, change the medium. sketch on paper, print and mark up, cut the pages apart and rearrange them on the floor. a different surface shakes loose a different idea.

**process**
- begin by reading everything the organization has published lately, plus whatever they say about themselves in public. note the vocabulary they reach for and the words they avoid. the design should sound like them on a good day.
- before any visuals, write the skeleton in plain text. headings, pull quotes, where charts go, what each chart is supposed to prove. if a chart can't state its point in a sentence, it probably shouldn't exist.
- rough pass in grayscale. no color, no photography, just hierarchy and rhythm. if it works in gray, color is a bonus and not a rescue.
- build the template pages before the special pages. the dull pages carry the document, so they deserve the most care.
- test layouts with ugly real content, not tidy placeholder text. long names, awkward line breaks, charts with outliers. the real material always breaks something.
- step away from the work before the final read-through. fresh eyes catch the stray widows, the mismatched captions, the heading that drifted off the grid.
- keep a changes log in plain language so feedback rounds don't turn into archaeology.
- export early and often, and look at the output on a different screen and on paper. color shifts and tiny type problems only show up away from the working window.

**things to keep in mind**
- accessibility isn't an afterthought. contrast, readable sizes, sensible reading order, alt text for images, color never being the only carrier of meaning. build it in at the skeleton stage so it doesn't have to be bolted on.
- a report has to survive being skimmed, being printed badly, being forwarded as a file with the fonts missing, and being read on a phone in a hallway. design for the worst reading conditions and the best ones will take care of themselves.
- the people who sign off are rarely the people who read. keep both audiences in mind: the reviewer who wants reassurance and the reader who wants a reason to keep turning pages.
- photography and illustration carry more weight than they get credit for. a weak image makes strong typography look cheap, so budget attention for sourcing and editing, not only for layout.
- be honest in charts. start axes where they should start, label directly where possible, avoid decorative effects that distort the shape of the data. trust is the whole product here.
- keep source files tidy as I go. named layers, consistent styles, a clear folder structure. future me is the most frequent collaborator and the least patient.
- feedback is usually about a feeling that gets expressed as a specific fix. ask what's bothering them before changing the thing they pointed at.
- protect the quiet sections. the appendix, the notes, the contact page. they're where careful design shows, and where sloppiness gets noticed by the people who read closely.
- stay aware of how much the production side depends on other people's timing: writers, editors, printers, reviewers. leave slack in the process for their delays, since they always arrive.
- being a studio of a single person means no second set of eyes by default. build in proofing habits that substitute for one: reading aloud, reading backward, reading in a different typeface, reading on paper.

**open-ended ideas**
- a report that changes density as it goes. sparse and airy at the start, tightening toward the data-heavy middle, then opening up again for the close. the pacing would feel like a piece of music.
- using a single recurring graphic device, a line, a shape, a texture, that evolves across the sections and ties the story together without needing a mascot or a gimmick.
- a modular chart kit that can be reused across sections so the visual language stays consistent and production gets faster with each section completed.
- treating the cover as a poster rather than a title page. something that earns a place on a wall or a desk, so the report has a life beyond the inbox.
- a companion piece that's much lighter than the report itself: a fold-out summary, a set of cards, a short sequence of social-ready panels. the heavy document stays authoritative while the light one does the inviting.
- sound or motion for the digital version, if it would serve the content. small transitions that clarify a change in the data are fine; anything that only decorates is out.
- typography experiments: a variable typeface that shifts weight with emphasis, a display face used only for numerals, a text face chosen for how it behaves at very small sizes in footnotes.
- print finishing as a storytelling tool. paper with a texture that matches the subject, a spot treatment on a single word, an uncoated stock for the sections that should feel candid.
- an index of voices: pull a short phrase from every contributor and set them as a running marginal thread through the pages, so the reader hears people and not just an institution.
- a visual glossary at the back, built as a small reference spread that explains the chart types used. it makes the whole document feel generous.

**loose bits**
- keep a list of things I'm tired of seeing: stock handshake photos, gradient overlays on every image, charts with a rainbow of categories. avoiding clichés is easier when they're written down.
- look for places where a table is better than a chart. sometimes the honest answer is rows and columns set beautifully.
- think about the physical weight of the thing. a report that feels substantial in the hand says something before a word is read.
- reserve time for the unglamorous finishing work: consistent punctuation in captions, matching spacing in lists, checking that every cross-reference points at the right place.
- remember that restraint is a skill, and that most of the pages that look effortless took the most deleting.
- note down what worked and what didn't after any project wraps, while it's fresh, in plain language, with no polish. those notes are worth more than any portfolio piece.
- stay curious about the subject matter. the better the understanding of what the organization actually does, the less the design has to pretend.
- keep room for play between projects. small experiments with no audience keep the instincts sharp and occasionally turn into the next big idea.
