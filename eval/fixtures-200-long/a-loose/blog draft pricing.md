general working notes

**approach**

start with the problem behind the problem. requests usually arrive as a solution already ("we need a new logo", "can you redo the site") and the useful move is asking what went wrong that made them reach for that. the answer shapes the work more than any brief does.

questions worth keeping handy:
- what does a good outcome look like, in plain language and not design language?
- what have they tried that didn't land?
- what are they afraid of getting wrong? this tends to surface constraints nobody mentioned
- who isn't in the conversation but will have to live with the result?

keep early research cheap. look at how their customers actually behave, read the support inbox if they'll share it, wander through competitors' material with a skeptical eye. the goal isn't a mood board. it's a feel for what's crowded and what's wide open.

prefer working in the open. share rough thinking early, while direction is still easy to change, instead of unveiling something polished that people feel obliged to love or reject. rough work invites honest reactions. polished work invites politeness.

**process**

- sketch on paper before screens. paper is slower in a helpful way, it keeps me from falling for whatever shows up early
- make lots of bad versions on purpose. the bad ones map the edges of the space
- name the idea in a sentence before building it out. if the sentence is mushy, the design will be too
- step away from the screen before judging anything. fresh eyes catch muddy hierarchy and clumsy spacing right away
- build a system, not just an artifact. good work knows what to do when it meets a situation nobody planned for
- test at the awkward extremes: tiny, huge, cropped, grayscale, squinting, on a cracked screen, in bad light
- write the rationale while designing, not afterward. reasons recalled later are usually reasons invented
- keep a running list of things that bug me about the work. most are noise, a few are the real problem

handoff deserves care. leave behind more than files. a short explanation of why things are the way they are lets future people extend the work instead of accidentally breaking it. think of it as a guided tour rather than a manual.

**things to keep in mind**

- clarity beats cleverness nearly every time. cleverness is fine as a garnish
- constraints are friends. a tight palette, a restrained choice of type, a strict grid: they make choices faster and the result more coherent
- taste turns into a trap when it replaces listening. my preferences are an input, not a verdict
- feedback is information about a reaction, not an instruction. "make it pop" usually means something feels flat, and the fix may have nothing to do with making anything pop
- scope creeps quietly. notice when a small favor is becoming a new body of work, and say so kindly and early
- be honest about what design can't fix. a confusing offer stays confusing inside a beautiful layout
- accessibility isn't a finishing pass. contrast, legibility, motion sensitivity, and keyboard paths are far easier to bake in than bolt on
- some projects are better passed along to a better-suited person, and recognizing that early is a form of respect for the client
- rest matters. tired judgment is bad judgment, and clients can tell

there's also a difference between a client who wants a collaborator and a client who wants a vendor. both are legitimate, but they need different energy, different communication, and different expectations about who owns the big calls. worth noticing which situation I'm in before getting too deep, because mismatched expectations cause more friction than any design disagreement.

and a reminder that people rarely read what we make the way we read it. we see every decision. they see a feeling, a first impression, a sense of whether something is trustworthy or fussy or friendly. designing for that actual experience, not for the version only I can see, keeps the work honest.

**running a studio solo**

every role lands on me: designer, bookkeeper, salesperson, janitor of the file system. a few habits that help:

- admin expands to fill whatever space it's given. protect space for actual design
- name and organize files as if a stranger will inherit them, because future me is basically a stranger
- isolation shows up as doubt. having a trusted peer to show work to is worth a lot
- write things down while they're fresh: why a choice was made, what the client said, what was tried and dropped. memory is a lousy archive
- clear boundaries around availability make the work better, not worse
- variety is a feature. a mix of small, quick jobs and long, deep ones keeps both skills and curiosity from going stale
- rituals help: a way of opening a project, a way of closing it, a way of marking the shift from exploring to refining

it's easy to romanticize independence and forget that the trade-off is carrying all the uncertainty personally. the steadier the routines around the work, the less that uncertainty leaks into the design itself.

**open-ended ideas**

- a plain-language glossary for clients, covering the terms that make people nod without really understanding
- showing process artifacts (rejected directions, messy sketches, wrong turns) as part of the story instead of hiding them
- a small library of reusable discovery questions, grouped by the kind of problem they tend to uncover
- case writeups that focus on the problem and the reasoning rather than just the pretty end result
- an experiment in teaching: explaining the work to a total beginner forces precision, and exposes lazy habits
- a recurring self-review of older projects, asking what held up, what aged badly, and why
- constraint games: what if the project had to work with no color, no imagery, only type? what if it had to survive being printed on cheap paper?
- collecting examples of boring things done beautifully: forms, receipts, error messages, signage, packaging inserts
- making handoff documents friendlier, closer to a conversation than a spec sheet
- wondering whether a studio's values (care, plainness, patience) can live in the way it communicates and works, instead of being stated on a page
- a habit of looking at old, unglamorous interfaces and asking what they got right that newer ones forgot
- trying to describe a design purely in words, with no visuals, to see whether the idea actually holds together

**loose thoughts**

the best work usually feels inevitable in hindsight, which is misleading, because the path to it was full of detours. worth remembering when a project feels stuck: stuck is often just the middle.

curiosity is the real raw material. the more I'm interested in a client's world, the better the questions get, and the better the questions get, the less guessing the design has to do.

also worth remembering that a good explanation is part of the deliverable. people trust what they understand, and they defend what they trust. clear reasoning is how the work survives meetings I'm not in.

finally, being kind in critique, including toward my own earlier work, makes it easier to see clearly. harshness blurs things as much as flattery does.
