general working notes

approach

most design problems are communication problems wearing a visual costume. before opening any software, it helps to get the actual question out of the person asking. what are they afraid will happen if this goes badly? what would make them exhale? the brief usually answers a slightly different question than the real one, and the gap between the two is where the interesting work lives.

start loose and ugly. paper, bad marker, a text file full of fragments of sentences. the early stage is for volume of thought, not polish. polished things get defended, rough things get improved. if a direction survives being drawn badly, it's probably sturdy enough to refine.

keep scope visible to everybody involved, me included. scope creep rarely shows up as a big ask. it shows up as "while you're in there" and "could we just". worth noticing when a small request appears and naming it as a request before absorbing it, even when the answer turns out to be yes.

a studio this small can't compete on volume, so the edge is attention. notice the thing the client didn't mention, the awkward pause on a call, the reference image they keep returning to without explaining why. those clues are cheaper than any formal research method and often more honest.

pacing matters as much as talent. work that's rushed looks rushed, and work that's overworked looks nervous. there's a point where a layout has stopped improving and started merely changing. learning to feel that point is a skill, and it mostly comes from ruining a few good things by pushing past it.

process

- listen before sketching. take notes in the client's own words and keep them around, because those words make better headlines and labels than anything invented later.
- write the problem as a sentence a stranger could understand. if it takes a paragraph, the problem isn't understood yet.
- gather references widely and then throw most of them away. what remains shows taste, and taste is the thing actually being offered.
- explore in rounds that get narrower. wide and strange early, tight and practical late. mixing the stages makes everybody anxious.
- show work in context. a logo floating on white tells nothing; the same mark on a cluttered shelf, a phone screen, a tired tote bag tells a story.
- present with a point of view. a menu of equal options hands the burden of judgment to the person least equipped to carry it. lead with a recommendation and explain the reasoning in plain language.
- collect feedback as observations, not instructions. "it feels cold" is more useful than "make it blue," and the fix for cold might be spacing rather than color.
- leave breathing room between rounds. ideas that sit untouched come back sharper, and a fresh look catches the crooked alignment and the clumsy word.
- keep working files named like a kind stranger will have to read them. layers, folders, versions, all of it.
- finish by tidying: archive working versions, write a short handoff for whoever picks the work up later, and note the reasoning behind choices that aren't obvious.

working with feedback

feedback arrives wrapped in feelings, and the feelings are data too. a client who says the logo is "fine" in a flat voice is telling you something different from a client who says it's fine and leans forward. when a reaction is vague, ask what the design reminds them of. associations are easier to talk about than preferences.

when feedback contradicts itself, don't treat it as a failure of the client. it usually means there are stakeholders who haven't been in the room. a useful question is who else will have an opinion about this, and what would they say.

defensiveness is a signal to pause. if a comment stings, it's often because it's partly right. write the reply, leave it alone for a while, then reread it with the sting gone.

things to keep in mind

- being the whole studio means being designer, salesperson, accountant, janitor, and complaints desk. each hat needs its own mental space. switching without noticing is how mistakes sneak in.
- clarity beats cleverness. a clever idea that needs explaining has already lost some of its power.
- constraints are friends. a tight palette, a rigid format, a limited supply of attention: all of them force choices that a blank canvas never will.
- say what the work is not. boundaries written down prevent a surprising amount of confusion later.
- protect the maker's time. long stretches of uninterrupted focus are rare and precious, and small admin chores expand to fill whatever room they're given.
- good records are boring and invaluable. future confusion is cheaper to prevent than to untangle, so keep the paper trail tidy as work goes along instead of in a panic afterward.
- be wary of fear of silence. when a client goes quiet it rarely means displeasure; they're usually just busy. resist the urge to fill the gap with freebies or extra concessions.
- credit the work honestly. if a collaborator contributed, say so, loudly.
- watch for burnout signals: irritability at small things, avoiding the sketchbook, scrolling instead of making. rest is part of the process, not a reward after it.
- trust the nagging feeling. if something feels off in a layout, it is off, even before the reason is clear.
- remember that taste develops by looking at a lot of things, including bad ones. keep feeding it.
- not every project deserves a portfolio piece. some are just jobs done well, and that's allowed.

open-ended ideas

- a small library of reusable questions for kickoff conversations, sorted by what they uncover: fears, audiences, history, constraints, taste.
- a "failure folder" of work that didn't land, annotated with honest guesses about why. patterns might emerge that no single project could show.
- what if every project ended with a short reflection written for nobody but the studio? what worked, what dragged, what surprised.
- experiment with presenting work as a story told in sequence rather than a gallery of options. does it change how feedback arrives?
- a loose habit of making something purely for fun between projects: posters for imaginary events, packaging for products that don't exist. low stakes, high learning.
- explore how sound or motion could enter static identity work without turning into a production nightmare.
- a plain-language guide for clients explaining how the studio works, what they can expect, and where their input matters most. might cut down on repeated explanations.
- is there a way to make handoff documents feel like a gift rather than a chore? maybe a friendly tone, annotated examples, a note on the thinking.
- think about how to teach clients to see: a short walk-through of what makes type feel trustworthy or spacing feel generous.
- partnering with other small studios for overflow or specialties. the shape of that kind of arrangement is worth pondering before it's ever needed.
- keep a running list of words clients use to describe what they want. "clean," "warm," "modern," and "honest" show up constantly and mean different things to different people. a glossary of what each word tends to mean might sharpen early conversations.
- try describing a design out loud to a friend who can't see it. whatever they picture is a decent test of whether the idea is clear.

loose odds and ends

- default to saying less in messages. long emails invite long replies.
- the best critique often comes from people outside the field; they notice what insiders have stopped seeing.
- print things out. screens flatter everything.
- squint at layouts, flip them upside down, view them tiny. each trick reveals a different weakness.
- give projects memorable internal nicknames. it makes conversations easier and files more fun to find.
- a clear head is a better goal than a clear inbox.
- when stuck, change the medium. if it's stalled on screen, draw it; if it's stalled on paper, cut it out and rearrange the pieces.
- look at old work with kindness. it was the best that could be done with what was understood at the time, and the distance between then and now is the evidence of growth.
- keep a shelf of physical things that are well made: a book with perfect margins, a box that closes with a satisfying sound. they recalibrate the eye better than any screen.
- the quiet stretches between projects aren't wasted. they're where curiosity gets to wander, and curiosity is what keeps the work from going stale.
