general working notes

**approach to a new piece of work**

- start loose. before anything gets polished, get the mess onto paper. bad shapes, wrong words, arrows pointing at nothing. the goal is to find out what the work is really about, which is not always what the brief says it is about
- ask what the thing has to do for the person looking at it. pretty is a side effect of clear
- keep a running list of questions that the brief can't answer. batch them instead of trickling them out, because it reads as more considered and costs everybody less attention
- look sideways for references. architecture, packaging, old signage, textiles, the labels on stuff in a kitchen cupboard. the best ideas rarely come from other designers' portfolios
- write the problem in plain sentences before drawing anything. if the sentences are mushy, the work will be too

**process thoughts**

- work in rounds that get tighter. wide exploration, then narrowing, then polish. trouble starts when I polish during the wide stage, because then I get attached and stop seeing alternatives
- name files so that future me can read them. a vague name is a small debt that grows quietly
- keep a folder of rejected directions. they aren't waste. a direction that was wrong for this project is often the seed of the next one
- show work in context early. a logo on a blank page lies. on a shopfront mockup, a mobile screen, a tote bag, it tells the truth
- step away from the screen before judging anything. look at it small, in greyscale, upside down, from across the room. if it still holds up, good
- when stuck, change the medium. if it's stalled on screen, cut paper. if it's stalled on paper, build it in type only
- leave rough work visible for a while before cleaning it up. the rough stage often has a energy that the tidy version loses

**keeping a solo studio sane**

- I'm the designer, the admin, the salesperson, and the support desk. switching between those hats is the hidden cost. grouping similar tasks together helps the brain stop changing gears
- admin expands to fill whatever space is left for it. a small, boring system that I actually trust beats an elaborate one I resent
- protect the long stretches of quiet. creative work needs uninterrupted blocks, and notifications are the enemy
- say no to work that drags the studio away from what it's good at, even when it's tempting. a muddy portfolio attracts muddy briefs
- being solo means nobody catches my blind spots. build in ways to get outside eyes, even informal ones
- keep a "not now" list for ideas that show up mid-project. writing them down stops them from hijacking the current work
- don't let the tools become the hobby. tinkering with setups feels productive and mostly isn't

**working with clients**

- the brief is the start of a conversation, not the truth. most briefs describe symptoms
- explain choices in the client's language, not design jargon. "this typeface feels steady" beats a lecture on letter proportions
- present fewer options with a clear reason behind each rather than a wall of possibilities. too much choice makes feedback vague
- feedback is often a feeling in disguise. when somebody says "make it pop," ask what they want people to feel when they see it
- write down the reasoning behind choices as they happen. it makes later conversations calmer, and it stops the same debate from running in circles
- be kind to the version they hated. it taught both sides something about what they actually want
- respect that people are protective of their own thing. nervous clients usually need reassurance more than they need revisions

**craft reminders**

- type does most of the heavy lifting. spend more attention on spacing and rhythm than on decoration
- test colour in bad conditions: poor lighting, cheap screens, paper that soaks up ink. a palette that only works under perfect conditions isn't a palette
- constraints are friends. a limited set of shapes, a restrained range of shades, a tight grid
- contrast comes in many forms: size, boldness, density, texture, silence. white space is a deliberate material, not leftover room
- check accessibility early. legibility, contrast, how it reads for people with different eyesight. it's cheaper to build in than to bolt on
- good alignment is invisible and bad alignment is loud. nudge things until the page stops complaining
- hierarchy is the whole game. if everything shouts, nothing is heard
- handoff matters. leave files that a stranger could open and understand: layers named, styles consistent, fonts gathered, a short readme explaining anything unusual
- keep the original source of everything, and keep it somewhere that isn't the same place as the working copy

**open-ended ideas**

- a small library of my own textures, scanned from paper, fabric, rust, peeling paint, so the work carries a hand that nobody else can buy
- an ongoing side project with no client, just for play. a poster series, an alphabet, icons for imaginary objects. it keeps the eye hungry
- a process page on the studio site that shows messy early stages. people trust what they can watch being made
- a kickoff checklist short enough to actually get used. questions about audience, tone, what's off limits, what success would look like
- a swipe file organised by feeling instead of by medium: calm, loud, nostalgic, severe, playful, awkward
- constraint games for warming up: design with only straight lines, only circles, only type, only what's lying around the desk
- trade critiques with other independent designers. fresh eyes catch the habits I can't see
- write short case notes after projects while memory is warm: what surprised me, what I'd change, what I'd keep. not a report, just honest lines
- think about motion and sound even for static work. how would this identity move, how would it sound if it spoke. it often exposes weak spots
- small-run printing and hand-bound booklets as a way to let clients hold the work, since holding changes how people judge it
- a collection of "ugly but right" examples, where breaking the rules was the correct call, to remind myself why rules are only defaults
- an idea worth turning over: workshops or talks for people who are curious about how a studio like this actually runs, including the unglamorous parts

**looking after the person doing the work**

- eyes, wrists, back. stand up, look at something far away, drink water, stretch. the body is part of the toolkit
- inspiration is a habit, not a mood. go for walks, visit places that have nothing to do with design, read fiction, watch how strangers use ordinary objects
- comparing myself to large studios is a trap. it's a different game with different resources, and the solo scale has real advantages: speed, intimacy, a consistent voice
- rest is part of the work, not a reward for it. the best solutions tend to arrive when I'm doing the dishes
- keep a few things that are purely for fun and never get shown to anybody
- notice when criticism is about the work and when it's about the brief, and don't absorb the second kind personally
- curiosity is the real asset. protect it from burnout, from cynicism, and from the pressure to have an answer right away

**loose thoughts to leave here**

- good design is mostly careful attention paid repeatedly
- the boring middle of a project is where quality gets made
- clarity is a form of generosity
- if I can't explain why something is there, it probably shouldn't be
