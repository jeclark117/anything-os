general working notes

**approach**

Listen before drawing anything. People tend to arrive with a solution already formed in their heads, and the useful work is finding the problem underneath it. What is the thing supposed to do for the people who will see it? What should they feel, and what should they do afterward? The visual answer usually falls out of that conversation more easily than it does from staring at a blank canvas.

Keep early work rough on purpose. Rough sketches invite honest reactions. Polished comps invite politeness, and politeness is no help when the goal is finding the right direction. A scribble that gets argued with is worth more than a beautiful frame that gets nodded at.

Work with constraints rather than against them. A tight brief, an awkward format, a limited palette: all of it gives the work a spine. When everything is possible, nothing ends up distinctive.

Working solo means every role lands on the same desk. That's a strength for coherence, since nobody has to translate intent between departments, and a weakness for blind spots, since nobody is around to say the idea is thin. Worth building in ways to get outside eyes on things, even casual ones.

**process**

- Start with a wide, messy collection: references, words, textures, things that annoy, things that delight. Don't sort it yet.
- Write a plain description of the project in sentences before opening any design software. If the sentences are mushy, the design will be too.
- Explore broadly, then narrow with a clear reason. "I like it" is a fine start but it needs a because attached.
- Test things at the size and in the context they will actually live. A mark that looks great huge can fall apart tiny, and a layout that sings on a big screen can collapse on a phone.
- Step away between passes. Fresh eyes catch the muddy spacing and weak hierarchy that familiarity hides.
- Squint test, blur test, grayscale test. If the structure survives, the details can be trusted to carry the rest.
- Present work with the reasoning attached, not just the artifact. People respond better to a story about why than to a pile of options.
- Keep files tidy as things go: layers named, styles consistent, assets in obvious places. Future me is a stranger who didn't witness any of this.
- Document the odd choices. A short note beside a strange decision saves a lot of head scratching down the road.
- Let the work be slightly unfinished in the middle. Forcing polish too early locks in mistakes.

**working with people**

Trust gets built through small things: answering questions plainly, admitting when something is unclear, flagging problems before they turn into emergencies. Clients rarely remember the perfect file. They remember whether the process felt calm.

Explain jargon without condescension. Most people aren't confused by design; they're confused by the way designers talk about it. Translate, and check that the translation landed.

Ask for what the work needs: access, context, honest answers, timely feedback. Silence stalls everything, and asking early is cheaper than guessing.

Be honest about uncertainty. "I'm not sure yet, here's how I'd find out" is a perfectly professional sentence.

Feedback is information, not a verdict. When a reaction stings, try to translate it into the underlying need. "Make the logo bigger" often means "I can't find the thing I care about" or "I don't feel confident in this."

**things to keep in mind**

Clarity beats cleverness nearly every time. The clever idea is exciting to the person who had it and puzzling to everybody else. If a concept needs a paragraph of explanation, it might be the wrong concept, or it might need better execution. Worth asking which.

Whitespace is a tool, not leftover. Empty areas shape rhythm and give the eye somewhere to rest. Resist the urge to fill everything just because it feels like wasted room.

Type does more of the heavy lifting than most people give it credit for. Spend real attention on pairing, spacing, and the way lines break. A ragged edge or an awkward orphan quietly lowers trust.

Accessibility isn't a bolt-on. Contrast, legible sizes, clear focus states, sensible reading order. Doing it from the start is easier and makes better design for everybody, including people on bad screens in bright rooms.

Protect the boundaries of a project. Scope drifts quietly, through tiny favors and "while you're at it" requests. Notice it early, name it kindly, and keep the conversation about what the work actually needs.

Don't confuse busy with productive. Plenty of the best progress comes from thinking, walking, reading, and letting a problem sit.

Be wary of trends. They date work fast. Borrow the principle behind a trend rather than its surface look.

Look after the practice itself. Rest, admin, paperwork, and the boring maintenance all deserve attention so the creative work has room to breathe.

**on taste**

Taste is a muscle. Look at a lot, notice what pulls at you, and ask why. Keep the range wide: old posters, hand lettering, signage, packaging, scientific diagrams, album sleeves, the labels on forgotten tins. Be suspicious of personal habits, because the hands default to familiar moves. Now and then make something deliberately outside the comfort zone, even if it never leaves the folder.

**open-ended ideas**

- A small library of reusable starting points: layout grids, type scales, spacing rules, color relationships. Not templates to stamp out, more like warm-up exercises that get the thinking going.
- Writing more about process in public. Not to chase attention, but because explaining a choice out loud tends to sharpen it. Would need a format that feels natural rather than like homework.
- A running collection of favorite mistakes: things that went wrong and turned into something better. Patterns might show up.
- Constraint games for fun. Work only with type. Work only with shapes. Work with no images and no color. See what survives.
- Closer ties with people in adjacent crafts: writers, photographers, illustrators, makers, developers. A solo studio can still have a loose network of trusted collaborators. Interesting question of how to make that feel mutual rather than transactional.
- A better habit for capturing ideas as they show up in the world, whether by photographing or jotting, without overthinking the filing.
- Explore how the studio sounds, not just how it looks. Voice, tone, the way messages are phrased. Consistency in how it talks to people may matter as much as any mark.
- Borrow structure from fields that live and die by clarity: wayfinding, maps, technical manuals, recipes.
- A light habit of reflecting after a project wraps: what worked, what dragged, what's worth repeating. Keep it small enough that it actually happens.
- Think about what kind of work feels most alive, and how to attract more of it by showing it clearly rather than chasing it.

**loose bits**

- Good questions beat good answers. Keep a list of the ones that open conversations up.
- Most problems are communication problems wearing a design costume.
- Details are where trust gets built, but don't polish what hasn't earned polish yet.
- Naming things well saves hours of confusion.
- A clear no is kinder than a resentful yes.
- Keep a place for work with no audience. Play keeps the eye honest.
- Reread the brief at the end and check the work against the original intent.
- Make it easy for people to agree: plain language, obvious steps, no jargon.
- Remember why this work started to begin with.
- Leave room for happy accidents. They only arrive when the schedule has slack in it.
