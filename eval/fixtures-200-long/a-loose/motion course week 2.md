motion course week 2

working notes, general

**how i come at a piece**

start from the feeling, not the movement. before anything moves, figure out what the thing is supposed to feel like: heavy, nervous, playful, tired, certain. every choice after that gets measured against it. if a move doesn't serve the feeling it's decoration, and decoration piles up fast.

rough before pretty. block the big beats with crude shapes and blunt movement so the rhythm is readable before any polish goes on. if the rough version doesn't work, the polished version is just a nicer way of not working. rhythm is far easier to fix while everything is still ugly and cheap to change.

watch it from different distances. squint, shrink it down, watch with the sound off, then listen with the picture ignored. the bones of a piece should be legible from any of those angles. if it only works under close inspection, it's probably leaning on detail to hide a weak structure.

keep asking what the viewer is supposed to notice, and what they're allowed to miss. motion is a way of directing attention, and attention is the scarce thing.

**things to keep in mind**

- everything that moves is asking to be looked at. if everything moves, nothing is being asked for in particular.
- stillness is information. a quiet moment tells the viewer where they are in the story and gives them room to catch up.
- weight is communicated mostly by how a thing starts and stops, not by how it travels through the middle. the middle is where i spend the most effort for the least payoff.
- anticipation before an action and settle after it are what make the action believable. skip them and things feel pasted on.
- parts of an object shouldn't all arrive together. let lighter parts lag and heavier parts drag or lead, depending on what's being said about them.
- curves in the graph editor are a sketch of intent. smooth and symmetrical usually reads as generic. lopsided usually reads as a choice.
- default easing is a habit, not a decision. ask what the object would actually do given what it is and what it wants.
- consistency across a piece matters more than cleverness in any given moment. establish a motion vocabulary and stay inside it so the viewer learns the language quickly.
- context changes everything. a piece that sits at the edge of a viewer's attention needs gentler movement than a piece that owns the whole frame.
- sound and motion are the same conversation. a movement that feels slightly wrong is often fixed by moving a beat in the audio rather than a key in the animation.
- clarity beats flourish. if a viewer has to decode the movement, the movement is getting in the way of the message.

**working habits**

- keep a reference folder of real-world movement: how a person hesitates before speaking, how a door settles, how a leaf drops. real movement is always more irregular than what i'd invent.
- act it out. physically performing a gesture usually reveals the rhythm faster than staring at a timeline.
- tap out beats on a desk or hum them into a voice memo before touching any animation. it's a surprisingly good storyboard.
- name layers properly and keep the file tidy, because future me is confused and unforgiving.
- save versions generously. the take that felt too slow often turns out to be the right base once the pacing around it changes.
- step away and come back with rested eyes. pacing problems are much easier to feel when i'm not attached to the last tweak.
- when stuck, strip the piece down to a single shape and see whether the emotion still comes through. if it does, the rest is dressing. if it doesn't, the problem is upstream of the polish.

**talking about motion with people who don't animate**

- show movement early in a crude form. people react to rhythm viscerally and can't always say why.
- translate vague reactions into specifics. "it feels off" usually means too eager, too mushy, too mechanical, or too apologetic.
- words like "snappier" or "smoother" mean wildly different things to different people. ask what they want the thing to feel like instead of what they want it to do.
- keep the reasoning behind choices written down so feedback conversations don't turn into taste battles.
- show the version that's too much alongside the version that's too little. people find their preference faster by bracketing it.

**open-ended ideas**

- what would a piece look like if the most important moment were the stillest part of it? treat the hold as the hero and everything else as lead-in.
- let the sound design lead and have the picture follow it, instead of the usual reverse.
- restrict the vocabulary on purpose. what if only scale and opacity were allowed? constraint as a generator of style.
- motion that responds to the viewer versus motion that plays regardless. when does control feel like a gift and when does it feel like a chore?
- study how silent film performers and comic timing handle rhythm. the pause before the punchline is the whole joke.
- borrow from music: syncopation, rests, call and response, tension and release. maybe map a motion piece like a score before building it.
- imperfection as a tool. tiny overshoots and hand-made wobble versus perfectly mechanical movement. when does each belong, and what does each say about who made the thing?
- if a brand's personality moved, would it amble, snap, glide, or bounce? what does that reveal about its temperament that the logo doesn't?
- reduced-motion alternatives that preserve meaning instead of just switching things off. what does the calm version of a story look like, and is it sometimes better?
- build a personal library of small motion sentences: arrival, departure, emphasis, doubt, relief. reusable gestures with a consistent accent.
- look at interfaces that feel good and try to name exactly which timing choice makes them feel that way. then look at ones that feel bad and name the same thing.
- explore anticipation as a storytelling device on its own: a long wind-up that never releases, a release with no wind-up.

**pitfalls i keep running into**

- polishing before the rhythm is right.
- leaning on presets until everything has the same personality.
- confusing speed with energy. fast can be lifeless, and slow can be intense.
- adding movement to fill silence instead of letting the silence do work.
- forgetting that the end of a piece has to land, not just stop.
- falling in love with a clever transition that interrupts the thing it's supposed to connect.
- ignoring the viewer who sees it with no context, no sound, and half their attention.

**loose thoughts**

there's a tension between control and spontaneity that never quite resolves. planning gives structure, but the best moments often come from nudging something by feel and noticing that it suddenly breathes. probably the answer is to plan the skeleton and improvise the muscle.

taste for rhythm is hard to teach and easy to starve. the cheapest way to feed it is watching a lot of work and asking, each time, what the emotion was and which timing choice caused it. sometimes the answer is a bold move, but more often it's a small hesitation that nobody consciously noticed.

worth remembering that motion is a kind of acting. the object has an inner life, even if it's a rectangle. figuring out what it wants, and how badly, gets most of the way there before any keyframes exist.
