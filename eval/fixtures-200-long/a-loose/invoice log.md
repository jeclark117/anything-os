general working notes

how i like to approach things

- start by asking questions and then actually staying quiet. the brief a person hands over is rarely the real problem, it's just the surface of it. the useful material comes out sideways, in offhand remarks about what annoyed them before, what they're embarrassed by, what they wish they could say about their own business.
- keep a scrap file of those remarks. they end up steering the visual direction more than any moodboard does.
- sketch badly and early. rough marks on cheap paper beat polished screens because nobody gets precious about a rough mark. if a direction can't survive being drawn with a fat marker, it probably won't survive being refined either.
- say the idea out loud as a plain line before drawing it. if the line is mushy, the work will be mushy.
- be suspicious of anything that arrives too easily. a smooth early idea is usually somebody else's idea that i've vaguely remembered.

process, loosely

i try to think of a project as a loop rather than a conveyor belt. explore wide, narrow hard, then widen again when the narrow thing turns out to be thin. the tempting version is research, concept, polish, handoff, each stage sealed off from the next. in practice the best bits come from walking back into the research with a fresh question and noticing something that was sitting there all along.

there's also a stretch in the middle of most projects where everything looks wrong. the early excitement has faded and the end isn't visible yet. it helps to name that stretch when it arrives so it doesn't get mistaken for failure. usually it just means the work is somewhere between a rough idea and a resolved one, and the only way out is to keep making things.

habits that seem to help when stuck:

- change the medium. if it's stuck on screen, go to paper, or scissors and glue, or a photocopier that makes everything slightly worse.
- constrain something arbitrarily: a restricted palette, a restricted type family, no extra weights.
- ask what the thing would look like if it were made by somebody who hated decoration, then by somebody who loved it.
- walk away and do something with my hands that has nothing to do with design.

working solo

- nobody is around to catch the obvious mistake, so build small rituals for it. read all text out loud. look at the work upside down, in a mirror, shrunk to thumbnail size, blown up huge. each trick shows something different.
- every role is mine: designer, bookkeeper, receptionist, the person who makes the tea. switching between them costs more than it feels like. batching admin away from making keeps the making cleaner.
- admin left to pile up turns into dread, and dread leaks into the creative work. a light, steady touch on the boring stuff is kinder than a heroic cleanup after it's got out of hand.
- keep records tidy enough that future me isn't annoyed at past me.
- isolation is real. it's worth having somebody to show unfinished work to, somebody who'll say what they actually see rather than what's polite.

working with clients

- people hire a designer partly to be told no, kindly. a good no with a reason is worth more than a cheerful yes that produces mediocre work.
- show fewer options than feels generous. too many choices invite committee thinking, and the safest option wins by default. present with a point of view and explain what each option is for.
- feedback comes in disguise. "make the logo bigger" frequently means "i'm worried this looks amateur." ask what's underneath before touching anything.
- recaps after calls save arguments later. a plain summary of what was said, in ordinary language, keeps memory from drifting.
- scope creep is usually polite and incremental. notice the small asks stacking up and name them kindly rather than absorbing them in silence and resenting it.
- clients differ in their appetite for process. some want to be walked through every step, others only want to see the end. neither is wrong, and it's worth finding out which they are early.
- small projects are where trust gets built; large ones are where it gets tested. each teaches something different about how a person behaves under pressure.

craft reminders

- type does most of the heavy lifting. get the hierarchy right and most of the design is solved.
- whitespace isn't empty, it's doing a job.
- a palette should have a firm opinion. timid colors read as indecision. check things in grayscale to see whether the structure holds without color.
- test at the smallest and largest sizes the work will ever live at, and on bad screens and cheap printers.
- consistency beats cleverness. a clever flourish used inconsistently reads as a mistake.
- name files like a stranger will inherit them. nothing called final_final_really.
- leave room for the work to be used badly. people will crop it, stretch it, and drop it on backgrounds i never imagined. a good system survives that.

on taste, ego, and turning work away

taste is a tool, not an identity. as soon as i start defending a choice because it's mine rather than because it works, it's time to step back. the client's customers are the real audience, and they've never heard my reasoning. if the work needs a speech to land, it hasn't landed.

at the same time, deference isn't humility. a studio that bends to every preference ends up with nothing distinctive to offer. the trick is holding a view loosely: strong enough to argue for, loose enough to drop when something better shows up.

not every inquiry is a fit, and the signs usually show early: vague answers about who it's for, a strong attachment to a competitor's look, reluctance to talk about the actual problem. a mismatch costs more than an empty stretch does.

keeping energy up

- the work gets worse when i'm depleted, and it's hard to notice from inside. a walk, a change of room, or a different kind of task resets more than forcing through.
- inspiration is mostly input. look at things unrelated to design: signage in odd corners, packaging from grocery aisles, old manuals, handwriting, hardware store labels, hand-painted shop fronts.
- stay curious about whatever the client makes or does. the more genuine interest in their world, the better the questions get.
- rest is part of the process, not a reward for finishing it.
- comparing my work to polished portfolios of big agencies is a trap. they show the winners and hide the wreckage.

open-ended ideas to mull

- a collection of reusable questions for discovery calls, organized by what they uncover rather than by topic.
- a visual diary of rejected directions. rejected ideas often turn out to be seeds for something else.
- what would a project look like if the deliverable were a way of working rather than an artifact? leaving a client with a small system they can run themselves.
- is there a better way to show process without it feeling like a performance? sometimes sharing the mess builds trust, sometimes it just creates anxiety.
- a plain-language guide for clients explaining what happens in a project and why, without jargon.
- an experiment in constraints: building a whole identity from what's already lying around. no new typefaces, no fresh photography, just rearranging.
- documenting finished work candidly, including what didn't work, so the portfolio reflects thinking and not just outcomes.
- making the end of a project feel like an ending. handoffs fizzle. a proper close, with a short thank-you and a pointer to how the work is meant to be used, might leave a better feeling behind.
- collecting the words clients use to describe what they want ("clean", "modern", "pop") and mapping what each actually means in practice. the vocabulary gap is where a lot of misunderstanding lives.
- whether there's a version of this practice that stays small on purpose and treats that as a strength rather than a stage on the way to something bigger.
- what it would look like to teach this stuff, even informally. explaining a habit out loud often exposes how shaky it is.
