general working notes

approach, mostly. stuff i keep circling back to whenever packaging work lands on the desk.

start with the structure, not the surface. a flat layout looks like a puzzle until it's folded, so it helps to fold it in my head, or better, fold a rough paper mockup. scrap paper, a crease, some tape. paper doesn't lie about which panel faces out, which ones hide, and which edges rub against each other when somebody opens the thing.

things to keep in mind when art meets a dieline:

- the dieline is a map of cuts and folds, not part of the artwork. keep it on its own layer, clearly labeled, and make sure it can't accidentally end up in an export meant for print.
- fold lines and cut lines usually look different on the template. learn the legend before touching anything. mixing them up is the kind of mistake that only shows itself once something is physically made.
- text near a fold wants breathing room. type that sits right on a crease looks fine on screen and awkward in the hand.
- panels that meet at a corner should feel like they belong together. a pattern or a horizon line that continues across a fold is satisfying. one that breaks across it is jarring.
- orientation matters more than it seems. which way is up when the box sits on a shelf, and which way is up when it's held? sometimes those answers differ, and the design should know which one it's serving.
- keep a margin of safety inside the trim for anything important. logos, small type, and thin rules all get nervous near an edge.
- the inside of a box is real estate too. it's easy to forget until someone opens it and finds a blank, unconsidered void.

process thoughts, loosely ordered:

i like to begin with a pile of words rather than images. what is this thing supposed to feel like when it arrives? quiet? loud? expensive in a restrained way, or friendly in an almost silly way? write the words down, cross out the ones that feel generic, keep the weird ones. the weird ones are usually closer to the truth of the client's brand than the polished adjectives they use in meetings.

then thumbnails, tiny and ugly. the point is to test composition on a folded form without getting precious. if a layout can't survive being drawn badly, it probably isn't structurally strong.

after that, move to the actual template and let the real proportions push back. what looked good as a sketch often needs to bend once it meets the true dimensions of each panel. that's fine. the template is the boss.

color deserves its own quiet pass. screens glow, paper absorbs. a color that sings on a monitor can go muddy on uncoated stock, and a deep tone can swallow detail. ask the printer what the sheet will actually be, if that hasn't been settled, and let that shape the palette before getting attached to anything. proofs are worth the effort. a physical proof catches things no amount of zooming ever will.

on working with a printer:

- they're a collaborator, not a vendor to be managed. the people who run the machines know what ink does on a fold, what coatings crack, and which fine details won't hold. ask questions early and without ego.
- keep a running list of questions instead of sending them one at a time. it's kinder to their inbox and it forces me to think through the whole job at once.
- when a file is handed back with notes, read every note, even the ones that look boilerplate. the boring ones are often the ones that save a reprint.
- save the version of the template exactly as received, untouched, and work on a copy. always. being able to compare against the original has rescued me more than once.

on client communication:

clients often respond better to something they can hold than something they can scroll. a flat print folded by hand, even rough, changes the conversation. suddenly they're talking about how it feels to open instead of whether the blue is the right blue. that's a better conversation.

try to explain constraints as opportunities, not obstacles. "this panel is narrow" becomes "this panel is a good place for a single bold gesture." framing matters, and it genuinely makes the work better, not just the mood of the meeting.

also worth remembering that clients rarely see the dieline at all. they see the finished object. so the logic of the flat layout is my problem to solve quietly, and their experience should feel effortless.

open-ended ideas, unfiltered:

- what if the unfolding of a box were part of the design? a message that only reads fully once the thing is opened flat, a sort of reward for the curious.
- there's something interesting about treating the hidden panels as a secret layer of the brand. a pattern nobody sees on a shelf but everybody discovers at home.
- could the dieline's own geometry become the pattern? the cuts and creases already form a kind of drawing. leaning into that shape, instead of covering it up, might give the packaging an honest, architectural feel.
- texture without ink. embossing, blind debossing, a coating that only catches light at an angle. restraint can be louder than color.
- think about the sound of a box. the snap of a lid, the whisper of a flap sliding in. nobody briefs for sound, but people remember it.
- what happens to the box after the product is gone? does it get kept, reused, flattened, tossed? design for the afterlife a little, even if only in spirit.
- a modular system, where related boxes share a grid so they stack into a larger image on a shelf. could be gimmicky. could be lovely. worth sketching before judging.
- materials that age well. some printed surfaces scuff and get uglier, others scuff and get character. hard to predict, but worth asking about.

habits worth protecting:

keep a folder of boxes and packaging that made me stop and look. not for copying, for noticing why. was it the proportion, the paper, the restraint, the single unexpected detail?

leave room in the schedule of my own head for the idle looking-around stage. it feels unproductive and it's actually where the strongest ideas surface.

name files in a way that future-me can decode without effort. versions pile up quickly, and a tidy trail beats a heroic memory.

step away from the screen when something feels stuck. fold the paper again. often the problem is spatial, and the hands solve it faster than the eyes.

last thought, nothing to act on: the best packaging feels inevitable, as if the shape and the surface were always meant for each other. that feeling usually comes from a lot of unglamorous checking behind the scenes, and a willingness to let the structure lead.
