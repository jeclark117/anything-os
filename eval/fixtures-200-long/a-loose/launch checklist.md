launch checklist

working notes, general

mostly a place to think out loud about how i like to work when a project is getting close to the end. not rules, more like habits i keep drifting back to.

**approach**

- make the rough version early and look at it. a messy draft on screen teaches me more than staring at a blank canvas ever does.
- treat the client's goals as the brief and my taste as the tiebreaker. when that gets flipped, the work turns precious and the feedback turns awkward.
- keep the scope honest. most trouble in this line of work comes from vague edges, not hard problems.
- being a studio that's just me means there's no separate reviewer. so i have to build in ways of seeing the work with fresh eyes: step away, change the window size, print it, read the copy out loud, squint at it until the details blur and only the hierarchy is left.
- say the uncomfortable thing early. it's almost always cheaper to raise a concern in conversation than to fix it after everything is built.
- prefer fewer, better choices. every extra option in an interface is a small tax on whoever has to use it.

**process, loosely**

it usually starts with listening. not just to what people say they want, but to the words they use when they're describing their own work. those words end up in the headlines, the navigation labels, the little bits of microcopy. if the site sounds like them, half the design job is already finished.

then references, but with discipline. collecting inspiration is fun and also a trap. the useful kind is specific: i like how this page handles a long list, i like the spacing here, i like that this button doesn't shout. the useless kind is a folder of pretty screenshots that all say nothing in particular.

sketches stay loose and cheap, on paper when possible. the point is to find the shape of the thing, not to impress anybody. once the shape feels right, i move into the browser as early as i can stand it, because layouts behave differently when they're live and real text pushes against real edges.

real content matters more than almost anything. placeholder text hides problems. a headline that fits perfectly in lorem ipsum will break the moment it becomes an actual sentence written by an actual person. so i push for real copy, real images, real names, even if they're rough drafts.

and then the long middle, where i refine, remove, and refine again. removing is underrated. a lot of the best moments in a project are when i delete something and nothing is lost.

**things to keep in mind near the end**

- the finish line is where small things pile up. polish is mostly just noticing.
- people use things in messy conditions: weak signal, bright glare, thumbs only, half paying attention, a cracked screen, a tired brain. design for that person, not the ideal one sitting at a big desk with perfect lighting.
- the unglamorous pages are the ones people actually hit. error messages, empty states, confirmation text, the page that appears when something breaks. these deserve as much care as the homepage, maybe more, because they show up at the moment someone is already a little frustrated.
- trust is quiet and fragile. a link that goes nowhere, a message that says thanks when nothing was sent, a page that jumps around while it loads, a button that looks pressed but did nothing. each of these costs more goodwill than any clever animation earns.
- keep a record of what was changed and why. future me will not remember, and neither will the client.
- leave the client able to maintain things without me. handoff is part of the design, not an afterthought stapled on at the end.
- accessibility isn't a layer to add on top. contrast, focus states, sensible heading structure, text that can be resized, labels that mean something. if these are built in from the start they cost almost nothing, and if they're retrofitted they cost a lot.
- speed is a design feature. heavy images, too many fonts, scripts nobody remembers adding. people feel slowness even when they can't name it.
- check the thing on a device that isn't mine, in a setting that isn't mine, with hands that aren't mine. asking a friend to poke at it without any instructions is wildly informative. watch where their cursor hesitates.

**on working with clients**

people tend to be nervous near the end of a project, even when it's going well. they've lived with it for a long time and suddenly it's about to be public. a little reassurance goes a long way, and so does showing them what to expect, what normal looks like, what a minor hiccup looks like versus a real problem.

it helps to explain the why behind choices in plain language. not a defense, just context. most disagreements dissolve when both sides understand what a decision was trying to protect.

also worth remembering: feedback about a feeling is more valuable than feedback about a solution. "this feels cold" is a gift. "make the blue bigger" is a guess at a fix. i try to ask what's behind a request before reaching for the mouse.

**open-ended ideas**

- a personal library of small checks, organized by what tends to go wrong rather than by which part of a site it touches. failures cluster in patterns, and the patterns are more memorable than the parts.
- a short reflection template for after a project wraps: what surprised me, what i'd skip next time, what i'd lean on harder. keep it short enough that i'd actually fill it in.
- try recording a quick screen walkthrough for handoff instead of writing a long document. people might actually watch it. maybe both, with the video as the front door.
- build up a bank of realistic sample content, awkward on purpose: very long names, very short names, missing images, text in alphabets i can't read, strings with odd punctuation. a stress kit for layouts.
- a "pre-mortem" habit: imagine the project has gone badly, then list why. it's strange how easy that is, and how many of the reasons can be addressed while there's still room to move.
- explore whether a quiet stretch after launch, spent just watching how people use things, teaches more than any amount of testing beforehand.
- a constraint exercise: what would this design look like if images failed to load? if only the keyboard worked? if the text were twice as large? good design usually survives all of those.
- trade critiques with a friend in the field. not for approval, just for a fresh pair of eyes that knows what good looks like.
- keep a running list of things that annoyed me as a user in the wild. annoyance is a decent compass, and it fades fast if i don't write it down.
- think about how a studio like mine could be more legible to the people who might need it. a clear way of describing the work, a few honest case notes, a plain explanation of how a project unfolds. less pitch, more transparency.

**small reminders**

- rest matters. tired eyes miss misaligned things and tired judgment misses bad ideas.
- good work doesn't need to be loud.
- when stuck, change the medium. sketch instead of typing, type instead of drawing, walk instead of sitting.
- the goal isn't a perfect launch. it's something that holds up when real people touch it, and that is easy to fix afterward when it doesn't.
- be kind to the version of me who will inherit this folder later. name things clearly. leave breadcrumbs.
