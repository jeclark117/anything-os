webinar notes

general working notes

**approach**

- the work is only as good as the shared understanding behind it. most trouble in past projects traced back to assumptions nobody said out loud, not to disagreements about taste.
- clarity beats cleverness, in the work and in the emails around the work. if a sentence can be read in a way i didn't intend, eventually it will be.
- a studio run by a lone person means the client experiences a human being, not a process. warmth is easy to give. consistency takes more deliberate effort.
- show rough work early and in a plain state. polished presentations invite people to judge details before the direction has settled.
- ask more questions at the beginning than feels comfortable. the awkwardness is brief and the savings are large.

**process thoughts**

discovery tends to go best when the conversation starts with what the business is trying to change rather than what it wants made. people often arrive with a solution already in mind, like "we need a refresh," when the real problem is that nobody can tell what they do at a glance. listening for the problem underneath the request is most of the job. useful questions: what would be different if this went really well? what have you tried that felt wrong, and why did it feel wrong?

direction. keep early exploration deliberately wide, then narrow using the client's reactions rather than my own preferences. reactions are data. a client who says "too busy" and a client who says "too loud" might be pointing at the same thing, and it's worth asking before assuming.

feedback. it helps to separate what people feel from what people prescribe. "this feels cold" is more useful than "make it blue." translate prescriptions back into feelings before acting on them. a feedback format that asks for the reaction, the reason, and the worry, in that order, might be worth sketching out.

revisions. tie each round of feedback back to the original goals. if the goals haven't changed, the work shouldn't swerve. if the goals have changed, that deserves to be named honestly instead of absorbed quietly.

welcome packet for new clients
set expectations in writing

files and handoff. name things so a stranger could find what they need. keep working files apart from delivered files. a short plain-language readme with anything delivered, explaining what's inside and what's meant to be edited versus left alone, helps the client and whoever works on the project after me.

**things to keep in mind**

- scope creep rarely arrives as a big ask. it arrives as small favors, each reasonable by itself. notice when a favor starts becoming a pattern.
- clients don't know what they don't know. things that feel obvious to a designer, like why files come in different formats or why feedback from many voices has to be gathered into a stream, are invisible from the other side. explain without condescension.
- silence is not agreement. a quiet client may be happy, confused, busy, or politely unhappy. a light check-in costs very little.
- the tone of a message sets the tone of the relationship. warm and direct works. over-apologizing for things that are simply part of the process doesn't.
- a calm, predictable rhythm of communication matters more than speed. people forgive slowness they were warned about. they rarely forgive surprises.
- a no said early and kindly is a gift compared with a yes that quietly turns into resentment.
- be plain about what rights come with the finished work, and keep that language readable rather than legalistic.
- people with strong opinions often have strong reasons. dig for the reason before arguing with the opinion.
- good work can still land badly if the client hasn't been brought along. the story of how a decision was reached matters almost as much as the decision.

**working alone**

- isolation is the hidden cost of running a studio by myself. there's nobody across the desk to say "that's not working." find ways to get outside eyes: a trusted peer, a critique group, even reading the work aloud to an empty room.
- keep a swipe file of work that makes me envious or curious, and write down why, not just what. the why is the useful part.
- watch for the urge to over-deliver as a way of soothing anxiety. extra work given out of nerves rarely lands the way it's meant to, and it sets a standard that's hard to maintain.
- rest is part of the process. some of the best solutions arrive while doing something unrelated.
- keep the admin light enough that it actually gets used. a tidy system nobody maintains is worse than a messy one that works.
- the lack of a boss is freeing and a little dangerous. without outside structure, it's easy to let the loudest request set the priorities instead of the most important one.
- comparison is a trap. other studios show their best work and hide their mess. my own mess is not evidence of failure.

**learning from webinars and talks**

- most talks have a small handful of ideas worth keeping, and the rest is framing. capture those ideas and translate them into the studio's own words, otherwise they stay as borrowed language that never quite fits.
- be wary of advice built for larger agencies. process that suits a team with project managers and account leads can smother a studio run by a lone person.
- pay attention to the questions the audience asks. they often reveal what practitioners are really struggling with more honestly than the slides do.
- separate "interesting" from "applicable." plenty of good ideas don't fit, and that's fine.
- notice which advice is about craft and which is about business. they get mixed together a lot, and they age differently.

**open-ended ideas**

- a short plain-language glossary for clients covering the terms designers use casually that clients may quietly not understand: vector, raster, bleed, kerning, lockup, comp.
- a visual way of showing the stages of a project, so clients can see where they are without having to ask. a simple map rather than a schedule.
- a "what good looks like" exercise at the start, where the client collects examples of work they admire and work they dislike, and explains each reaction in a sentence.
- a lightweight reflection after each project, jotted while memory is fresh: what surprised me, what i'd explain differently, what the client seemed to value that i hadn't expected.
- a way of recording the reasoning behind design choices so clients can defend them internally when somebody in their organization questions them. rationale as a gift to the client's internal politics.
- a small library of example emails for the awkward moments: late feedback, unclear feedback, feedback from a surprise stakeholder, a request clearly outside the scope, a client who has gone quiet.
- worth wondering how to talk about taste without it becoming a contest. maybe framing choices in terms of audience and purpose so personal preference has less room to take over.
- what if the end of a project included a short note about how to look after the work: how to use it, how to resist the urge to tweak it, and when it might be worth revisiting.
- worth wondering whether reading is how every client prefers to absorb information. some might respond better to a short recorded walkthrough or a conversation.
- a running list of questions clients have asked that i didn't expect. patterns in those questions probably point at gaps in how the work gets explained.
- a habit of writing down what a project felt like from the client's side, not just from mine. the gap between the two is where most of the learning sits.

**loose closing thoughts**

- good clients are usually made, not found. clear structure and generous communication tend to bring out the best in people who might otherwise be anxious or controlling.
- the relationship is part of the product. the work can be excellent, but if the experience felt chaotic, the chaos is what gets remembered.
- trust accumulates through small, boring reliability: replying when expected, doing what was described, flagging problems early and plainly.
- stay curious about the client's world. the better i understand how their customers think, the less the work depends on guesswork.
- design is a service to a purpose, not a performance. when the ego gets loud, go back to the question of who this is for.
- not every problem is a design problem. sometimes the honest answer is that the thing needing attention is somewhere upstream of anything i can make.
- the best notes are the ones that stay useful when reread cold. write for the version of me who has forgotten the context.
