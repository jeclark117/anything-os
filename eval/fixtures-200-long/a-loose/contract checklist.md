general working notes

loose thoughts on how the studio tends to work. mostly here so there's something to skim when a project feels muddy.

**approach**

- understand the problem before touching visuals. it's tempting to open a blank canvas straight away because that feels like progress, but the projects that wobble are usually the ones where the gap between what the client needs and what they asked for never got examined. those rarely match.
- write the problem out in plain language, as if explaining it to a friend outside the industry. if the explanation is clumsy, the thinking is clumsy, and polish downstream won't rescue it.
- keep a running list of questions instead of guessing. a question asked early is cheap. an assumption discovered late is expensive.
- show rough work early. people react more candidly to something that looks unfinished, because they don't feel obliged to be polite about it.
- look for the real subject underneath the request. a logo request might really be about trust. a layout request might really be about getting people to stop feeling lost.

**process**

the rhythm that seems to work is gather, explore, narrow, refine, hand off. the trouble is that it never stays that tidy. exploration bleeds into refining, and gathering keeps going right up to the end because new information shows up unannounced. rather than fight that, it helps to treat the stages as moods rather than gates. wandering back into exploration doesn't mean something went wrong.

while gathering, collect reference material widely and then be a little ruthless. a folder stuffed with images turns into noise. pull out what each reference is actually doing: the spacing, the restraint, the way the type behaves, the relationships between colors. then let go of the reference and keep the observation.

while exploring, aim for range. push past the obvious direction, because the obvious direction is what the client already imagines. make some directions deliberately uncomfortable. even if they're rejected, they mark the edges of the space and make the middle feel chosen rather than default.

when narrowing, name the criteria out loud. what is this supposed to feel like? who is looking at it, where, and how closely? a design that sings on a large screen may collapse in a tiny browser tab or on a small printed label. test against the harshest context, not the friendliest.

refining is where little things pile up: alignment, rhythm, contrast, the awkward gap between a heading and whatever follows it. step away and come back with fresh eyes. view the work upside down, view it small, view it in grayscale. problems that hide in plain view show up when the way of looking changes.

for handoff, package things so a stranger could open the files and understand them. clear naming, labeled layers, notes on why choices were made. future me counts as a stranger too.

**type, color, space**

- type does most of the heavy lifting. before reaching for decoration, see whether hierarchy, spacing, and a thoughtful pairing of typefaces can carry the idea.
- color is relational. a shade looks different depending on its neighbors, so judge it in context, never as a lonely swatch.
- whitespace is a material, not leftover space. crowded work usually signals that nobody decided what mattered most.
- when a layout feels off and the reason is elusive, check alignment and rhythm first. the cause is almost always that structural.
- restraint reads as confidence. a limited palette and a small set of type choices usually look more intentional than a buffet.

**working with clients**

- people are rarely bad at feedback; they're bad at the vocabulary. "make it pop" usually means something specific hiding underneath. ask what they were feeling when they said it.
- separate taste from goals. personal preference matters, but it's not the same as whether the work does its job. steer conversations back to the goal, gently.
- explain the reasoning along with the visuals. work presented with a rationale is much harder to dismiss with a shrug.
- be wary of a crowded review loop. feedback from a crowd sands everything down into something safe and forgettable. it's worth learning early who actually gets to say yes.
- silence isn't approval. a client who goes quiet may be confused, busy, or politely unhappy. check in plainly.
- people trust a process they can see. showing how the work moves from rough to refined makes surprises less likely.

**critique**

- critique the work, not the person, and that includes yourself.
- when feedback stings, wait out the sting and then look for the kernel of truth. there's usually one, even in clumsy comments.
- ask "what is this doing?" before "do I like it?" the second question is mostly about taste, and taste is a poor measuring stick.
- a good critique leaves the work with more options, not fewer.

**things to keep in mind**

- the brief is a starting point, not scripture. it can be wrong, and noticing that is part of the job.
- constraints are friends. limited palettes, limited typefaces, tight formats: they force choices that make the work sharper.
- consistency beats cleverness. a flourish that appears in just a spot and nowhere else looks like an accident.
- accessibility isn't a bolt-on. contrast, legibility, and clear hierarchy are part of what makes something good, not a layer added afterward.
- save energy for the choices that matter. agonizing over a minor detail while the overall structure is shaky is a classic trap.
- keep track of where inspiration came from, so that borrowing never quietly becomes copying.
- jot things down while they're fresh. the reasoning behind a choice evaporates fast.
- good work is often quiet. not everything needs to announce itself.

**working solo**

working solo has its own hazards. nobody across the room says "that doesn't look right," so it helps to build substitutes: a habit of explaining the work out loud, a trusted outside reader, a pile of past projects to compare against. isolation can also make taste drift without anybody noticing, so look at things from outside the field regularly: architecture, packaging, old posters, signage, textiles, whatever pulls the eye.

creative energy isn't linear. some stretches are generative and others are better suited for admin and cleanup. matching the task to the energy saves frustration. forcing a breakthrough when everything feels flat rarely works, and the dull necessary chores are a fine use of a flat stretch.

it's also easy to let the studio's own materials fall behind because there's always something more urgent. a tidy set of templates, a clear way of naming files, and a habit of leaving breadcrumbs for later all pay off quietly.

**open-ended ideas**

- a personal library of small studies: color combinations, type experiments, grid sketches made with no client attached. a place to play that feeds the paid work.
- a reflection habit at the end of a project: what felt smooth, what felt painful, what would be handled differently. a short, candid paragraph is enough.
- a plain-language guide for clients explaining how the process works, so expectations get set before the awkward moments arrive.
- a collection of before-and-after comparisons showing how rough ideas became finished work. could be useful for explaining the process to newcomers.
- a deliberately ugly draft ritual: make the worst version on purpose to get the fear out of the way.
- constraint exercises: design something using only shapes, or only type, or only a restricted palette, and see what survives.
- a vocabulary list for feedback, with words that translate vague reactions into direction. warm, loud, crowded, hesitant, brittle, sleepy.
- walking away from the screen: sketching on paper, rearranging printed pieces on a table, looking at layouts from across the room.
- a swipe file of well-solved problems organized by problem rather than by style, so it answers "how do I handle this situation" instead of "what looks nice."
- imagining how a brand would age. what does it look like when it's tired, stretched, miscolored, printed badly? designs should survive being mistreated.
- ending a project with a small artifact that shows the thinking, like a short note about how to use the work well.
- what would it look like to design for the person who sees the work for a split second, distracted, on the way to something else? probably simpler than expected.

**small reminders**

- clarity before cleverness.
- ask the awkward question while it's still easy to ask.
- rough is fine until it isn't.
- rest is part of the process, not a break from it.
- the work should be able to explain itself.

- scope matches the proposal
- payment terms
- usage rights
