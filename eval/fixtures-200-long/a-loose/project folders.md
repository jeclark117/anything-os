general working notes

**approach**

- listen more than I talk at the start. the real problem is rarely the one named in the opening message, and it tends to leak out sideways, in some offhand remark about what the thing is supposed to feel like
- collect the feeling words people use before the practical words. "calm", "confident", "a bit cheeky" end up steering more choices than any spec does
- get something ugly onto the page fast. a rough thumbnail, a scribble, a blobby layout. ugly is cheap, and cheap is honest
- ask what would make the whole thing a failure, not just what would make it a success. the answers are always more specific
- keep early rounds loose enough that nobody is precious yet, including me

**process thoughts**

Most of the craft is deciding what to leave out. When a layout feels stuck, the fix is usually subtraction rather than another clever addition. The urge to add a flourish is worth treating as a smell. It often means the underlying structure isn't carrying its weight.

Work in broad strokes before fine ones. Type, spacing, rhythm, and hierarchy get sorted out in grayscale with placeholder text well before colour or imagery gets a say. If it holds together in black and white with nonsense words, it will survive real content. If it only works once the pretty photograph drops in, it doesn't really work.

Use real content as early as it can be had. Placeholder text hides problems. A headline that runs awkwardly long, a name that won't fit, a caption nobody has written yet: these break layouts, and it's better to meet them while the layout is still soft.

Step away from the screen. Print something, pin it up, walk across the room and squint at it. Hierarchy problems that hide at full zoom become obvious from a distance. Flipping the work upside down or mirroring it helps too, because composition flaws can't hide once the content stops being readable.

Keep a running scrap of "things that bugged me." Nothing formal, just a loose list. Patterns show up: the same awkward spacing situation, the same type pairing that feels forced, the same habit of centering everything when I'm unsure.

Name files for the person who will open them in a panic. Descriptive beats clever, and anything called "final" is a small lie waiting to happen. Future me is basically a stranger with a deadline and no memory of what I was thinking.

**things to keep in mind**

- clients are rarely wrong about how something feels and frequently wrong about how to fix it. take the feeling seriously, hold the proposed solution lightly
- a request for "pop" or "more energy" is a request for contrast somewhere. find where contrast is missing before reaching for brighter colour
- explain the reasoning along with the work. a clear rationale makes feedback better, because people react to the argument rather than just to taste
- feedback that begins "I don't know, it just feels off" is still useful. ask what it reminds them of
- consistency beats cleverness in systems. if a rule needs an exception every time it's used, it's a bad rule
- leave breathing room. whitespace isn't wasted space, it's what makes everything else audible
- accessibility isn't a layer added at the end. contrast, legibility, and sensible reading order belong to the design from the start
- working as a studio of just me means nobody else catches my blind spots. build habits that stand in for another pair of eyes: fresh looks after a break, checklists, reading copy out loud
- tiredness makes everything look acceptable. if all the options suddenly seem fine, that's a sign to stop, not a sign of success
- constraints are a gift. a tight palette, a limited set of typefaces, a rigid grid: these remove the paralysis of infinite choice

**talking with clients**

Plain language wins. Jargon makes people nod politely and then privately worry. Showing a rough idea and saying what it's for does more than a paragraph of design theory.

Frame choices as options with trade-offs, not as a menu of equally good answers. Presenting a spread of alternatives with no opinion attached is a way of dodging responsibility. Having a point of view, and being willing to say why, is the actual service.

Silence after sending work is normal and not necessarily bad news. People are busy and often need to sit with something before they know what they think. Resist the urge to fill the quiet with apologies or unrequested revisions.

When feedback contradicts itself, it usually means the underlying goal wasn't pinned down. Go back to the feeling words instead of trying to satisfy every comment literally.

Say no gracefully to things that don't fit, and say it early. A clear "that isn't really what I do well, but here's what might help" builds more trust than a shaky yes.

**energy and craft**

Some of the best ideas arrive while doing something unrelated: washing up, walking, staring out of a window. Protect the empty space where that happens.

Look at things that have nothing to do with the current job. Old packaging, hand-painted shop signs, municipal forms, hardware store labels, children's books. The unglamorous stuff teaches more about clarity than award annuals do.

Make things by hand regularly, even badly. Cut paper, ink, clay, a wobbly stamp carved from an eraser. It keeps the eye honest and reminds me that digital tools are only one way of making marks.

Taste is built by noticing why something works, not just that it does. Pull apart work that moves me and name the specific moves: the scale jump, the odd margin, the restrained colour, the unexpected word choice.

**open-ended ideas**

- build a personal library of textures scanned from everyday objects: worn paper, scuffed metal, fabric, cracked paint
- try designing something using only a handful of basic shapes and see how far the constraint stretches
- keep a "wrong answers" sketchbook of deliberately terrible ideas, since the awful ones sometimes point toward the good ones
- write a reusable set of kickoff questions that dig under the surface request
- put together a plain-language explainer about how a project unfolds, aimed at people who have never commissioned design before
- sketch to music without lyrics and see what shapes it suggests
- swap tools for a spell: pencil only, cut paper only, a borrowed typewriter, anything that changes the rhythm of making
- walk around unfamiliar neighbourhoods just to photograph signage and lettering
- ask of every digital piece: what would this look like as a physical object, and what would that change?
- jot down what surprised me when the dust settles on a piece of work, in a loose and unpolished way, and see whether themes emerge
- explore how much meaning a layout can carry with almost no words
- invite a friendly outsider, maybe a writer or a carpenter or a cook, to react to work in progress and say what they see, with no design vocabulary allowed

None of this is a rulebook. It's more a pile of reminders to reread when the work gets muddy and the best next move isn't obvious.
