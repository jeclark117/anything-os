working notes, loose and unfinished

**approach**

Most of the good work comes from asking slightly annoying questions early. Not hostile ones, just the kind that make a brief say what it actually means. "Make it pop" is almost never the real request. Underneath it is usually something like "people don't trust us yet" or "we look like we belong to a different era." Worth digging until the real worry shows up, because that's what the work is for.

Being small is part of the appeal. The person who talks to you is the person who does the work, so nothing gets lost in a handoff. That's worth protecting, even when it would be easy to blur it.

Rough work shown early beats polished work shown late. Polished work leaves people with only yes or no. Rough work invites them to think alongside me, and they usually end up more invested in the result.

Taste matters, but it isn't the same as preference. Taste is knowing why something works. Preference is just liking it. When the two get tangled, the work gets fussy.

**process, roughly**

- words before pictures. Write the sentence the design is supposed to say. If it can't be said plainly, no layout will rescue it.
- gather references from far outside the field. A bakery's identity often looks best when it borrows from architecture or textiles rather than from other bakeries.
- build the system before the pages: type, spacing, color, and some rules about when it's fine to break them. Pages come quickly after that.
- keep early sketches ugly on purpose. Ugly sketches are easy to throw away, which makes bolder moves possible.
- step away before sending and come back cold. Coming back cold catches what enthusiasm hides.
- write down why choices were made, in plain language. Explaining them later gets much easier, and it protects good choices from being quietly undone by a passing comment.
- name files and layers as if a stranger will inherit them. Future me is a stranger.

**things to keep in mind**

People rarely remember exactly what was said during a project. They remember how it felt. Calm is a feature, and so is clarity about what happens next.

Scope creep is usually not sneaky. It's people discovering what they want by seeing things. Treat it as information about the brief rather than as an annoyance, even when it does need a conversation.

Feedback often arrives as feelings, and it helps to translate. "Too busy" can mean "I don't know where to look." "Too plain" can mean "I'm nervous it won't impress anybody." The fix for each is different.

Work I can't care about shows. It's worth noticing that early, before it turns into a grind.

Admin eats the same energy that thinking needs. Protecting the thinking isn't laziness.

Working solo means nobody says "that's not working" unless I arrange for somebody to. Outside eyes aren't a luxury. A trusted peer, a patient friend from outside the field, even a printout pinned to a wall and glanced at while making tea.

Failure modes worth recognizing: perfectionism dressed up as care, over-explaining out of nerves, hiding in software instead of thinking, agreeing to things just to be liked, and letting a good idea sit unshared because it feels too small.

**on clients and communication**

- Say the awkward thing kindly and early. It rarely gets easier by waiting.
- Short, plain messages. Questions at the end, clearly marked, so they don't get buried.
- Show work with a bit of context: what problem it solves, what feedback is wanted, what is deliberately unfinished.
- Be careful with jargon. Specialist words can sound like gatekeeping even when they're meant as precision.
- People relax when they can see the shape of the process, even if the details change.
- Notice who actually holds the power to say yes and who simply has opinions. Each matters, in different ways.

**keeping the whole thing sustainable**

A studio this size lives or dies on energy more than on skill. Skill is mostly there; energy is the variable. Noticing what drains and what refills is a kind of project management.

Deep work likes a quiet room and a clear head. Admin likes a loud playlist. Matching the task to the mood saves effort.

Boundaries around availability are a kindness to all concerned, including the person on the other end, who gets a clearer sense of what to expect.

Keep a record of kind words from clients somewhere findable. Hard days need evidence.

Learn from what went well as carefully as from what went badly. It's easy to only autopsy the failures.

Curiosity about a client's business is the cheapest advantage a small studio has. Big agencies can't always afford to care about the details of a niche. A small place can.

Money talk is easier when it's treated as part of the craft rather than a separate awkward thing.

**open-ended ideas**

- Write up how a project really went, dead ends included, as a case study that isn't a victory lap. Honest process writing is rare and probably more useful to the right readers than polished showcases.
- Go and look at old work out in the world. See how signs fade, how packaging scuffs, how layouts hold up after other hands have been editing them. Weathering teaches things that screens can't.
- A pre-mortem habit: imagine the project went badly and ask why, before it begins.
- A discovery questionnaire that doubles as a way for clients to clarify their own thinking, so the questions are useful even if nothing else happens.
- Limits as a game: only the plainest typeface, only black on plain stock, only what can be drawn with a ruler. Constraints tend to produce personality.
- A private collection of patterns noticed in how organizations describe themselves versus how their customers do. The gap is where the interesting design lives.
- Collaborating with a writer, photographer, or developer whose temperament is very different. A loose circle of people to trade favors with, who might also say "have you thought about..."
- A place for unfinished ideas that isn't a guilt pile. Something like a drawer rather than a to-do list.
- Explaining critique to non-designers: how to react to work, what to look at, what questions to ask. Might work as a handout.
- Recording short voice notes after meetings, while the mood of the conversation is still fresh, because mood is what gets lost.
- Making things for no reason: posters for imaginary events, packaging for products that don't exist. Pure practice, no audience required.

**questions I keep coming back to**

- What would this look like if it were braver?
- What can be removed without anybody noticing?
- Who is this really for, and are they in the room?
- What will this look like when it's tired and worn and nobody is looking after it?
- What does the client already have that they're undervaluing?

**small reminders**

Good design is mostly attention. Notice the spacing, notice the word choice, notice what the person asking is not saying.

Whitespace isn't empty. It's doing a job.

Consistency beats cleverness more often than expected.

If an idea needs a long explanation to defend it, it might be the wrong idea, or it might just need a better presentation. Hard to tell until it's tried out on somebody else.

The best portfolio pieces tend to come from projects where the client was brave. Bravery in a client is worth recognizing and encouraging, gently.

Rest isn't separate from the work. Some of the best solutions show up while walking, washing up, or staring out of a window.
