general working notes

approach

mostly i try to understand the problem before i draw anything. sounds obvious, but the pull to open the software and start pushing shapes around is strong, and shapes always feel productive even when they're answering a question nobody asked.

- read the brief, then read it again hunting for what's missing instead of what's there
- ask what the work should make people feel before asking what it should look like
- write the problem in plain words. if i can't say it plainly the design will probably be muddy too
- gather reference loosely, not to copy, just to find the edges of the territory and spot the open space
- sketch on paper before the screen, badly and fast, with a thick pen so fussy details can't creep in
- figure out who the work is really for and what they're doing when they bump into it. a poster seen from across a street is a different animal than a screen held a hand's length away

process

the middle of a project is where things drift. a loose rhythm helps: explore wide, narrow down, then polish. the trap is polishing too early, because a polished idea is hard to abandon even when it's wrong. a rough sketch is easy to throw away, while a glossy mockup makes everybody fall in love with it.

- keep explorations deliberately ugly until the direction is clear
- make the bad version on purpose. it shows what the good version needs
- name files so future me can decode them without context. "final-final-real" is a warning sign
- save versions generously. storage is cheap and regret is not
- when stuck, leave the screen. walking beats staring almost every time
- squint at the work, flip it upside down, shrink it to a thumbnail, strip the color. if it still holds together the structure is sound
- check contrast and legibility early rather than as an afterthought
- build with real material as soon as possible, because placeholder text lies about how a layout behaves
- let the grid serve the work and break it when the work asks, but know why

working with feedback

feedback is usually about a symptom, not the cause. "make the logo bigger" often means "i'm not confident in this yet". digging for the feeling underneath saves a lot of pointless nudging.

- separate taste from function. taste is negotiable, function mostly isn't
- don't defend the work in the moment. write the reaction down, let the sting fade, respond with a clear head
- when presenting, start with the story of the problem so the solution lands as an answer instead of an opinion
- a tight set of options beats a wall of them. a wall hands the labor of choosing back to the client
- vague praise is as useless as vague criticism. ask what specifically is working
- occasionally the best response to a note is a question

things to keep in mind

- scope creep is quiet. it arrives as "while you're in there" and leaves as a tangle. learn to hear the phrase
- clear edges around what's included make everybody more relaxed, me included
- good work often comes from good constraints: a limited palette, a limited choice of typefaces, a fixed format
- coherence is a kindness to the end user. spacing, alignment, naming, behavior, all of it feeling like it came from the same hand
- accessibility isn't a separate pass. it's part of what "works" means
- say less in the layout. whitespace is a choice, not leftover room
- be wary of trends. borrow lightly or the work ages right along with the trend
- the client knows their world better than i do, and i know how to translate it. respect that split
- tired eyes make bad kerning calls. rest is part of the craft
- ask for the reason behind a request before reacting to the request itself
- good enough to ship and good enough to be proud of are different bars, worth knowing which one is being asked for

being on my own

working solo means nobody to bounce ideas off, so it helps to build substitutes.

- talk the problem through out loud, even to an empty room
- write a short summary of the project as if explaining it to a stranger. gaps show up immediately
- keep a running file of dead ends so a forgetful future self doesn't wander back into them
- swap critique with other independent designers, informal and low pressure
- keep a swipe file of things that made me stop scrolling, with a note on why

the hardest part of working solo isn't the workload, it's the lack of friction. nobody pushes back, so weak ideas survive longer than they deserve to. building in friction on purpose, a stranger's opinion, a deliberately hostile reading of my own work, keeps things honest.

admin and handoff

- take notes right after a call while the details are fresh, including what was asked and what was answered. it helps when memories differ
- tidy files at the end: labeled layers, sensible folders, a short explainer of why things are the way they are
- write down the reasoning behind choices as they're made, because rationale evaporates fast
- leave the project in a state where a stranger could pick it up
- a polite, clear message beats a clever one

where ideas come from

inspiration mostly comes sideways. the best ideas rarely show up while staring at other designers' portfolios. they show up in weather, textiles, old maps, hardware stores, the worn labels on things people use constantly. looking at what isn't design tends to feed design more than looking at design does.

open-ended ideas

- a personal project with no client, purely to practice a skill client work rarely allows
- a library of textures, scans, and found lettering photographed while out and about
- publish rough process notes, a way of thinking in the open
- try designing with a deliberately tiny toolkit just to see what survives
- experiment with sound or motion even though it's outside the usual lane
- a small set of reusable starting points: a proposal outline, a questionnaire for new clients, a handoff checklist
- what would the studio look like if it made its own products instead of only serving others? worth turning over
- show a work-in-progress to a stranger for an initial glance and ask what they see
- hand-lettering practice to loosen the wrists
- revisit old projects and ask what i'd do differently. a decent gauge of growth
- team up with an illustrator, photographer, or writer on a self-initiated piece
- keep a "wrong answers" folder: ideas that failed but had a spark worth stealing from later
- study signage in odd places, packaging, instructions, handwritten notices, and ask why they work or don't

questions worth carrying around

- what is this really for
- who meets it, and where
- what can be removed
- what would make this feel inevitable instead of arbitrary
- where is the work pretending to be something it isn't
- what am i avoiding looking at

and when in doubt, make the thing simpler and see what's missing.
