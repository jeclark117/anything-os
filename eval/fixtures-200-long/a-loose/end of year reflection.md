end of year reflection

general working notes

**approach**

- the brief is usually a symptom. the real problem sits underneath, in how people describe what they're nervous about. listen for that more than for the stated goals.
- pay attention to the words a client keeps reaching for without realizing it. those words are often the identity already, just waiting for someone to draw it.
- working alone means decisions don't get diluted, which is great. it also means nobody pushes back on a bad instinct. pushback has to be manufactured: let work sit and come back with fresh eyes, explain it out loud to an empty room, show it to a trusted outsider who owes you nothing.
- taste is a filter, not a plan. it's good at telling you what to throw out and useless at telling you what to make.
- the best work tends to come from constraints that were chosen on purpose, not constraints that just happened to show up.
- a good project has a spine. if the whole thing can't be explained in a sentence a stranger would understand, the spine is missing and no amount of polish will hide it.

**process**

- sketch on paper before touching the screen. rough marks keep ideas loose. once something looks finished it gets defended, and defended ideas are hard to improve.
- write down the opposite of what the project is meant to be. sometimes that clarifies the target better than describing the target does.
- gather references widely, then throw most of them away. the keepers are the references that still make sense after the initial excitement wears off.
- work in rounds of wide and narrow. wide means lots of rough directions and no judgment. narrow means picking the direction that holds up under questioning, then pushing it until it breaks, then fixing where it broke.
- keep a running file of dead ends. rejected directions are often exactly right for a different project, and it's painful to rediscover them from scratch.
- present work with the reasoning attached. a design shown without a story gets judged on taste alone, and taste is where disagreements turn personal.
- when feedback arrives, sort it into reactions, preferences, and problems. reactions are information about feeling. preferences are information about the person. problems are information about the work. only problems are something to fix directly; the rest are something to understand.
- tidy files pay off later: named layers, sensible folders, little notes on why a choice was made. future-me is a stranger who wasn't in the room.
- finishing is its own skill. the last stretch of any project is mostly small, unglamorous corrections, and it's where the difference between good and almost good lives.

**things to keep in mind**

- energy is part of the budget. a project that drains more than it gives back costs more than any invoice shows.
- watch for scope creep that arrives disguised as friendliness. "while you're in there" is a phrase worth noticing the moment it appears.
- vague feedback usually means the question was vague. better questions early save a lot of confusion later.
- the people paying and the people using are often different, and the work has to serve both without pretending they want the same thing.
- silence from a client isn't approval. it's often confusion, or a worry that hasn't found its words yet.
- be wary of work that looks great in a portfolio but didn't solve anything. be equally wary of the reverse: work that solved everything and looks like nothing, and still deserves to be shown properly.
- enthusiasm at the start of a relationship is cheap. how someone behaves when a disagreement shows up is far more informative.
- being a studio of a person means being the designer, the bookkeeper, the salesperson, and the cleaner. admin leaks into creative space unless it gets boundaries of its own.
- rest isn't wasted. plenty of problems get solved while doing something unrelated, like washing dishes or staring out a window.
- notice what makes you proud when nobody is watching. that's a better compass than praise, and a better compass than a full calendar.
- confidence and certainty are different things. it's fine to present with confidence while still holding the work loosely.
- a client who respects the process usually respects the result. a client who treats the process as an obstacle usually treats the result as a commodity.
- saying "i don't know yet" out loud is underrated. it buys room to think and tends to build more trust than a quick, shaky answer.

**open-ended ideas**

- what would a project look like if it were designed backwards from the feeling someone should have when they put it down, or close it, or walk away from it?
- a personal library of small experiments not tied to any client, just to keep the hands moving and the curiosity alive. could be ugly on purpose.
- short postmortems for every project, even the good ones, written in plain language with space for what stayed unclear. the good ones might teach more because nobody interrogates them.
- is there a way to describe how the studio works so the right clients recognize themselves and the wrong ones quietly opt out before anyone wastes effort?
- an early question for any conversation: what would you be a little embarrassed to admit about your goals? people often warm up when invited to be honest.
- a collection of phrases clients use, grouped by what they usually turn out to mean. "make it pop" is a whole genre.
- trading critique with other small independent makers. outside eyes without outside politics.
- make something purely for the pleasure of it and see which instincts show up that never get room in commissioned work.
- how much of the process could be made visible to clients without overwhelming them? transparency can build trust, or it can create noise. probably depends on the client.
- what would a quieter studio feel like? less output, more depth? or is that a comforting story told to avoid pushing harder? worth sitting with before believing either answer.
- tools matter less than the habits around them. maybe the interesting question is which habits are doing quiet damage, not which software to swap.
- what does a good handover look like? what should a client be able to do on their own afterward, and what should stay a conversation?
- some work wants to be slow. is there a way to protect that kind of project from the pace of everything else around it?
- the best compliments are usually specific. it might be useful to collect those, and notice what they have in common.

**loose thoughts**

reflection is only useful if it's honest, and honesty gets harder when the person asking and the person answering are the same. a good trick is to write as if explaining things to a friend in the same line of work: no spin, no defensiveness, just what happened and what it felt like.

it's easy to confuse being busy with being on track. busy is a feeling. on track is a direction. sometimes the clearest sign of drift is that everything feels urgent and nothing feels chosen.

also worth remembering that small studios live and die on reputation, and reputation is mostly built from how people feel while working together, not only from what gets delivered. the work matters enormously, but so does the experience around it.

and finally, don't over-learn from a single bad experience or a single great one. patterns show up across many projects, and the real lessons tend to hide in the boring middle, not in the dramatic extremes.
