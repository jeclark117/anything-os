lettering practice

general working notes

**approach**

- start loose and ugly. the early marks are never the point, they're just a way of finding out what the letters want to do
- look at shape before meaning. a word is a cluster of rhythms, gaps, and weights long before anybody reads it
- keep a messy pile of rejects. the discards teach more than the keepers, and sometimes a rejected sketch turns out to be right for a completely different project
- trust the hand for rough ideas and the screen for refinement. the hand is quicker at being wrong, which is useful
- when stuck, change the tool, not the idea. a different pen, a bigger sheet, a worse brush. the constraint usually shakes something loose
- try to describe the feeling of a brand in plain words before drawing anything. warm, stubborn, quiet, a bit cheeky. then check every sketch against those words rather than against my own taste

**process notes**

Most projects go better when the early conversation stays long and the early drawing stays short. The temptation is to jump to polished options because they look impressive in a message. But polish creates attachment, and attachment makes honest feedback harder for everybody, including me. Rough thumbnails keep the conversation about direction instead of about kerning.

A rough order that tends to work, though it's not a rule:

- gather the mess: references, old materials, things the client already loves or hates, even if they can't explain why
- sketch wide, with no editing. fill pages. the obvious ideas come out early and need to be exhausted before anything interesting shows up
- narrow by squinting. blur the eyes, step back from the desk, look at the page upside down. whatever still reads as a shape survives
- build the survivors properly, at large scale, then shrink them hard to see what breaks
- test in context: on a dark background, on a light one, tiny, huge, cropped, reversed, stamped, embroidered, scratched into something. a mark that only works in ideal conditions isn't finished
- write up the reasoning in plain language so the client can defend the choice to their own colleagues when I'm not in the room

**things to keep in mind**

practice brush lettering on slow days
useful for logo work

- the counters and the gaps matter more than the strokes. space is half of what people see and nearly all of what they feel
- optical balance beats mathematical balance. a perfectly centered thing often looks off, and a nudged thing looks right
- hand-drawn quirks are an asset only when they're deliberate. an accidental wobble reads as sloppy, an intentional one reads as personality, and the difference is usually confidence
- consistency inside a mark matters more than correctness against any rulebook. if the terminals all behave the same way, the eye forgives a lot
- keep the mark simple enough to draw from memory. if somebody can't sketch it on a napkin, it probably won't survive being reproduced badly, and it will be reproduced badly
- small studios win on attention and speed of thought, not on scale. lean into being a person with opinions rather than pretending to be an institution
- say no to work that needs a different kind of studio. a poor fit costs more than an empty calendar
- rest the eyes. fatigue makes everything look acceptable, which is the most dangerous state to be in when judging spacing

**on feedback**

Feedback usually arrives as a feeling disguised as an instruction. "make it pop" or "make it more modern" or "it needs more energy" are all symptoms. The useful move is to ask what they were looking at when the feeling showed up. Often it's one letter, one curve, one awkward gap, and the rest of the design was fine.

It helps to separate three kinds of comments: things that are wrong, things that are different from what they pictured, and things that are simply unfamiliar. Wrong gets fixed. Different gets discussed. Unfamiliar often just needs a little exposure, and a day of living with a new mark changes how it looks. Not every comment deserves a revision, but every comment deserves a real question.

Also worth remembering: people judge a logo by the first impression and then by how it behaves in the wild. Show it in the wild early, on a mockup of their actual surfaces, so the conversation shifts from "do I like this" to "does this do its job."

**loose ideas, not plans**

- a library of favorite letterforms, drawn from memory rather than traced, to see which features stick in the mind and which blur away
- an exercise of drawing the same word in a dozen moods without changing the tool. aggressive, tender, bored, formal, sleepy. see how much the rhythm alone can carry
- collecting hand-made signage and notice which mistakes are charming. wonky shop signs, hand-painted menus, chalkboards, labels on jars. there's a lot of accidental wisdom in places nobody designed on purpose
- building a small set of "bad habit" sketches: the typical mistakes in spacing, weight, and contrast, drawn exaggerated, as a quick self-check
- trying a project where the logo is drawn with the wrong hand, or with eyes mostly closed, just to find out what the loosened grip does to the structure
- writing a plain-language guide for clients on how to look at a mark, so feedback gets sharper without making them learn jargon
- exploring how motion changes a mark. does it still feel like itself when it moves, fades, builds stroke by stroke? some marks come alive in motion and some fall apart
- pairing lettering with texture: ink bleed, dry brush, pressed paper, and seeing how much of that can survive being cleaned up into something scalable
- keeping a running list of words that are annoying to letter. certain letter combinations always fight back, and knowing them early saves grief
- finding out whether a mark can be recognized by its silhouette alone, with all interior detail removed

**questions worth sitting with**

- when does refinement stop improving a mark and start sanding off what made it interesting?
- how much of a studio's style should show up in client work, and how much should disappear entirely?
- is a mark better when it explains itself, or when it leaves a little mystery for people to fill in?
- what makes some old, imperfect marks feel more trustworthy than perfect new ones?
- how do you keep curiosity alive on work that's routine, without turning routine work into a playground?

**small reminders**

keep the sketchbook open on the desk, not in a drawer. keep a cheap pen within reach, because expensive ones make the marks too precious. look at things that have nothing to do with design: handwriting on receipts, cracked paint, the shape of shadows on a wall. step away when the work feels muddy, and come back with fresh eyes, since half of the problem is usually fatigue and the other half is overthinking.

and remember that most of the craft is just paying attention for longer than is comfortable.
