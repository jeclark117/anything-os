client t

general working notes

**approach**

- start from how a place feels, not how it should look. people usually reveal the feel when they describe their space without trying to sell it. listen for the adjectives they keep reaching for, and the ones they push away unprompted.
- small studios run on trust and word of mouth, so a site is less a brochure and more a handshake. it has to answer the nervous question a newcomer carries: will i fit in, will i look silly, is it okay that i'm not flexible?
- keep scope honest. a small business site can sprout arms and legs fast: booking, shop, blog, memberships, video library, newsletter. treat each of those as its own conversation rather than an assumed extra.
- find out how things work right now. there's often a paper sign-up sheet, a group chat, a spreadsheet somebody quietly loves. whatever already works is a clue to what the site should absorb and what it should leave alone.
- ask who maintains the thing after it's built. a site that needs a developer to change a class description will rot. a site the owner can edit between teaching sessions stays alive.

**voice and words**

the biggest risk with any small business site is that it ends up built around the designer's taste instead of the owner's voice. the words matter as much as the layout. many owners are more comfortable talking than writing, so it can help to record them describing what they do and pull phrases from that. the copy that comes out sounds like a person rather than a template, and that's most of what makes a site feel trustworthy.

watch for the clichés of the category. lotus icons, sunsets, words like journey and transform and unlock. they're fine if the owner genuinely talks that way, and hollow if they don't. plain, warm sentences usually beat poetic ones, especially for people who are already a bit anxious about walking into a new room.

also worth remembering that a wellness site is partly a promise about atmosphere. the tone of the microcopy (button labels, confirmation messages, error states) carries as much of that promise as the hero image does. a harsh error message on a booking form undoes a lot of careful calm.

**visuals**

- photography makes or breaks this kind of site. stock images of flawless people in impossible poses on a cliff put visitors off and signal that nobody cared. real photos of the real room, the real teachers, the real mats, even slightly imperfect, do more work than polished fakes.
- natural light in the actual space is worth pushing for if there's any way to arrange it. so are candid shots of people mid-laugh, mid-stretch, mid-wobble.
- calm does not mean empty. restful design is easy to confuse with sparse design, and sparse can slide into vague. generous spacing, a quiet palette and gentle motion all work, but only with clear hierarchy and obvious buttons.
- pale sage on cream looks lovely and is hard to read. wellness brands love it. check contrast early, before everybody gets attached to a palette that fails.
- motion should be slow and optional. respect reduced-motion settings, since some visitors are dealing with sensitivities that a drifting parallax effect will make worse.

**practical stuff**

people look up a studio on a small screen, often right before they decide to go, standing somewhere else entirely. so the practical bits (where it is, what's on, how to book, what to bring, what to wear) should be reachable without scrolling through atmosphere. design the small screen first and let the larger layout be the generous version.

the "what's on" information is the part that changes most, so it deserves the most thought about who updates it and how. if updating it is a chore, it will go stale, and a stale timetable is worse than none because it sends people to a locked door.

think about what happens when the internet is patchy. light pages, careful image handling, and fonts that don't block the whole screen while loading. a calm brand loses its calm when the page jumps around.

**process notes**

- gather inspiration from outside the category, not just other studios. ceramics, fabric, book covers, packaging, hand-painted signs from the neighbourhood. the owner's taste shows up more honestly in the things they like that have nothing to do with their business.
- write the structure as plain sentences before any visuals. if the page can't be explained in words, the layout won't rescue it.
- sketch on paper, then look at the sketch from across the room in bad light. if the hierarchy survives, it will survive a small screen.
- build the type and colour system early and keep it small. fewer decisions means more consistency and an easier hand-off.
- show rough work early. busy owners react better to a rough thing than to a description of a polished thing.
- leave room for the owner's discomfort. if a design makes them wince, find out why before defending it. the wince is usually information about their audience that they can't quite articulate.
- keep the feedback loop narrow. too many opinions from friends, teachers and relatives flatten a design into something nobody chose.

**open-ended ideas**

- a "what to expect" page written like a friendly walkthrough from the front door to the mat, with real photos at each step.
- a short, optional greeting from a teacher on the home page, silent by default so nobody gets startled.
- a quiet "new here?" path that stays visible no matter where you are on the site.
- teacher pages that show style and personality, so people can choose a person and not just a class type.
- a home page that shifts gently to reflect what's happening in the studio, so it never looks abandoned.
- a journal section only if somebody genuinely wants to write. an empty blog is worse than no blog.
- weigh embedded booking against linking out to a booking tool. embedded feels seamless but can be brittle. linked out is sturdy but breaks the mood. worth testing with real users, not guessing.
- a printed companion piece, like cards or a poster that echo the site, because studios live in physical space and the website should feel like part of that space.
- a gentle way to ask for feedback from regulars, which doubles as a source of honest copy.
- an honest accessibility note about the space itself (stairs, changing rooms, quiet corners), since that's often the real barrier and rarely gets mentioned.

**questions worth keeping in the back pocket**

- what do the regulars say when they recommend the place to a friend?
- what do people get wrong about the studio before they walk in?
- who do they not want to attract?
- what would make them embarrassed to point at the site?
- who else has a say: partners, teachers, a relative who "knows computers"?
- what do they already own? logo files, photos, domain, accounts, and who holds the logins?
- what do they dread about having a website at all?

**housekeeping**

- keep notes on voice separate from notes on visuals. they drift apart otherwise.
- record the reasons behind choices, not just the choices, so the thinking can be revisited without starting over.
- write assumptions down where they can be checked. most project trouble starts with an assumption nobody said out loud.
- running a solo studio means being designer, writer, emailer and bookkeeper all at once. protect space for the actual design thinking, because admin will happily eat all of it.
- stay curious about the owner's world. the best details usually come from noticing small things about how they run their space, like how they greet people, what's on the shelf by the door, and what they apologise for.
