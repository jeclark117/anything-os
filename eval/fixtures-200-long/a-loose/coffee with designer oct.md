general working notes

Trying to keep the studio's process boring so the work itself doesn't have to be. Boring meaning predictable: clear handoffs, no surprises about what happens next, nobody guessing. All the energy goes into the thinking and the making.

**Approach**

Hear people out before proposing anything. Folks rarely arrive with the real problem. They arrive with a symptom, or a request for a specific deliverable that sits well downstream of what's actually wrong. The job at the start is mostly archaeology.

Questions that usually open things up:
- What would success look like for the people who use this, not just the people who sign off on it?
- What have they already tried, and what felt off about it?
- What would make them wince if it showed up in the final work?
- Who else has a say that hasn't come up yet?
- What are they quietly worried about?

That last question gets the best answers, usually after a pause.

Keep a running scrapbook of references, but write a reaction next to each. "I like this because it feels calm" is worth more than a pile of screenshots with no explanation. The reaction is the transferable part.

Prefer principles over rules. A brand or interface built on a principle ("make the next step obvious") can flex in situations nobody planned for. A pile of rules breaks as soon as reality shows up.

**Process**

Loose shape, bends to fit each project:
- Gather and hear people out
- Sketch wide, on paper where possible, ugly on purpose
- Narrow toward a direction worth testing
- Build something real enough to react to
- Refine, and polish only what has earned it
- Hand off cleanly, with notes that a stranger could follow

Early sketching is where the best ideas live because nothing is precious yet. Resist opening the polished software too soon. Crisp pixels make everything look finished, and finished-looking work stops people from questioning it.

When showing work, give context. Never just drop a file in an inbox. Explain what problem it's addressing, what to look at, and what to ignore for now. Ask for reactions rather than opinions about details. Rough work is allowed to be rough. A prototype with a clear question attached beats a polished mockup with no question.

Stepping away helps. Leave a layout alone, come back with fresh eyes, and the obvious flaw is suddenly obvious. Walks count as work.

**Working with feedback**

Feedback comes in disguises. "Make the logo bigger" often means "I don't feel seen in this." "It feels off" often means the structure is fine but a detail is nagging. Separate the stated request from the underlying worry, then solve the worry. Sometimes the request, taken literally, would make things worse, and the kind move is to show why without lecturing.

Defensiveness is the enemy. When work is criticized the urge to explain is strong. Better to jot down the reaction, say thanks, and think it over away from the conversation. The best response is rarely the quickest.

Keep a record of what was tried and rejected, so the same idea doesn't circle back wearing a new outfit.

**Things to keep in mind**

- Scope creep rarely announces itself. It arrives as "while you're at it." Worth having a warm, easy way of naming it without making the relationship feel tense.
- Being a solo studio means every role lives in my head: designer, bookkeeper, salesperson, janitor. Switching hats badly is where quality slips.
- Putting things in writing protects against selective memory, mine and theirs. A short recap after a conversation removes a surprising amount of friction.
- Saying no is part of the craft. A studio with a clear point of view attracts work that fits. A studio that says yes to everything slowly turns into a generic service.
- How a service is framed shapes what people think they need. Presenting what a studio offers is a design problem like any other, and it deserves the same care as a logo or a layout.
- Clients are frequently more nervous than they look. Being a calm presence is part of the service.
- Taste is a muscle. Look at things deliberately, including things outside design: architecture, signage, old manuals, the way shops arrange their shelves.
- Be wary of trends adopted because the rest of the field adopted them. Borrowing is fine. Borrowing without understanding why something worked is where work goes generic.
- Accessibility and legibility aren't extras. Treat them as part of the material, like grain in wood.
- Keep files and naming tidy. Future me is a stranger who has never seen this project.

**Communication**

Plain language wins. Jargon creates distance, and distance creates mistrust. If a concept can't be explained without design vocabulary, it probably isn't understood well enough yet.

Be clear about what's needed from the other side and what happens when it doesn't arrive. Projects stall more because of waiting than because of difficulty.

Share bad news early and with a suggested path forward. Silence feels worse than any update.

Watch the energy in a conversation. When a person goes quiet after seeing work, that's data. Ask gently what's sitting with them.

**Rhythm and energy**

Creative work doesn't arrive at a steady pace. There are stretches where everything flows and stretches where nothing does. Planning for both, with padding for the stuck stretches and protection for the flowing ones, beats pretending output is constant.

Guard the stretches when thinking is sharpest for the hard problems. Leave admin for when the brain is flat. Variety helps too. Switching between making, thinking, and tidying keeps any of them from going stale.

**Quality checks**

Before anything leaves the studio, a mental walk-through:
- Does it solve the problem that was actually named?
- Does it still make sense squinted at, shrunk down, or seen in poor light?
- Is the hierarchy obvious without explanation?
- Would a stranger know where to look?
- Are the details consistent: spacing, alignment, naming, the little repeated things?
- Is anything included purely because it was fun to make?

That last question isn't an instruction to cut it. Sometimes the fun part is the heart of the piece. It's just worth knowing which is which.

**Open-ended ideas**

- A library of reusable intake questionnaires, so onboarding feels personal without being reinvented for every project.
- A habit of jotting a short reflection after wrapping up a project: what surprised me, what dragged, what I'd steer differently. Could work better as a loose practice than a formal ritual.
- Experimenting with sharing process in public, rough stages included. Could build trust, could also invite backseat driving. Tradeoffs worth thinking through.
- A loose circle of other independents (writers, developers, photographers, illustrators) where referrals and questions flow naturally. Peers facing the same oddities frequently have clever workarounds.
- A personal project with no client at all, purely as a playground for odd techniques that later leak into paid work.
- Designing the studio's own materials (proposals, invoices, onboarding documents) with the same care as client work. They're frequently the most-read things the studio produces.
- Thinking about what the studio is known for rather than what it can do. Those are different, and the gap is interesting.
- Explaining the process out loud, in writing or to a friend, to expose its weak spots.
- A small archive of past work with candid notes about what worked and what didn't.
- Finding ways to make long projects feel lighter for the client, such as small visible wins along the way.
- Wondering whether constraints could be offered as a feature: a narrow focus, a distinctive method, a signature way of working.

**Questions to sit with**

- What kind of work gives energy back, and what kind quietly drains it?
- Where does the process rely on willpower instead of structure?
- Which parts of the work would be missed if they disappeared, and which would be a relief to lose?
- What does a good relationship with a client look like when the project is over, not just in the middle?
- How much of the studio's reputation rests on the work, and how much on how it felt to work together?

Not everything here needs resolving. Some of it is just a place to keep thoughts until they ripen.
