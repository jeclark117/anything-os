general working notes

approach

the work usually goes better when it starts from the problem rather than the look. a good frame tends to pull the visuals along with it, and a bad frame can't be rescued by polish. so before opening any design software, it helps to write the brief in plain language. if it can't be said plainly, it probably isn't understood yet.

- ask what the thing needs to do for the people who will use it, then what it needs to say about the people who made it
- find the constraint that matters most and let it shape the rest. a tight constraint is often a gift
- sketch badly and fast before sketching well. the ugly sketches are where the surprises are
- keep a running list of questions for the client instead of asking them as they arise, so the questions arrive as a set and are easier to answer
- try to name what a project is not, as well as what it is. the edges clarify the middle

working alone has its own rhythm. there's nobody to bounce things off, so the process has to supply its own friction. that means leaving gaps between stages, looking at work with fresh eyes, and being willing to show unfinished things to somebody trusted before they feel ready.

process

the loose shape is: listen, gather, explore, narrow, refine, hand over, reflect. it's rarely that tidy, and stages bleed into each other, but the sequence is a useful default.

listening is the stage most likely to get squeezed. clients often describe a solution when what they have is a problem. "we need a new logo" might really mean "people don't understand what we do." worth sitting with the first request and asking what's underneath it before accepting it as stated.

gathering is references, existing materials, competitors, and anything that shows the context. it's easy to over-collect here. a small, sharp set of references beats a huge mood board that points in every direction. a good test is whether each reference can be explained in a sentence. if not, it's decoration.

exploring should feel wide and slightly irresponsible. the point is to find out what's possible, so it's okay to chase ideas that will never ship. some of the best final directions started as a joke or a mistake.

narrowing is where taste and judgment do the work. useful questions:
- does this still serve the problem from the brief?
- would it survive being seen small, in grayscale, or in a hurry?
- is it distinctive because it's right, or just because it's odd?
- can the client explain it to somebody else without me in the room?

refining is craft: spacing, rhythm, alignment, the small decisions nobody consciously notices but everybody feels. it's slow, and it's easy to rush because the big idea already exists. resist that. the details are what separate something competent from something good.

handing over deserves more care than it usually gets. files organized, naming sensible, a short guide for how to use things and how not to. the goal is that the client can carry on without needing to call, even though they probably will.

reflecting means looking back at what worked and what dragged. a few honest lines written down right after a project ends are worth more than a vague memory later.

things to keep in mind

- clarity beats cleverness nearly every time. cleverness is a bonus when it's earned
- feedback is information about a reaction, not an instruction. "make it pop" means something is flat. work out what, rather than literally adding pop
- be wary of the urge to polish early. a polished wrong idea is harder to abandon than a rough one
- clients rarely have the vocabulary for what they like. show options and watch their faces instead of asking them to describe
- say no to the work that would need a different studio to do well. it's kinder than doing it badly
- rest matters. the eye gets tired and starts forgiving things it shouldn't
- keep the boring parts of the business boring. clean records, clear paperwork, tidy folders. that way creative energy goes into the creative work
- good boundaries with clients are a design problem too: scope, expectations, and communication all benefit from deliberate shaping
- write things down. ideas that feel unforgettable in the moment are gone by the time they'd be useful

communication

a lot of trouble in projects comes from mismatched expectations rather than bad work. a few habits that seem to help:

- restate what was heard before responding to it
- send a short recap after any conversation that included decisions, so there's a shared record
- present work with the reasoning attached. the same layout lands very differently with and without the story behind it
- be plain about what stage the work is in so nobody mistakes a rough direction for a finished piece
- when bad news has to be delivered, deliver it early and with a suggestion attached

tone matters as much as content. warm but not gushing, confident but not defensive. a client who feels respected will tolerate a surprising idea far more readily.

open-ended ideas

- a personal library of "almost" work: directions that didn't fit a project but might fit another. could be a source of fresh starting points
- a small ritual for closing out a project, maybe a short written note to self about what was learned
- experimenting with constraints as a warm-up: limited palette, limited type, limited shapes, just to see what survives
- making a habit of studying work outside the field, like architecture, textiles, signage, packaging, old manuals. the best borrowing comes from far away
- building a reusable questionnaire for early conversations that draws out the real problem without feeling like a form
- writing short process pieces for the studio's own site, not as marketing but as a way to think out loud
- finding a peer or two to trade critique with, even informally. a studio of one still benefits from outside eyes
- exploring how motion, sound, or physical materials might enter projects that are currently purely visual
- keeping a "bad ideas" file on purpose. sometimes the reverse of a bad idea is the good one
- considering how to make the studio's own identity less of an afterthought. the cobbler's children problem is real

a note on taste

taste develops by looking a lot and noticing why something works or doesn't. it's worth being specific in private: not "that's nice" but "the spacing under the headline gives it room to breathe." that kind of naming builds a vocabulary, and a vocabulary makes decisions faster and easier to defend.

it also helps to keep an eye on one's own habits. every designer has defaults, favorite moves, comfortable palettes. they're useful until they become a rut. now and then, deliberately do the opposite of the instinct and see what it teaches.

a note on sustainability

the studio only works if the person running it stays interested. that means protecting some space for curiosity, for side experiments, for work that's fun and not just useful. burnout in a small studio doesn't announce itself; it shows up as indifference, and indifference shows up in the work.

it also means paying attention to what kind of work gives energy and what drains it, and noticing patterns. if a certain type of project keeps leaving things feeling flat, that's data. if another keeps sparking ideas well past its scope, that's data too.

finally, keep the whole thing in proportion. design matters, but it's rarely life or death. a calm, curious, slightly playful attitude tends to produce better work than anxiety ever does.
