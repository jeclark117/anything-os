general working notes

approach

- start from the feeling, not the layout. before opening any file, write down the words that describe how the thing should land when a person picks it up. warm, a little uneven, quiet, slightly playful. those words become the filter for everything that follows.
- treat the system as the real deliverable. a pile of lovely pieces that don't talk to each other is worse than a modest set that clearly belongs together. think in families: what stays constant, what shifts to signal a difference.
- keep the physical constraints in view while sketching. paper, ink, finishing options, and the shape of the packaging decide what is possible, and ignoring them early just means redoing work later.
- sketch on paper before the screen. screens make everything look finished too soon. rough pencil work leaves room to be surprised.

process

i tend to work in loose passes instead of straight lines. a pass for mood and references, a pass for rough structure, a pass for detail, then back around again when the pieces start to disagree with each other. the point is to keep the early passes cheap so nothing feels precious and nothing gets defended just because it took effort.

references help most when they come from outside the category. a good source of ideas is old shop signage, hand-lettered price cards at a market stall, seed packets, apothecary jars, stamped shipping crates, the handwriting on a recipe card. the goal isn't to copy any of it, just to absorb why it feels trustworthy. usually it's because a person clearly made decisions with their hands and didn't over-correct.

when a direction starts to take shape, build a rough physical mockup as early as possible. paper folded and taped, a label printed on a home machine and trimmed with scissors. holding something changes opinions fast. proportions that looked right on screen often feel wrong in the hand, and type that seemed generous turns out to be cramped once it wraps around a curve.

keep a running list of questions that only the client can answer, and bring them together instead of trickling them out. it respects their attention and makes the conversation feel more like a collaboration than an interrogation.

show work in context whenever possible. a label floating on white tells very little. the same label on a shelf, next to other things, under warm light, tells the truth.

type, color, texture

- pick a typographic voice that can carry a lot of different names without strain. some letterforms look great with short words and fall apart with long ones, and product names vary.
- hand-drawn elements work best when they are restrained. a single drawn detail does more than a page of them. too much and it turns into costume.
- color should do two jobs: make the family feel unified and make each member findable at a glance. a shared base with a changing accent is the usual route, but it's worth testing whether the reverse feels fresher.
- texture is part of the message. uncoated stock, visible fibers, slightly soft edges, ink that sits a little unevenly. these cues carry more warmth than any illustration can.
- test everything at actual size and at a distance. a design that only works when viewed up close will disappear on a shelf.
- remember that print rarely matches the screen. soft colors drift, fine lines thicken, and light tints can vanish. build in margin for that.

things to keep in mind

- warmth and polish pull in different directions. the trick is deciding where precision matters (alignment of the important information, legibility, consistent spacing) and where looseness is welcome (borders, ornaments, small marks).
- handmade qualities are easy to fake badly. random wobble applied evenly looks mechanical. real imperfection has a logic to it: pressure builds, ink pools at corners, a stamp lands slightly crooked and nobody minds. study how the real thing behaves and borrow the logic.
- the more the system relies on variation, the more important it is to define the rules that hold it together. write them down, even informally, so decisions stay consistent when energy is low.
- legal and practical text always arrives late and always wants more room than expected. leave space for it from the start, and design it as part of the piece rather than an apology at the bottom.
- think about the unboxing as a sequence, not a snapshot. what is seen first, what is felt next, what is smelled, what is kept afterward. packaging that earns a second look at the end is rarer than packaging that wins on arrival.
- accessibility isn't a bolt-on. contrast, type size, and clear naming matter for everybody, especially on a shelf in dim light.
- be wary of cleverness that needs explaining. if a detail only works after a paragraph of rationale, it probably isn't working.
- save every version, including the ones that look wrong. the discarded direction often contains the exact detail that rescues the favorite.

open-ended ideas

- a system of small stamps or marks that can be combined, so each variant gets its own character from the same set of parts. it keeps the family coherent while letting each member feel individual.
- the inside of the packaging as a quiet surprise: a pattern, a handwritten-style line, a texture that only shows after opening.
- a way to let the maker's hand show up in the printed piece, like a signature mark, a thumbprint-style stamp, or a deliberately imperfect border that was truly drawn rather than generated.
- naming the variants with a consistent grammar, so the words on the labels feel as designed as the visuals around them.
- a label shape that echoes the object it sits on, following a curve or a fold instead of fighting it.
- ink and paper pairings that change slightly across the family, the way a seasonal catalog might, without breaking the overall unity.
- tactile cues for the sake of touch alone: a slight emboss, a rough edge, a ribbon or cord that can be reused.
- a small insert that tells a short story about the making, written in plain voice, never marketing voice.
- a set of rules for what never changes, no matter how far the family grows: margins, a corner treatment, the position of the name. it makes future additions easy and keeps the whole thing from drifting.
- a quick internal test: cover the logo and see whether the piece still feels like it belongs. if yes, the system is doing its job.

loose reminders

keep the sketchbook nearby. notice what's pleasing in everyday objects, such as a worn tag, a taped box, a pencil note on kraft paper, and ask why. leave room for accidents. step away from the work long enough to see it fresh, then return and cut whatever isn't pulling its weight. the best version usually has fewer parts than the early versions, and a bit more nerve.
