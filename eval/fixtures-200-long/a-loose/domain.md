working notes, general

**approach**

- start from the problem, not the look. most projects that go sideways trace back to a vague brief that everybody nodded along to. before sketching anything, try to say in plain sentences what the thing is supposed to do for the person using it. if that sentence is mushy, the visuals will be mushy too.
- small studio, small footprint. fewer handoffs, less overhead, more responsibility for the boring parts nobody sees. worth remembering the boring parts are part of the service.
- prefer rough and honest over polished and wrong. a loose sketch that gets a real reaction beats a beautiful comp that gets polite silence.
- always ask what can be removed. most of the improvement in a layout comes from taking things out, not adding them.
- trust the constraints. tight limits on type, color, and space usually produce cleaner work than open fields do.

**process, loosely**

Every project seems to breathe in and out. There's a stretch of gathering: references, old materials, the client's own words, screenshots of things that annoy people. Then a stretch of making a mess: lots of directions, most of them bad, a couple of them interesting. Then a stretch of narrowing, where the interesting ones get pushed until they either hold up or fall apart. Then the careful finishing work, which is slower than it looks from outside.

Some habits that help:

- keep a running scrap file per project where half-thoughts go. half the good ideas show up while doing something unrelated, and they're gone by the end of the day if not caught.
- look at work small and look at it huge. a layout that falls apart at thumbnail size has a hierarchy problem. a layout that falls apart zoomed in has a craft problem. both are worth checking.
- squint at it. blur it. flip it. view it in grayscale. view it on a bad screen. view it on a phone held at arm's length in bad light.
- step away from the work before sending it. fresh eyes catch the stray misaligned thing, the typo, the awkward gap.
- write the rationale in plain language while it's still fresh. if the reasoning can't be explained simply, it probably isn't finished.
- save versions generously. disk space is cheap and regret is not.

**working with clients**

- people hire a designer partly to be told what they can't see themselves. don't just take dictation, but don't be precious either.
- feedback is rarely about the thing it says it's about. "make the logo bigger" often means "I don't feel seen." dig a little before reacting.
- show fewer options than feels natural. a huge spread invites cherry-picking and mushy hybrids. a small, confident set invites a real conversation.
- explain the why behind a choice before the what. people accept a color much more readily when they understand what job it's doing.
- keep the tone warm and the paper trail clear. friendly and documented are not opposites.
- be upfront when something is outside the studio's strengths. nobody ever resented an honest "that's not my thing."
- expect that the person approving the work is often not the person who briefed it, and plan the explanation accordingly.

**keeping the lights on**

Running a one-desk operation means a lot of small background obligations that don't announce themselves. Accounts, logins, hosting, mailboxes, backups, licenses, certificates, the quiet infrastructure that makes the studio look like it exists. None of it is interesting, and all of it is the kind of thing that fails silently and at the worst possible moment.

Some general thoughts:

- anything that can lapse without a visible warning deserves a place in a list somewhere that gets looked at regularly.
- it helps to know where each important login lives and who else, if anyone, could get in during an emergency.
- backups are only real if a restore has been tested. a backup nobody has opened is a hope, not a backup.
- keep personal and studio things separated as much as possible. mixing them makes everything harder to untangle later.
- documentation for future-you is a gift. assume future-you remembers nothing.

**things to keep in mind**

- good work needs slack. a calendar packed edge to edge leaves no room for the idea that arrives sideways.
- rest is not a reward for finishing, it's an input to the work.
- taste is built by looking at a lot of things, including bad ones. keep feeding it.
- don't confuse busy with productive. a lot of motion can hide the absence of a decision about the actual problem.
- the best portfolio pieces are usually the ones where the client was brave. notice which collaborators make that possible.
- accessibility is not a bolt-on. contrast, legibility, and clarity are design, full stop.
- pay attention to what people actually do with the work, not just how it looks at launch. a thing that looks great and confuses people is still a failure.
- be wary of trends that arrive with a lot of enthusiasm and no reasoning behind them.
- say thanks to the people who make the work possible: printers, developers, photographers, the friend who proofreads.

**open-ended ideas**

- a small library of questions to ask at the start of any project, sorted by what they uncover: goals, fears, audience, constraints, politics. could grow organically over time.
- a habit of writing short post-project reflections: what worked, what dragged, what the client understood differently than expected. not a report, just a paragraph.
- experiment with a visible process page on the studio site that shows messy intermediate work. might build trust, might scare people off. worth testing.
- collect a private archive of overlooked everyday design: signage, packaging, forms, receipts, ticket stubs. useful as a reminder that most design happens without a designer.
- think about whether the studio wants a distinctive voice in writing as well as in visuals. the way a proposal reads says a lot.
- try constraint exercises on slow days: only one typeface family, only two colors, no images, no grid. then see what survives into real projects.
- consider a recurring habit of giving away small useful things, like templates, checklists, or short guides. generosity tends to circle back in odd ways.
- explore what it would look like to collaborate with other small studios on bigger jobs without turning into an agency. what would the shared rules need to be?
- wonder about teaching or mentoring in some low-key form. explaining the work out loud is a good way to find the holes in it.
- keep an eye out for slow, unglamorous categories of client that are underserved by design: local trades, community groups, small makers. there's often more room to do real work there than in the flashy corners.
- leave a margin of open space for personal projects with no client, no brief, and no audience. that's usually where the next real direction comes from.
