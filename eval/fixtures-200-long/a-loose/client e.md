general working notes

**how i usually approach recurring graphic work**

recurring work lives or dies on systems, not inspiration. the fun part of a studio is the blank page, but steady work for social wants the opposite: a kit that makes the next piece easy to start and hard to get wrong. so most of my thinking goes into scaffolding before the pieces themselves.

- build flexible templates rather than a pile of finished layouts. each template needs clear slots for a headline, an image, a supporting line, and a mark, and it should survive being stretched into different shapes without falling apart.
- keep a living style sheet: type choices, color roles (not just swatches, but what each color is *for*), spacing rules, how photos get cropped, how illustration is handled. write it in plain language so it makes sense to a person who has never opened my files.
- name layers and files as if a stranger will inherit them. future me is that stranger.
- save export presets for every placement so nothing gets resized by eye.

**batching beats scattering**

when requests trickle in, it's tempting to answer each the moment it lands. that fragments my focus and the output starts to look like it came from different hands. gathering related requests and working through them in a sitting keeps the visual voice cohesive, and it makes it easier to notice when a series needs variety or has gone stale.

**before opening any software**

- restate the brief in my own words. if i can't sum up the purpose of a graphic in a line, the graphic won't be able to either.
- ask what the viewer should feel or do afterward. announcement, reminder, invitation, proof of credibility, plain entertainment. each calls for a different hierarchy.
- gather reference from outside the category. if everything looks like the rest of the feed, it disappears into the feed.
- sketch small and ugly. thumbnails on paper catch composition problems faster than polished comps do.

**working with the client**

- ask for reactions in terms of goals, not taste. "this feels off" is the start of a conversation, not an instruction. useful questions: what is it making you feel, who is it for, what would you expect to see here.
- present work with a short rationale, not a lecture. show it in a feed-like frame rather than on a blank canvas, because a graphic behaves differently once it's shrunk next to other things.
- track feedback patterns. if the same kind of note keeps coming back, it belongs in the style sheet so it stops coming back.
- share rough directions early. it's cheaper to find out a direction is wrong while it's still loose.
- keep the tone warm and plain. most people respond well to clarity and short messages.

**scope and boundaries**

recurring work attracts small extras that quietly pile up: a tweak here, an extra format there, a rush request. each is reasonable alone, but together they reshape the relationship. worth keeping a light log of what gets asked so the pattern is visible and any conversation about it can be calm rather than tense.

- separate maintenance (using the system) from invention (growing the system). new illustrations, new layouts, and new campaign looks are invention and deserve their own consideration.
- revisions work best when aimed at a goal, not a mood. revising toward "make it feel more welcoming" goes better than revising toward "make it pop."
- be kind and clear about limits. a boundary stated early is gentler than a boundary stated after resentment has built up.

**craft checks before sending anything**

- shrink it to thumbnail size and see whether the main message survives.
- view it in light and dark interface modes.
- check safe margins so crops and overlays don't swallow text.
- read all text backwards, word by word, to catch typos. verify names and spellings against the source, not memory.
- check contrast with a critical eye. simulate color blindness if the tools allow it.
- confirm image rights and keep a record of where every asset came from.
- compress sensibly: sharp but not heavy.
- look at it on a small screen, since that's where it will actually live, not just on the big monitor.

**rhythm and variety**

a feed is a sequence, not a collection of isolated pieces. think about pacing: a loud piece followed by a quiet piece, text-heavy beside image-led, a recurring format people learn to recognize. anchor formats build familiarity; wildcards earn a second look. too many anchors and it gets dull, too many wildcards and nothing feels like a brand.

**open-ended ideas**

- a library of small decorative elements (shapes, textures, doodles, hand-drawn marks) that can be recombined so pieces feel handmade but stay on-brand.
- motion as a light layer: subtle loops derived from static pieces, so a still can be reused in a livelier form.
- a mood-based palette shift that stays inside the brand's rules, for variety without drifting off-system.
- pull quotes from customers as graphic material, treating real words typographically.
- behind-the-scenes pieces: process shots, rough sketches, material samples. people like seeing how things get made.
- data as graphic: simple charts and diagrams that make dry information friendly.
- a starter pack of editable files the client's team could use for tiny changes, so small edits don't need to route through me. might reduce friction; might also dilute uniformity. worth thinking about, not settled.
- alt text suggestions delivered alongside graphics, as a quiet accessibility habit.
- a swipe file of work i admire, annotated with why it works rather than just that it does.
- a recurring "wildcard" format reserved for experiments, so risk-taking has a home and doesn't leak into everything.

**things to keep in mind**

- the audience is not me. my taste is a tool, not a target.
- steady work can slide into autopilot. protect some room for play, even if it never ships.
- notice when a piece is portfolio-worthy, and remember that sharing it publicly is a permission question, not an assumption.
- responsiveness is a feature, but constant availability is a trap. a calm pace produces better work than a jumpy one.
- if the client ever shares how posts performed, write down which formats landed and which fell flat. results turn taste arguments into evidence.
- assets arriving late (photos, logos, copy) are the most common source of stalls. a lighter way to collect them might save more effort than any design trick.
- keep source files tidy and backed up in more than a single place, even when it feels like overkill.

**open questions**

- how much of the system should the client own versus me?
- what's the least intrusive way to ask how things are working?
- how do i keep a long-running visual language from calcifying?
- where does a refresh become necessary, and who notices the need first?
- is there a way to make the style sheet something the client actually reads, rather than something that sits in a folder?
- what would a quiet, low-effort check-in look like, one that surfaces problems before they become frustrations?

**small habits worth noticing**

keep a scrap file of half-ideas from finished work, since a rejected direction for one piece is often the right direction for another. leave a short note on the last file of a batch about what i'd try next, so picking the thread back up doesn't start cold. step away from a layout before judging it; fresh eyes catch spacing problems that tired eyes forgive. and when something works unusually well, take a moment to figure out why, so it can be repeated on purpose rather than by luck.
