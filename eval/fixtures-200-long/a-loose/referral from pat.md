general working notes

Some loose thoughts on how the studio runs when it's running well, and what tends to slip when it isn't. Less a plan, more a pile of reminders.

**approach**

The best work usually starts with a lot of listening and very little drawing. The urge to open the canvas right away is strong and almost always premature. A conversation that wanders is more useful than a brief that has been polished into vagueness. People rarely say what they need in the opening exchange. They say what they think they're supposed to want, and the real thing shows up sideways, in an offhand comment about what annoyed them on a competitor's site, or why an old logo still makes them wince.

Running a studio of just myself is both the advantage and the hazard. There are no handoffs, so nothing gets lost in translation. But there's also nobody to push back when I fall in love with a direction too early. So the habit worth building is manufacturing my own friction: step away, come back with fresh eyes, and ask what a skeptical stranger would say about the work.

**process**

Rough shape of how a project tends to move, bending depending on what it is:

- Absorb. Read everything the client sends, even the rambling parts. Collect references without judging them yet.
- Reframe. Write the problem back in plain language, as if explaining it to a friend who knows nothing about the field. If the restatement feels flat, the understanding is probably shallow.
- Explore wide. Sketch badly and quickly. Nothing in this phase is precious. The goal is to find the edges of the space, not the answer.
- Narrow. Pick the directions that feel alive and let the rest go. Resist keeping a "just in case" pile, because it dilutes the presentation and invites the client to pick the safest option.
- Refine. This is where craft lives: spacing, rhythm, how a shape behaves when it's tiny, how a color looks on a bad screen in bad light.
- Present with a story. Work without a rationale invites arguments about taste. Work with a clear line of reasoning invites useful discussion.
- Hand over cleanly. Organized files, plain notes on how to use everything, and an honest list of what was left unresolved.

Feedback deserves its own paragraph. Vague reactions ("it needs more energy") are usually pointing at something real but misdescribed. The job is to translate rather than defend. Asking "what would you expect to feel when you see this?" tends to get closer to the truth than asking "what would you change?" Also worth remembering that feedback from a committee is often a negotiation among the committee members, not a verdict on the design. Finding out who actually holds the decision is a design problem in itself.

**things to keep in mind**

- Scope creep starts politely. It arrives as "while you're in there, could you also..." and it deserves a friendly, clear acknowledgment that it's a different piece of work. Ignoring it breeds resentment on my side, and absorbing it silently teaches the client that boundaries are soft.
- Silence is not agreement. If a client goes quiet after a presentation, the instinct is to read it as approval. More often it's confusion, or politics, or a busy inbox. Gentle follow-up beats assumption.
- Protect the making. Admin expands to fill any space left open: invoices, emails, tidying the website, reorganizing folders. None of it is optional, but it needs a container so it doesn't swallow the hours when the real thinking happens.
- Keep the files honest. Name things so that a stranger, or a future version of me who has forgotten everything, could find the right layer in a hurry. Cleverness in naming is a loan that comes due at the worst moment.
- Tired eyes lie. Work reviewed at the end of a long stretch looks either better or worse than it is. Distance is a tool. Printing something, flipping it upside down, or viewing it very small all reveal problems that screens hide.
- Say what's uncertain. Clients tend to trust a designer more, not less, when the designer is plain about what's unknown, what's a guess, and what's a firm opinion.
- Don't confuse busy with good. A full inbox feels like momentum, but momentum is measured by whether the work is getting better and the relationships are getting easier.
- Look after the body. Wrists, back, eyes. A one-person studio has no replacement parts.
- Remember why the work matters to the people who commission it. A logo or a layout is rarely the point. It's usually a nervous person trying to feel like their small enterprise is taken seriously.

**on relationships**

Most of the work that arrives comes through people, and that deserves a bit of thought beyond the transactional. Good relationships with past clients and with peers are the quiet infrastructure of the whole operation. They don't need to be managed like a pipeline. Checking in without an agenda, sharing something useful, and remembering what mattered to someone are all cheap and rarely wasted. The danger is treating warmth as a tactic, because people can tell, and it also makes the warmth less enjoyable to give.

It's also worth being deliberate about fit. Some projects are a good match for how the studio thinks, and some are an uneasy stretch. The uneasy ones can still be valuable, but they cost more energy than they appear to, and it helps to notice that early rather than late.

**open-ended ideas**

Things that might be worth poking at when there's room, none of them urgent:

- A small archive of abandoned explorations, the directions that were good but wrong for the project at hand. Revisiting them sometimes seeds something new, and it's a gentle reminder that nothing made is truly wasted.
- Plain-language case studies that focus on the problem and the reasoning rather than the polished final images. The thinking is the actual product, and it's rarely shown.
- A recurring exercise of redesigning something mundane, like a receipt, a form, a signpost, or a bus stop. Low stakes, high learning.
- An opening questionnaire that works more like a prompt for stories than a form to fill in. Questions like "what's the worst thing that could be said about this business?" tend to unlock more than "describe your brand values."
- Constraint experiments: a restricted palette, a tightly limited type setup, a layout built from only rectangles. Limits sharpen decisions.
- A visual diary of things noticed in passing: worn paint, hand-lettered signs, odd packaging, accidental typography. A habit of looking closely is the raw material for everything else.
- Collaborations with other independent makers, such as illustrators, writers, developers, and photographers, where the combined result is something none of us would produce solo.
- A short reflection template to fill in after a project wraps: what went smoothly, what dragged, what was learned, what was misjudged. Patterns only show up when the evidence is written down.
- Writing in public about process, not to perform expertise but to clarify thinking. Explaining something is the quickest way to find the gaps in understanding it.
- Questions to leave open: what would the studio look like if every project were something worth showing? What kind of work does it avoid out of habit rather than conviction? What would a slower, more deliberate rhythm look like, and what would it cost or give back?

**a few reminders to close**

Curiosity is the renewable resource here. When the work feels stale, the fix is rarely more effort and usually more input: a walk, a book from an unrelated field, a conversation with someone who has no stake in the outcome.

Taste is built by paying attention, and it erodes when attention goes to autopilot. Staying a little uncomfortable, a little unsure, and a little interested in how other people solve problems keeps the work honest.

And the small stuff counts. A clear email, a tidy handoff, a considered note at the right moment. The craft of running the studio is part of the craft of the studio.
