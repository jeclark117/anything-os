general working notes

**how i think about feedback**

feedback usually shows up as short fragments, and the fragments carry more than they say. a comment about a feeling is rarely about the exact spot it points at. so before touching anything i try to sort comments into rough buckets: taste, function, brand, and nerves. taste can be talked through. function mostly can't be argued with. brand means zooming out and checking the wider system. nerves need a conversation, not a revision.

- read the whole batch before reacting to any of it
- look for the comment underneath the comment
- ask what problem a request is solving before deciding how to solve it
- if a new request pulls against an earlier request, say so out loud instead of quietly picking a side
- keep a list of things that were said casually and then dropped, they have a way of coming back

a request framed as a small tweak is sometimes a symptom of something bigger. when a client keeps asking for little nudges to the same area, the area itself probably isn't working, and nudging won't fix it. worth stepping back and asking whether the structure underneath is the real issue.

**working rough before refined**

i like to stay ugly for as long as possible. grayscale, placeholder text, boxes standing in for imagery. it's easier to judge hierarchy when color and polish aren't doing the persuading. if the layout reads clearly with everything stripped back, it'll survive the decoration. if it only works once it's dressed up, that's a warning sign.

- squint at the page and see what survives; that's the real hierarchy
- shrink the whole thing down and check whether the main message still lands
- try the layout with the worst possible content: long headlines, awkward images, missing images
- test empty states and overflow states, not just the happy path
- leave room for content to change without breaking the composition

there's also a trap in showing polished work too early. people respond to polish as if it were finished, and then even small changes feel like a step backward. rough work invites bigger conversations, which is usually what's needed at the start.

**color and contrast**

color choices carry mood, but they also carry responsibility. whatever palette ends up on screen has to hold up for people with tired eyes, bright glare, cheap displays, and different kinds of vision. a shade that looks rich in a dim studio can go muddy on a small screen outdoors. check text against its background in several conditions, not only on a calibrated monitor.

adjusting a shade in one place ripples outward: buttons, links, hover states, borders, focus rings, illustrations, charts, form fields. a palette is a system, so changing a member of it means walking through the rest and seeing what shifted.

- build tints and shades as a ramp instead of picking values by eye each time
- check how accents behave next to the main color, not just by themselves
- write down why a shade was chosen so later tweaks don't undo the reasoning
- view on real devices in real light whenever possible
- watch for colors that look different next to photography than they do on a flat background

**small screens**

small screens are where a design gets honest. things that felt generous on a wide canvas turn cramped, and things that felt minor turn out to be load-bearing. brand elements especially tend to get squeezed, so it's worth designing the small version of an identity deliberately instead of letting it scale down by default. tap targets need room. text needs room. imagery needs a considered crop rather than a mechanical one.

- design for thumbs and for interrupted attention
- think about what gets cut, not only what shrinks
- check what happens when a visitor rotates the device or bumps up the text size
- slow connections deserve a thought; heavy imagery can ruin a good layout
- scroll the page the way a bored visitor would, fast and slightly impatient, and see what registers

a good test is to hand the layout to a stranger on their own device and watch where their thumb goes. the hesitation tells you more than any opinion would.

**type and spacing**

spacing does more quiet work than almost anything else. generous, consistent rhythm makes a page feel calm and confident even when the content is ordinary. inconsistent spacing makes even good content feel nervous. when a layout feels off and nobody can say why, spacing is the usual suspect.

- pick a spacing scale and respect it, break it only on purpose
- let headlines breathe; crowding them rarely adds emphasis
- check line length in the widest and narrowest layouts
- be suspicious of type that looks great at display size but falls apart in body text
- use weight and size contrast before reaching for more color

**communication habits**

- write feedback summaries in plain language and send them back for a sanity check
- show work with a short explanation of the reasoning, so reactions are to the idea and not just the surface
- offer options only when the options are genuinely different; near-identical variants waste attention
- separate "here's what changed" from "here's where i'd push back"
- be honest when a request would weaken the result, and offer an alternative that serves the same goal
- avoid surprising people with large changes in the middle of small rounds

it helps to remember that the person giving feedback is usually not a designer and shouldn't have to be. their job is to tell me how the work feels and what it needs to do for their business. my job is to translate that into decisions. when the translation goes wrong, it's usually because i skipped asking a question.

**being a solo studio**

being solo means every role lives in my head: designer, account manager, bookkeeper, support desk. context switching is the real cost. keeping notes tidy is a kindness to my future self, and the habit of writing down why, not just what, pays back constantly. also worth protecting: long stretches of uninterrupted making, kept separate from stretches of replying.

- name files in a way that makes sense to a stranger
- label layers and components, resist the urge to leave a mess "just for now"
- archive old directions instead of deleting them; they're sometimes useful later
- jot open questions at the end of a working session so there's a place to restart
- keep a running scrap file for stray ideas so they don't clog the actual work

**open-ended ideas**

- a personal glossary of vague feedback phrases and what they usually turn out to mean, like "make it pop" or "feels off"
- a lightweight kickoff template that asks about fears and past disappointments, not just goals
- a reusable review checklist covering contrast, tap targets, spacing rhythm, alt text, and focus states
- short screen recordings in place of long written explanations
- a mood vocabulary: adjectives paired with examples, so "warm" or "serious" means something shared
- a short retrospective note after each project: what felt smooth, what dragged, what to ask earlier
- letting clients annotate directly on a prototype instead of sending lists
- a token-based system that makes global tweaks cheap and low-risk
- presenting rationale visually, maybe with before-and-after comparisons
- testing whether showing work on a phone before a desktop changes how people react to it
- a small library of layout patterns that have held up well under pressure

**things to keep in mind**

- taste is real, but so is evidence; try to bring both to the table
- polite agreement isn't the same as enthusiasm
- a quiet response can mean satisfaction or confusion, worth gently checking
- the best feedback conversations feel like thinking together, not grading
- people attach to what they've already seen, so early directions linger longer than expected
- constraints are a gift; a tight brief usually produces sharper work than an open one
- when in doubt, simplify; when still in doubt, step away and look again with fresh eyes

there's a recurring pattern where the most useful feedback is the least polished. an offhand remark about how something felt on a phone, or a pause before answering, often says more than a formal review. worth building habits that catch those moments instead of filtering them out.

one more loose thought: it might be useful to treat every project as a small experiment in how to work, not only what to make. the deliverable matters, but so does what gets learned about process along the way, and that learning compounds if it gets written down.
