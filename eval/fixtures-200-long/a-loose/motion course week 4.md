general working notes

**how i tend to approach motion**

motion is mostly about deciding what the viewer should look at and when. before anything moves, i try to say what the piece is for in plain language. if i can't, no amount of easing will rescue it. a lot of the thinking happens on paper or in a loose text file: what changes, what stays put, where the eye should end up.

i like to treat movement as part of the design, not a coat of paint on top of it. if a layout only works when it's animated, that's a sign the static version isn't doing its job. if the static version is strong and the motion only clarifies it, that's usually the right balance.

- movement should explain something: where an element came from, what it's connected to, what just happened
- restraint reads as confidence. the urge to make everything move is almost always wrong
- timing carries more personality than shape. the same shape can feel playful, nervous, or heavy depending on how it accelerates
- start with the weight of things. heavy objects take longer to get going and longer to stop
- hierarchy applies in time as well as space. what arrives early gets noticed, what arrives late feels like an afterthought

**process, roughly**

mostly a loop of sketch, rough, look, adjust.

- thumbnails for the story beats, with arrows and scribbles. ugly is fine
- a rough pass using plain blocks standing in for the real elements, just to feel the rhythm. no polish, no color, no detail
- watch it on loop with the sound off, then again with sound on, because they tell different stories
- adjust the biggest problems first. overall pacing before individual curves, curves before tiny offsets
- only after the rhythm feels right do the real assets go in
- step away and come back with fresh eyes. things that felt smooth often feel mushy after a break, and things that felt abrupt often turn out fine

i keep a small pile of reference clips that i like, mostly not from design work. film title sequences, sports replays, the way a cat lands from a jump. real physical movement is a better teacher than any other motion piece, because it's honest about momentum.

**things to keep in mind**

- *anticipation and follow-through.* a small wind-up before a move and a little settle after it make things feel alive. skip them and everything feels like a slideshow with extra steps
- *overlap.* parts of an object shouldn't all stop at the same moment. let the tail, the shadow, the secondary elements drift a touch behind
- *easing is the whole game.* linear movement is nearly always wrong for interface and brand work, but extreme easing gets tiring fast. aim for something that disappears into the experience
- *consistency.* if the system has a feel, every moving part should share it. a bouncy element in a calm piece feels like a stray from a different project
- *readability.* a move that's beautiful but too fast to follow is a failed move. the viewer needs time to register what happened
- *accessibility.* some people are sensitive to large sweeping movement, parallax, or flashing. there should be a calmer version of anything that travels a long way across the screen
- *file weight and playback.* a gorgeous piece that stutters on an ordinary device is not gorgeous. test on the weakest hardware around, not just the nice machine
- *sound.* even a very quiet cue changes how motion is perceived. silence is a choice too
- *handoff.* describe the motion in words as well as showing it, so whoever builds it can reproduce the intent and not just the look

**small habits worth keeping**

- name layers and groups like a stranger will open the file tomorrow, because it might be future me, who is basically a stranger
- save versions often with plain descriptive names, not "final" and "final-really"
- keep a notes panel beside the work for questions i'd otherwise forget
- export rough previews early and send them around for reactions, since a clip tells the truth faster than a description does
- when stuck, slow everything down to see what's actually happening, then speed it back up and trust the feeling
- when a piece feels flat, check contrast in time: is there enough difference between fast and slow, still and moving?

**open-ended ideas**

things i'd like to poke at sometime, with no pressure attached:

- motion as a brand voice. what would it mean for a studio's identity to be defined as much by how things move as by how they look? a set of movement rules that sits alongside the color and type rules
- tiny interactions as portfolio pieces. showing the small, almost invisible moments, like a button settling or a menu unfolding, as craft in their own right
- animating from the sound outward. build the audio texture first and let it drive the timing, instead of the usual reverse
- limited-motion pieces: designs where only a very small part moves, and the stillness around it does the heavy lifting
- the idea of "motion debt," where inconsistent little animations pile up over time until a product feels incoherent. maybe an audit method for catching it early
- drawing with time. treating duration as a material the way type and space are materials, and writing about what i learn
- hand-made imperfection: slight wobble, uneven spacing, a bit of roughness to counter the too-clean look that a lot of motion work has
- transitions between sections of a website as storytelling, not just a way of hiding loading
- a personal library of reusable movement recipes, described in words and not just saved as files, so the logic travels between projects
- running small experiments with a deliberate constraint, like only using movement along a single direction or only changing scale, to see what the limit teaches
- looking at how motion feels on different surfaces: a tiny screen, a huge display, a projection in a dim room. the same curve can feel entirely different

**questions to sit with**

- what is the minimum movement that still communicates the idea?
- where does motion help understanding, and where is it just decoration?
- how does the piece feel when it's repeated over and over? something charming the first time can become irritating on repeat
- what happens if the viewer misses the beginning? does it still make sense when joined halfway through?
- is there a moment of stillness, and is it earned?

**a closing thought, loosely**

the best motion is often the kind nobody comments on. people just feel that something is clear, responsive, and well made. chasing that feeling, and not the flashy reveal, is probably the right target for a small studio where every piece has to carry its weight and reflect the care that went into it.
