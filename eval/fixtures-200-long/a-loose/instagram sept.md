working notes, loose

**how i like to start a project**

- start by listening more than talking. the brief is rarely the real problem. what people ask for is usually a symptom, and the useful bit is hiding in how they describe what annoys them.
- gather a pile of references before sketching anything, then deliberately look away from them. if i sketch while staring at the pile, everything comes out as a remix.
- write the problem in plain words before touching a layout. if i can't explain it without design jargon, i don't understand it yet.
- keep a running list of questions that haven't been answered. a lot of mid-project pain comes from questions that were quietly skipped at the start.
- make ugly versions early. rough work is cheap, and it's much easier to react to something imperfect than to imagine something perfect.

**how the work moves**

the pattern that works best for me is wide, then narrow, then wide again. explore broadly, pick a direction, push it until it starts to creak, then step back and ask whether the creak is a flaw or just the interesting part. a lot of good decisions come out of that last question.

things i keep relearning:

- the rough stage is where the personality of a project gets set. if i polish too soon, i lock in choices i never actually made on purpose.
- constraints are friends. a tight palette, a limited set of type styles, a strict grid. the work tends to get braver when the rules get stricter.
- when stuck, change the medium. pencil instead of screen, scissors instead of cursor, walking instead of sitting. the problem usually isn't the idea, it's the posture i'm holding while thinking about it.
- leave space around things. whitespace isn't empty, it's doing a job. most weak layouts are just crowded layouts.
- if a detail only makes sense when i explain it, it's not working.

**working alone, practically**

being a studio of just me has upsides and some quiet hazards. nobody walks past my desk and says "that looks off," so i have to build that friction in myself.

- keep a habit of stepping away and coming back with fresh eyes. the work looks different after a night's sleep, a walk, a meal. flaws that were invisible become obvious.
- print things out when possible. paper is honest in a way a bright screen isn't.
- squint at the work, flip it upside down, view it small. if the hierarchy survives all that, it's probably sound.
- find a trusted outside eye now and then, somebody who isn't a designer. they'll say the thing a designer would politely skip.
- protect the boring admin hours, but don't let them swallow the creative ones. the balance drifts if i don't watch it.
- write down what went well and what didn't while it's still fresh. memory edits itself into a flattering story.

**communication habits worth keeping**

- say what i'm looking for in feedback. "does the overall feeling land?" gets a different response than "what do you think?"
- show reasoning, not just results. when people understand why a choice was made, they argue with the logic instead of with the taste.
- keep the language simple. fancy words in a presentation usually hide uncertainty.
- be clear about what's still open and what's settled, without being rigid about it. confusion about that is behind most revision spirals.
- when someone is unhappy with a direction, ask what they were hoping to feel. the answer is more useful than a list of changes.
- say no kindly and early rather than yes vaguely and late.

**thinking about showing the work publicly**

sharing work is its own craft, separate from making it. a few loose thoughts:

- people respond to a sense of a real person making real decisions. polished finals can feel distant, and a glimpse behind the curtain feels like an invitation.
- not everything needs to be shown. some work is better kept private until it's ready, and some never needs an audience.
- captions are underrated. a sentence of honest context beats a paragraph of marketing voice.
- consistency of voice matters more than consistency of schedule. a feed can feel coherent even if the rhythm is uneven.
- watch for the trap of making things because they'll photograph well. the work should lead and the documentation should follow.
- engagement signals are interesting but they're a weather report, not a compass. useful for noticing patterns, risky as a steering wheel.
- people save things they might use and share things that say something about them. worth asking which of those a given piece invites.

**open-ended ideas to chew on**

- a small collection of exercises that are purely for warming up: redraw a logo from memory, restyle an everyday object, design a label for something imaginary. low stakes, high looseness.
- a "found things" archive: textures, signage, handwriting, odd packaging, torn edges. a library of the accidental that no template can offer.
- what would it look like to write a short guide on how the studio thinks, just for myself? not a brand statement, more like a field manual.
- try designing something with the sound off, then with only sound in mind. how does a poster feel if it had to be read aloud?
- explore the idea of leaving deliberate imperfections. a slightly off-register print, a hand-cut shape, a wobble in the line. when does warmth tip into affectation?
- a recurring habit of revisiting old projects with new eyes. what would i change, what still holds up, what surprised me?
- collaborating more with people outside design: writers, makers, musicians. the best surprises come from somebody else's blind spots overlapping with mine.
- documenting failures as carefully as successes. the abandoned directions often contain better ideas than the chosen one.

**things to keep in mind**

taste is a muscle and it gets lazy if only fed what it already likes. look at things that annoy me, and ask why. there's usually a lesson in the irritation.

don't confuse busy with productive. a quiet stretch of thinking is still work, even if nothing visible comes out of it.

be wary of the urge to over-explain. if the work needs a long defense, either the work or the framing needs another pass.

good design is mostly attention. noticing what's actually there, what's actually needed, what's actually in the way. most of the craft is learning to look properly and then having the nerve to remove things.

stay curious about the unglamorous parts: file hygiene, naming, organizing. a tidy foundation is what lets the playful stuff happen without panic.

and remember why this started. the point was never perfection, it was making things that feel considered, that treat the person on the other end like they matter. when in doubt, return to that.
