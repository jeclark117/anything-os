invoice process

working notes

approach

start by listening more than talking. most projects drift off course because the brief was fuzzy and nobody said so out loud. ask what the thing is supposed to do for the people who will use it, not just how it should look. the look gets easier once the purpose is clear.

keep early work messy on purpose. rough marks, ugly layouts, placeholder words pasted in. polish too early and people get attached to details that were never meant to survive. rough work invites feedback. shiny work invites a yes or a no with nothing useful in between.

make the client's job easy. give them a clear question to react to instead of "thoughts?" something like "does this feel closer to calm or closer to loud?" gets a far better answer than an invitation to react to everything at once.

trust instinct, but check it against the brief. a gut feeling is a good starting point and a terrible final argument.

process

- gather: references, existing materials, anything the client already loves or can't stand. ask about the dislikes especially. they're usually more honest than the likes.
- explore: go wide before going deep. make directions that disagree with each other so the conversation has something to push against.
- narrow: pull the strongest direction forward and park the rest in a folder. don't delete them. parked ideas sometimes turn into the answer later.
- refine: small adjustments matter most here. spacing, rhythm, contrast, how a headline sits next to its neighbors.
- hand off: tidy files, label everything plainly, write a short guide so the work still makes sense when I'm not around to explain it.

communication

- say what's happening before it's asked for. silence reads as trouble even when everything is fine.
- after a call, write down what came up in plain language and share it back. misunderstandings are cheaper to catch while the conversation is still fresh.
- explain the reasoning behind a choice, not just the choice. clients can disagree with reasoning, which is a lot more productive than disagreeing with taste.
- be careful with jargon. words like "hierarchy" and "kerning" are shorthand for me and noise for most people.
- when feedback is vague, translate it. "make it pop" usually means something specific like more contrast, more energy, or more attention on a particular element. ask a question that gets at which.
- when feedback stings, wait before answering. the useful part usually survives a pause, and the defensive reply usually doesn't.

on feedback rounds

- ask for reactions before solutions. "this feels cold" is more useful than "make it red."
- separate what's subjective from what's functional. legibility problems aren't matters of taste.
- let the work breathe between rounds. coming back with fresh eyes catches things that staring never will.

craft

- constraints help. a limited palette, a restricted set of type styles, a strict grid. they cut down the ways to get lost.
- whitespace is a material, not leftover space. decide where it goes deliberately.
- squint at the work. blur it, shrink it, flip it upside down. whatever survives is the real structure.
- look at work in context. a logo that looks great on an empty canvas can fall apart on a cluttered shelf, a phone screen, a tote bag.
- save the boring details for when energy is high. alignment and layer naming are dull, but they separate tidy work from almost-tidy work.
- keep a swipe file of things that caught my eye, and study what they're doing rather than just how they look.

habits worth protecting

- keep the desk clear enough to think. a messy desk is fine for making and bad for deciding.
- name files as if a stranger will have to read them later, because eventually the stranger is me.
- walk away from stuck problems. the answer often shows up while doing dishes.
- write down stray ideas immediately, wherever they happen, then sort them later.
- limit the open tabs. too many references is procrastination dressed up as research.
- read outside the field. architecture, cooking, old signage, botany. good ideas travel across disciplines.
- protect the quiet stretch where the real thinking happens. notifications kill it.

keeping in mind

- running a studio alone means being the designer, the bookkeeper, the project manager, and the person who restocks the coffee. switching between those roles costs more than it seems. batching similar tasks together tends to feel better than hopping around.
- scope creep rarely shows up as a big ask. it shows up as small friendly requests that each seem reasonable. worth spotting the pattern early and naming it kindly.
- not every project is a good fit. the signs are often visible early: vague answers, no interest in the purpose, a fixation on copying something else.
- rest matters. tired eyes make worse calls about color and spacing, and it's hard to notice when it's happening.
- boring admin is part of the craft. clean files, clear records, tidy folders. they make the creative stretches feel lighter.
- don't compare my insides to other studios' outsides. portfolios are highlight reels.

open-ended ideas

- a small library of kickoff questions, sorted by what they reveal: goals, fears, taste, constraints.
- a short written reflection after each project about what felt hard and what felt easy, kept somewhere easy to search.
- a plain-language "how I work" page that might quietly filter out mismatched clients before conversations begin.
- a habit of describing the finished piece in words before drawing anything. if the words are mushy, the drawing will be too.
- self-initiated projects for quiet stretches: typeface experiments, poster series, rebuilding old templates, redesigning something everyday that annoys me.
- a printed wall of finished work to glance at when motivation dips.
- trading critique with other independent designers, maybe informally, maybe just over coffee.
- a way of keeping a record of small wins so they don't get lost.
- a reusable starter kit of project files. would it save energy or just encourage sameness?
- what would it look like to teach part of the process? writing it down for others might sharpen it.
- a collection of "before" states for projects that end up looking nothing like where they started, to see how far the thinking traveled.

questions to chew on

- where does the process feel heavy, and is the weight useful or just habit?
- which kinds of projects leave energy behind, and which drain it?
- what's the right amount of explanation for a client who doesn't want to hear any?
- how much of taste is trained and how much is stubbornness?
- when does a constraint stop helping and start hurting?
- is there a way to make the quiet stretches feel like part of the work instead of a gap in it?
