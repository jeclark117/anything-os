general working notes

**approach**

the brief is almost never the real problem. a client describes what they noticed, and what they noticed is usually a symptom. the useful material hides in offhand remarks: the thing they wince at, the thing they keep apologizing for, the thing they wave away with "oh, that doesn't matter." catching that is most of the job. the drawing gets easier once the question is right.

worth remembering that a small studio wins on care, not scale. there's no layer of account managers between the person asking and the person making, and that directness is the actual product. anything that dilutes it deserves suspicion.

**process, roughly**

- start loose and ugly. paper, marker, bad thumbnails. polish too early and everything gets precious and hard to throw away.
- make the wrong version on purpose. push it until it's obviously too much, then pull back. the right amount is easier to find from the far side of too much.
- squint test. step back, blur your eyes, see what survives. if the hierarchy collapses, it was never really there.
- grayscale before color. if it holds up without color, color becomes a bonus instead of a crutch.
- build the system before the pieces. type, spacing, a restrained palette, a short list of rules. then the pieces mostly assemble themselves.
- print things out. screens flatter everything. paper is frank about size, heft, and contrast.
- when stuck, change the medium instead of grinding. sketch what was meant to be digital, build digitally what was meant to be sketched.
- keep a folder of rejected directions. they're rarely dead, just early for the wrong project.

**type and layout**

type does the heavy lifting more than imagery does. a good pairing with sensible spacing can carry a layout that has no pictures at all. things i look at before anything else:

- the gap between lines of text, which most people leave at default and shouldn't
- how a headline sits beside body text, contrast through size and boldness rather than fancy styling
- awkward breaks: stray words dangling at the end of a paragraph, lines that end on a tiny orphan
- whether the margins feel generous or merely leftover

white space isn't empty. it's what tells the eye where to rest. when a layout feels cramped the fix is nearly always removal, not rearrangement. take something out and see if anybody misses it. usually nobody does.

**color**

- begin with the mood, not the swatch. words first: warm, dry, quiet, loud, dusty, clinical. then find color that behaves like those words.
- restraint ages better. a palette with a clear dominant, a supporting shade, and a sharp accent outlasts a rainbow.
- check color in bad light, on cheap screens, on uncoated paper. if it only works in ideal conditions it doesn't really work.
- neutrals deserve as much care as the loud colors. a gray that's slightly too cool can sour everything near it.
- people see differently. contrast has to hold up for tired eyes and for readers with color vision differences.

**working with clients**

- early on, ask what they'd hate to see. it's a faster route to taste than asking what they love.
- show fewer directions with stronger reasoning rather than a wall of options. a crowd of choices makes feedback vague.
- explain the why in plain language. nobody argues with "this reads clearly from across a room" the way they argue with "i just like it."
- feedback is information about a feeling, not an instruction about a pixel. when a client says make the logo bigger, they sometimes mean they don't feel seen. dig a little before reacting.
- keep correspondence warm and brief. long emails invite long replies, and long replies invite scope drift.
- be candid when an idea isn't working, including my own. that builds more trust than agreeable silence.

**things to keep in mind**

working solo means nobody taps you on the shoulder when you've wandered down a rabbit hole. build in small ways to step out: a walk, a different task, a conversation with a friend who isn't in the field. fresh eyes are a resource, and they have to be manufactured.

- naming files sensibly is a gift to a future self who has forgotten everything.
- version history is a safety net, but a messy one. tidy as the work goes, not in a panic afterward.
- keep the source material for client work somewhere that will still make sense far down the road.
- rest matters more than it looks. tired work looks tired, even when the maker can't see why.
- the wrong project can cost more than an empty gap does. worth remembering when a gap starts to feel uncomfortable.
- taste is built by looking widely. old posters, hand-painted shop fronts, instruction manuals, ticket stubs, seed packets, packaging from elsewhere.
- imitation is fine as practice, but notice what's being copied: the surface or the thinking underneath.
- critique is easier to take when it's about the work and not the worker. that's true when giving it, and doubly true when receiving it from yourself.

**open-ended ideas**

things that might be fun to explore, no pressure, no plan:

- a visual diary of overlooked lettering found around town. tiny handmade signs, faded paint, mismatched replacement letters on old storefronts.
- an experiment in designing entirely under constraints: only straight lines, only the type already installed, only leftover paper scraps.
- a loose set of prompts for the opening conversation of a project, sorted by mood instead of by industry.
- writing about process more openly. not tutorials, more like candid postmortems of what went sideways and why.
- collaborating with a printer, a writer, or a photographer on something purely for fun, where nobody is the client.
- a studio zine. something tactile and slightly strange to send to people who've been kind.
- designing toward a feeling instead of a product. what would it look like if the deliverable were calm? or mild mischief? work backwards from that.
- making an identity in wildly different registers, formal, playful, austere, just to see which version feels most like the client and which feels most like a surprise.
- treating sound and motion as part of an identity instead of an afterthought. what does a brand sound like when it walks into a room?
- building a shelf of self-initiated work that shows what the studio does when nobody's paying. clients tend to want to see that, and it keeps the muscles from going soft.
- revisiting old projects with fresh eyes and asking what would be different if they were started now. not to redo them, just to measure drift in taste.

**loose questions**

- how much of what feels like instinct is just pattern memory, and how could that memory be fed better?
- is there a way to show process without turning the website into a museum?
- what's the right level of weirdness to let into client work?
- where does consistency end and boredom begin?
- what would the studio's output look like if speed weren't a factor at all?
- which habits came from real need and which are just leftovers from somewhere else?

**small reminders**

stay curious about the boring parts: alignment, spacing, naming, file structure. the boring parts are where polish lives. the flashy parts get noticed, but the quiet parts get trusted.

when the work feels flat, it's frequently because there's no point of view. pick a stance, even a small one, and let the layout lean into it.

and when nothing is working, go look at something unrelated for a while. a garden, a market, a stack of old magazines. the answer tends to show up sideways.
