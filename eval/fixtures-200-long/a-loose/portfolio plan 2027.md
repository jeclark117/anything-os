portfolio plan 2027

general working notes

approach
- start from the problem the work solved, not from how it looks. attractive images are easy to come by, a clear account of why a choice was made is rarer and more useful to whoever is reading
- keep the framing honest. if the brief shifted midway, that belongs in the story and usually makes it better
- lead with the strongest image or moment, then explain. people skim, so the top of any page has to carry weight by itself
- write captions as if talking to a smart friend who isn't a designer. jargon only when it earns its place
- show a little of the mess. rough sketches, abandoned directions and marked-up proofs make the finished thing feel earned rather than inevitable
- a portfolio is also a sales document, even when it doesn't look like one. every piece quietly answers the question of what it would be like to work with me

how the work usually moves
Most of my better work comes from spending more time than feels comfortable at the start, just looking and listening. The temptation is to open the software straight away because making things feels like progress. It often isn't. A page of plain sentences about what the thing needs to do, who it is for, and what it should never feel like tends to save me from a lot of polished detours.

After that I like to work loose and wide. Thumbnails, scraps of type, colour guesses on the back of an envelope. The goal is to be wrong cheaply. If a direction is going to fail, I would rather it fail while it is still a scribble.

Then comes the narrowing, which is where taste matters most. Some questions that help:
- what can be removed without anything being missed
- does the idea still work in greyscale and at a tiny size
- if the logo, headline or hero image vanished, would the piece still be recognisable
- am I keeping this because it's good or because I'm attached to it

I try to leave space between making and judging. Looking at something with fresh eyes catches problems that hours of staring never will. Even a walk around the block counts.

documenting as I go
The worst version of writing up a project is trying to remember it afterwards. The best version is leaving breadcrumbs while it happens. Useful breadcrumbs:
- screenshots of work in progress, even the embarrassing ones
- a running list of the awkward questions the brief raised and how they got answered
- notes on what the people I'm working with actually said, in their own words, since their phrasing is often better than mine
- photos of the physical stuff: proofs on the desk, samples, test prints, things taped to the wall
- a rough sense of what I'd do differently, written down while it still stings

- client a case study once it launches in january
- client q report
- client s poster

Raw material like this makes the eventual write-up quicker and a lot more specific. Specific is almost always better than polished.

on longer written pieces
A write-up of a finished project works best when it has a spine. Something like: here was the situation, here was the tension, here is what we tried, here is what happened. Resist the urge to list every deliverable. A reader doesn't care that fourteen things were made; they care about the two or three decisions that changed the outcome. (that was a lapse in my own advice, ignore the counting, keep the principle.)

Things worth including when available: a before and after, a quote from someone who used the thing, a detail shot that shows craft, and some honest reflection on what was hard. Things worth leaving out: process diagrams that look the same on every studio site, vague claims about impact that can't be backed up, and any sentence that could be pasted onto an unrelated project without anyone noticing.

on data-heavy documents
Documents that carry a lot of information live or die on hierarchy and rhythm. A few habits:
- decide what the reader should grasp from a quick flick through, then make sure that survives
- let charts do one job each, and label them like a human would
- give the text room. generous margins and a calm type scale do more for credibility than clever layout
- treat tables as design objects, not afterthoughts
- check how it reads on a screen and how it reads in the hand, since they behave differently

When showing this kind of work publicly, the content is often confidential. Mock-ups with swapped content, cropped details, or a description of the structure without the substance can all work. Always ask before showing anything that might be sensitive, and prefer being too careful.

on print and large-format work
Anything meant to be seen from a distance has to win at a glance. Test by pinning it up across the room, or squinting, or shrinking it on a screen. If the main idea doesn't survive, it isn't ready.
- scale and contrast do most of the heavy lifting
- one strong image or typographic gesture beats a crowd of competing parts
- paper, finish and ink change the mood more than people expect, so proofs on the real stock are worth chasing
- photograph the finished piece in context, on a wall, in a window, in someone's hands, rather than floating on a grey mockup background

keeping things honest and kind
- credit collaborators: photographers, writers, printers, developers. it's right, and it makes the studio look generous rather than lonely
- don't claim results I can't prove
- be careful with how the people I've worked with come across. a story that flatters me at their expense is not worth telling
- keep the tone consistent across the whole portfolio so it feels like it was made by a single mind with a point of view, which, in a studio like this, it was

things to keep in mind
- the work that excites me most isn't always the work that best shows what I can do for the next person who might hire me. both matter, but they're different questions
- consistency of presentation counts for a lot. matching image sizes, captions in the same voice, and similar structure make a collection feel intentional
- loading speed, readable type and sensible navigation are part of the design, not extras
- keep the originals organised. a tidy archive of layered files, exports and photographs saves real grief later
- stay a little suspicious of trends. what looks fresh right now can look dated surprisingly fast
- rest matters. work made while exhausted usually shows

open-ended ideas
- a short "what went wrong" section on a few projects. failure stories build trust and are rarely told
- a side area for experiments and personal studies, clearly separate from commissioned work, where I can play with ideas without a brief
- recorded voice notes about a project, transcribed and edited, as a lower-effort way of capturing thinking
- a small gallery of tools, materials and objects from the desk, to make the studio feel physical
- a recurring format for talking about process, so each write-up shares a skeleton and is easier to produce
- asking collaborators for a sentence or two about what the experience was like, in their own voice
- sketching the portfolio's overall shape on paper before touching any layout: what the visitor sees, in what order, and what feeling they leave with
- looking at how other creative fields present their work, such as architecture, film, or food writing, for structures the design world doesn't use
- an "unfinished" shelf for ideas that never found a home, which might attract the right kind of curiosity
- a plain-language glossary for visitors who aren't from the design world

questions to ask now and then
- who is this actually for
- what would a stranger understand in the first few seconds
- what's missing that I'd miss if I were looking at this as a potential collaborator
- is there anything here I'd be uncomfortable defending in conversation
- what am I avoiding because it's hard rather than because it's wrong

loose closing thought
A portfolio is never finished, only paused. The useful habit is keeping it a little alive: noticing what's dated, pruning what no longer represents the direction I want to go, and making room for what's coming. Treat it like a garden more than a monument.
