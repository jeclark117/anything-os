general working notes

**approach**

Most of the real work happens before anything gets drawn. A brief is rarely the actual problem. It's usually a symptom, or a guess at a solution dressed up as a request. The early stretch of a project is best spent asking what the thing is supposed to do for the people who will meet it, and who those people really are. Plenty of design trouble comes from solving the stated problem beautifully while the real problem sits there untouched.

Staying small is a feature. There are no meetings that go nowhere, no handoffs that lose meaning along the way, and no committee sanding an idea down until it's beige. The flip side is that nobody catches blind spots, so habits have to do that job: step away and come back, read the work aloud, squint at it, flip it upside down, shrink it to thumbnail size and see what survives.

Good work has a point of view. If a direction could belong to any business in any industry, the thinking probably isn't finished. Look for the specific, slightly odd detail about the client's world that makes the work feel like it could only be theirs.

**process**

- start rough. paper, marker, ugly shapes. polish too early and exploration dies
- make plenty of wrong versions on purpose so the right direction shows up by contrast
- keep a running pile of "almost" ideas; they tend to become useful on a later project
- work in grayscale as long as possible so structure has to carry the design before color gets to help
- sort out type before imagery, because type does more of the heavy lifting than people admit
- when stuck, change the medium: sketch instead of screen, print instead of preview, or the reverse
- write the words early, or at least filler words that sound real. fake text hides problems
- show work in context, on a mockup of where it will live, not floating on a blank canvas

Presenting is part of the design. Explain the reasoning in plain language tied to the client's goals, not to taste. "I like it" is weak; "this makes the main action obvious to a person in a hurry" is strong. Showing fewer directions is usually better than showing more, as long as every direction shown is something that would be fine to see chosen.

**type and color**

Type sets the voice before anyone reads a word. Pairings work when they contrast enough to build hierarchy but share some underlying logic, like proportions or stroke character, so they feel intentional rather than accidental. Spacing between letters and lines matters more than which face got picked, more often than not. Sloppy spacing makes expensive fonts look cheap, and careful spacing makes humble fonts look considered.

Color is mood plus function. Choose palettes that survive being printed badly, viewed on a dim screen, or squeezed into a tiny icon. Check the work in black and white, since if the hierarchy collapses without color then color was doing a job structure should have done. Remember that colors carry different meanings for different audiences, and what reads as calm in some contexts reads as warning or grief in others.

**working with clients**

Vague feedback is normal, and translating it is part of the craft. Clients rarely know what they want but always know what they don't like. Treat negative reactions as information, and ask follow-up questions until a reaction like "it feels off" turns into something usable. Feedback about a color or a typeface is frequently feedback about a feeling, so find the feeling.

Restating what was heard before reacting to it saves a surprising amount of friction. It also catches the moments when two people nodded along to different meanings of the same word.

Scope creeps in quiet ways, usually as "while you're at it" requests that each seem tiny. Naming them kindly and early keeps the conversation about what's included from happening after frustration has set in. Clear edges are a courtesy to the client too, since they know where they stand.

Sharing work in progress is a balancing act. Too early and the feedback fixates on unfinished details; too late and there's no room to steer. Matching the rawness of the material to the kind of response wanted, and saying so up front, helps a lot.

**things to keep in mind**

- keep files organized as if a stranger will inherit them, because future-me is effectively a stranger
- name layers, name versions, and resist the urge to call anything final until it truly is
- accessibility isn't a late-stage check. contrast, size, and clarity shape choices from the start
- constraints are friends. a tight palette, a limited grid, a narrow set of typefaces all make decisions easier and results more coherent
- leave breathing room. white space isn't wasted space
- consistency beats cleverness in systems; cleverness beats consistency in standalone moments
- if an element can't justify itself, it's decoration, and decoration is the first thing to question
- if it's hard to explain, it's probably hard to use
- look at the work the way a stranger would: tired, distracted, in a rush, on a small screen in bad light. if it still works there, it works

**rhythm and energy**

Creative work isn't linear, and forcing it usually produces stiff results. It helps to notice when good ideas tend to arrive (walking, washing dishes, drifting off) and protect that kind of time. A wander outside can solve what staring at the screen cannot.

Admin work expands to fill every gap if allowed to. Batching the dull stuff keeps it from leaking into thinking time. Rest is part of the process too. Tired eyes make muddy decisions, and muddy decisions are expensive to untangle later.

Curiosity outside design feeds design. Reading widely, and looking at buildings, plants, signage, old manuals, packaging from other eras, pays off in ways that are hard to trace. The best references rarely come from other designers' portfolios.

**open-ended ideas**

- a small library of reusable grid and spacing setups, not templates exactly, more like starting positions
- a habit of collecting found lettering and packaging, photographed and sorted by feeling instead of category
- a short questionnaire for new clients that digs at tone, audience, and what they're afraid of looking like
- a brief postmortem note at the end of each project: what worked, what dragged, what surprised
- a personal type specimen to see how favorite fonts behave at awkward sizes and in awkward pairings
- motion as a quiet layer on static work: a subtle shift, a gentle reveal, nothing that shouts
- a plain-language guide handed over with finished work so clients can use a brand confidently without needing help for every small question
- a portfolio that shows thinking and process, not just glossy finished surfaces
- a shelf of physical samples, like paper, textures, and swatches, to touch while thinking
- what would it look like to design for the ear or the hand rather than the eye? sound identities, tactile packaging, scent
- a self-assigned project with an absurd limitation, like only circles or only black ink, just to see what the limitation forces
- a running list of questions worth asking at the start of any project, refined over time until it reads like a personal compass

**small reminders**

Be kind to early drafts and harsher with later ones. Ask why a lot. Remove before adding. Avoid falling in love with a direction before it's earned it. When a project feels flat, it's often because the concept is fine but the details are lazy, or the reverse. Figure out which, and the next move gets clearer.
