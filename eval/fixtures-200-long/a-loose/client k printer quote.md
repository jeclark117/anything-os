general working notes

the way things go best is when the question gets settled before the answer starts taking shape. what does this piece have to do, for whom, and where will it live? looks come easier after that. a lot of wasted effort comes from polishing something that was solving the wrong problem.

**approach**
- read the brief slowly, then read it again hunting for what's missing instead of what's there
- write down the question underneath the question. "we need something new" is rarely the real ask
- gather references early but keep them honest. a reference is a mood, not a template
- sketch badly and fast on paper before touching the screen. bad sketches are cheap and they argue back in useful ways
- keep early rounds loose so nobody gets attached to a direction too soon
- name the constraints out loud. they're usually the most helpful part of the project, not the annoying part
- when stuck, make the thing worse on purpose and see what that teaches

**the messy middle**

every project seems to hit a stretch where nothing looks right. that's normal and not a sign of failure. the urge is to tear it all up and begin again, but the fix is usually smaller than it feels: a spacing problem, a weak hierarchy, a color fighting its neighbors, a typeface trying too hard. stepping away and coming back with fresh eyes helps more than more staring does. it also helps to change the medium. print it, flip it, view it tiny, view it enormous, view it upside down. problems that hide on a bright screen show up fast on a rough printout taped to a wall.

**hierarchy**
- work out what the eye should land on, then what it should land on after that, then let everything else quiet down
- if everything is emphasized, nothing is
- contrast comes from size, weight, color, space, and position. leaning on a small set of those is usually enough
- squint until the layout blurs and see what survives. whatever survives is the real hierarchy, whether intended or not

**type**
- fewer families, used with more intention
- line length and line spacing do more for readability than font choice ever will
- pairings work when there's clear contrast or clear kinship. muddled in-between pairings look like accidents
- test headlines at awkward lengths, since real copy never behaves like the placeholder text
- big type can carry a whole layout if the space around it is respected

**color**
- start with light and dark before worrying about hue. if it works in grayscale it will survive almost anything
- screens lie. what glows on a display can go dull on paper, and saturated tones tend to shift the most
- limit the palette, then let a single hue do the heavy lifting. a quiet palette with one loud moment beats a loud palette with no moments
- think about what a color means in the audience's world, not just whether it's pretty

**layout and grid**
- a grid is a way to be consistent without thinking about it every time. breaking it works best when it's visibly on purpose
- white space isn't leftover, it's a material
- generous margins usually make the whole thing look more confident
- check the edges: what happens near trims, folds, and gutters, where things quietly get lost
- alignment is invisible when it's right and unmistakable when it's wrong

**for anything physical**
- paper stock changes the personality of a design. coated, uncoated, textured, thin, heavy: all of it shifts how color and type read
- finishing choices like folds, coatings, and foils add complexity and risk, so it's worth asking whether they earn their place
- keep files tidy for whoever handles production: fonts packaged or outlined, images linked, color mode correct, bleed included, live area respected
- ask the production side early what they need. they know their machines better than any template does
- anything that can be misread on a spec sheet eventually will be
- hold a physical sample in your hand whenever possible. weight and texture don't translate through a screen

**working with clients**
- people react to what's in front of them, so show real context instead of isolated artboards
- explain the why behind a choice in plain language. taste arguments go nowhere, reasoning gets traction
- feedback often arrives disguised as a solution ("make the logo bigger"). worth translating it back into the problem underneath
- vague feedback is a gift if the follow-up question is good: what did it feel like, what did it remind you of, where did your eye go
- keep a written trail of what was asked for and why. memory gets creative
- be clear about who owns which piece of the project and who talks to whom, because confusion there sours otherwise good work
- presenting less is often stronger than presenting more. a pile of options invites picking favorites by gut instead of by fit

**working alone**
- there's no art director down the hall, so the critique has to be built in. leaving work alone for a good while before judging it is the cheapest substitute
- keep a folder of things that made you stop and look, for no reason other than that they did
- scope creep is quiet and friendly and eats the whole schedule. notice it early, name it kindly
- rest is part of the process. ideas show up in the shower, on walks, while washing dishes, almost never while staring at the artboard
- isolation can make everything feel more precious than it is. showing rough work to somebody outside the project helps

**file hygiene**
- name things so a stranger could follow
- keep a working file and a clean file, and don't send out the messy one
- save versions at meaningful points, not constantly and not never
- leave notes inside the file about odd choices. they save grief later
- an archive that's easy to search is worth more than a beautiful one

**open-ended ideas**
- a running swipe file organized by feeling instead of by industry
- a short checklist for physical jobs that lives right next to the template
- the habit of writing the design rationale as a paragraph before presenting, as a test of whether it holds up
- a personal library of textures scanned from real surfaces: concrete, linen, cracked paint, old paper
- a "what went wrong" log kept without blame, mostly for spotting patterns
- stress-testing a layout by imagining it printed on the cheapest stock imaginable
- what would this look like if it had to work with type only? what if it had to work with no color at all?
- trying a project with deliberately limited tools, say paper, scissors, and a photocopier
- a plain-language explainer for clients who've never worked with a designer, covering how rounds work and what feedback is most useful
- a way of recording how clients phrase things, so patterns in their language start to show
- a small reading list of old design annuals and trade manuals, since old solutions to old constraints still apply

**things to keep in mind**
- finished and good enough beats perfect and abandoned
- the audience usually isn't other designers
- clarity is a kindness
- taste develops through volume and through looking at old things as well as new ones
- nobody remembers the process. they remember how the thing made them feel and whether it worked
- small details signal care, and careless details signal the opposite
- a project that's easy to explain is usually a project that's easy to approve
- being curious about the client's business pays off more than being clever about the design
- good habits feel boring until the day they rescue something

**loose thoughts**

sometimes the best move is subtraction. take out the extra rule, the extra color, the extra flourish, and see if anybody misses it. usually nobody does. other times the work is too timid and needs one bold gesture that feels slightly wrong. the skill is knowing which situation this is, and that comes from looking, making, and getting it wrong in low-stakes ways often enough that the instinct builds up.

also worth remembering that a studio is mostly a way of working, not a place or a brand. the process, the habits, and the way problems get framed are what carry from project to project. the rest is surface.
