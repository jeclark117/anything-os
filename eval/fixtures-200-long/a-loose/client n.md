client n

general working notes, nothing specific, just stuff i want to keep in front of me when a new inquiry lands.

**start with the listener, not the logo**

the artwork mostly lives as a tiny thumbnail in a crowded list, scrolled past by a thumb. so whatever i make has to survive being shrunk to the size of a fingernail. if the idea only works big, it's an illustration, not a cover. worth squinting at everything, blurring it, dropping it to greyscale, and seeing what's left. what's left is the real design.

things that tend to die when small:
- thin strokes and delicate texture
- long titles set in a fussy typeface
- subtle gradients that turn to mud
- tiny credits or taglines nobody can read anyway

**the conversation before the pixels**

the best work comes from understanding what the show feels like, not just what it's about. a few questions worth asking, in a relaxed way, not as a form:
- who is actually listening, and what are they doing while they listen? commuting, cooking, falling asleep?
- what does the host sound like? warm, dry, chaotic, earnest?
- are there shows they love the look of, and shows they'd hate to resemble?
- is the tone likely to shift as the show grows, or is it settled?
- do they already have a face, a mascot, a color, a recurring joke that people associate with them?
- where else will the artwork show up? banners, social posts, video clips, merch? that changes how flexible the system needs to be.

people often can't describe what they want but can instantly say what they don't want. collect the dislikes. they're sharper than the likes.

**reference gathering**

keep a loose pile of reference, but be honest about its purpose. it's for understanding the territory, not for borrowing. a good habit is to look at what the whole category looks like, notice the clichés (the microphone, the headphones, the sound wave), and ask whether it's smart to lean into them or sidestep them. sometimes the cliché is useful shorthand. often it's just wallpaper.

also look outside the category entirely. book jackets, record sleeves, old posters, packaging, signage, hand-painted shop fronts. the most interesting covers usually borrow a sensibility from somewhere unexpected.

**exploration, the messy part**

- sketch small and ugly before anything polished. thumbnails by hand, loads of them, before the screen gets involved.
- push for range early. one safe direction, one weird direction, one in between. if every option is a variation on the same idea, the client isn't really choosing anything.
- resist polishing a direction i've fallen in love with before it's earned it. polish makes weak ideas look finished.
- keep a graveyard folder. rejected ideas often turn out to be right for something else, or right again after the client's taste shifts.
- let a rough idea sit overnight-ish, then look at it cold. the embarrassing parts show up fast.

**type is half the battle**

a cover is usually a title plus a shape. the lettering carries personality more than the image does. think about:
- is the title readable at a glance, in a crowded row?
- does the letterforms' mood match the voice of the show?
- would custom lettering make it ownable, or is a good existing typeface plenty?
- licensing, always. make sure whatever gets used is allowed for the way it'll be used, including on merch and video, before it's baked into the identity.

**color**

pick a palette that can hold its own against a lot of competing thumbnails. high contrast between the title and the background is non-negotiable. also check how it looks inside both light and dark interface modes, since the surrounding frame changes the feel. a color that glows on one background can disappear on another.

worth testing the artwork next to other shows' covers on a mock screen. you learn more from seeing it in a crowd than in isolation.

**files and handoff thinking**

- build everything in a way that's easy to re-edit: layered, labeled, sensible. future me will thank present me.
- make variations that might be needed down the line without being asked: a version without text, a simplified mark, a version that works in a circle crop, a version for video.
- keep the source art and the exported art clearly separated.
- write down which fonts, which licensed assets, which brushes or textures were used, so nothing gets lost.
- clarify who owns what, and what usage is covered. that's a conversation to have plainly and early, not an afterthought.

**working with the client**

- explain the reasoning behind choices in plain language. people trust a design more when they understand the why.
- show work in context, on a phone-shaped mockup, not floating on a white page.
- feedback like "make it pop" or "something feels off" is a prompt to ask better questions, not a problem. try asking what they feel when they look at it, or what the cover is failing to say.
- be wary of feedback by committee. if the host's friends and family are all weighing in, ask who the final voice is.
- stay kind and steady when someone is emotionally attached to a show. it's their baby, even if the art is only a small piece of it.
- keep a record of what was asked for and when it changed, just for clarity, not for blame.

**boundaries and sanity**

being a one-person shop means i'm designer, project manager, accountant, and support desk all at once. protect the making part. batch the admin. don't let inbox anxiety eat the good thinking hours. also remember that saying "that's outside what i do" is a perfectly good sentence. scope creep usually arrives politely, wearing a "quick favor" costume.

**open-ended ideas to chew on**

- could a cover be designed as a system, with a stable frame and a changing inner piece, so it can flex for special releases without losing recognition?
- what if the artwork had a small hidden detail that regular listeners eventually notice and feel clever about? little rewards build loyalty.
- is there a tactile approach worth trying? cut paper, ink, photographed objects, scanned textures. handmade qualities stand out in a feed full of glossy vector shapes.
- what would the cover look like if it were a sticker, a stamp, a ticket stub? sometimes changing the object changes the idea.
- could the show's sound be translated visually in a more interesting way than a waveform? rhythm, pacing, tension, silence?
- what's the emotional opposite of the obvious choice, and does it accidentally work?

**things to keep in mind generally**

- the first impression is the whole job. nobody reads a cover, they just feel it.
- good design is mostly removing things until the idea is obvious.
- trends age quickly. if something screams of the moment, ask whether the show will still want to wear it later.
- accessibility matters: contrast, legibility, not relying on color alone to carry meaning.
- when stuck, change the medium. draw it, cut it, print it, look at it upside down, show it to a stranger for a glance and ask what they think the show is about.
- keep the curiosity alive. the work gets better when i'm genuinely interested in the subject, so listen to the show properly, not just skim it.

**after the work leaves the building**

worth reflecting on what worked, what stalled, and what took longer than it felt like it should. a few notes to self about process, written while it's fresh, are worth more than any template. also worth noticing which parts of the process were enjoyable, since that's a clue about what kind of work to chase more of.
