general working notes

these are loose thoughts on how to think about the site as a working tool rather than a trophy. nothing here is fixed. it's a place to park ideas so they don't evaporate, and plenty of them will probably turn out to be wrong.

**approach**

the site is a portfolio piece in its own right. if it feels clumsy, that says something about the studio before anybody reads a word. so the bar is the same as for client work: clear purpose, careful details, no decoration without a reason.

- start from what a visitor is trying to figure out, not from what looks good. people arrive with a question in their head and the page should answer it quietly before they notice they asked
- keep the voice close to how the studio actually talks. warm, plain, a bit dry. avoid sounding like an agency brochure
- show the thinking behind the work, not only the finished result. people hire a way of solving problems as much as a style
- less is usually better, but less doesn't mean empty. a sparse layout still needs a reason for everything on it
- being small is an advantage worth leaning into. direct access to the person doing the work is something larger outfits can't really offer

**process**

the rough version of anything exposes problems faster than the polished version ever will, so the order of work matters more than the tools.

- sketch on paper before touching a screen. bad structure shows up immediately when it costs nothing to throw away
- build the skeleton in plain grayscale so layout problems can't hide behind colour or photography
- test with real material instead of placeholder text. filler text lies about how things will break
- write the words alongside the design, not after it. copy squeezed into a finished layout always reads like an afterthought
- check the page on a phone early and keep switching between small and large screens. whichever gets checked late gets neglected
- squint test: blur the page and see what still stands out. if the wrong thing stands out, the hierarchy needs work
- step away and come back with fresh eyes. effects that felt clever after a long stretch of staring can look fussy after a break
- browse the site like a stranger, jot down each small annoyance, then group the annoyances by theme rather than fixing them in a scattered way
- read the copy out loud. if it sounds stiff spoken, it will read stiff too

**things to keep in mind**

- speed matters more than anyone admits. heavy files make a beautiful page feel broken, especially on a weak connection. compress thoughtfully and look at how things actually load
- accessibility isn't a bonus layer. contrast, readable type, keyboard navigation, meaningful alt text. it makes the site better for everybody, not just the people who strictly need it
- avoid effects that will look dated quickly. calm, well-spaced layouts age better than whatever is fashionable right now
- consistency in small things (spacing, alignment, button behaviour, link styles) builds trust without anybody noticing. inconsistency builds doubt the same way
- people skim. clear headings, short paragraphs, honest labels. assume the reader is distracted and slightly impatient, and be kind to them anyway
- don't hide navigation behind clever icons. clarity beats novelty almost always
- the footer deserves care. it's where lost visitors end up
- check that links go where they claim and that forms really deliver. check the error states too, which nobody designs until they're annoyed
- keep the structure easy to update so adding new work never means rebuilding anything. a site that's annoying to maintain stops getting maintained
- think about how the page looks when shared as a link in a message. the preview image and title do more work than expected
- write page titles and alt text for real people, because that's who reads them
- keep an eye on the emotional temperature of the whole thing. it should feel like a calm, competent studio, not a nervous one

**open-ended ideas**

these are possibilities, not plans. some might look silly in hindsight.

- a case-study format with a steady rhythm: the problem, the approach, what changed. keeps the writing honest and the reader oriented
- a loose notes area with sketches, rejected directions, unfinished thoughts. could show personality, could also become a chore, so think about how low-effort it can be kept
- subtle motion that explains instead of decorates: a gentle fade to show where something came from, a hover state that confirms something is clickable
- a plain-spoken explanation of how a project usually begins, what the studio needs from a client, and what it doesn't do. filters for fit and saves awkward conversations
- testimonials in the clients' own words, short and unpolished. skip them if they start to feel like wallpaper
- a typographic voice that does more of the work, with a restrained palette so the projects carry the colour
- a gentle dark mode, if it can be offered without making maintenance heavy
- a different way of browsing the work: by kind of problem or by feeling rather than by medium or category
- an archive that lets older work live quietly without cluttering the main view
- a short, human note about how the studio works as a solo practice, framed as a strength without being cute about it
- small touches of personality in the places nobody expects, like a thoughtful not-found page or a friendly confirmation message
- a way to learn what works that doesn't turn into analytics obsession: watch where people hesitate, or ask a friendly outsider to think aloud while using the site

**questions worth chewing on**

- how much of the studio's personality belongs on the surface, and how much should wait for the conversation?
- where does polish end and fuss begin?
- what should a visitor feel in the opening moment: calm, curious, reassured?
- is the work better served by a tightly curated selection or by a generous spread that shows range?
- what does a stranger need to know to feel safe reaching out, and is any of it missing?
- which parts of the site would still make sense if the styling were stripped away entirely?

**on writing and tone**

plain words beat clever ones. describe the work in terms of what it did for the people using it, not in adjectives about how it looks. avoid jargon that only other designers would enjoy. where a sentence can lose a word without losing meaning, let it. a little humour is welcome as long as it never gets in front of the clarity.

**on staying sane**

collecting references is useful until it becomes a way of avoiding the work. look widely, then close the tabs and make something. imitation creeps in quietly, so check whether an idea came from a real problem or from a screenshot that was admired. keep the habit of asking what a choice is for. if the answer is "it looks cool", look harder.
