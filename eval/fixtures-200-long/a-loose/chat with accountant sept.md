general working notes

**approach**

the work goes better when the brief gets argued with a little before anything gets drawn. not in a hostile way, just poking at it. what is this really for, who is going to look at it, what happens if it fails quietly instead of loudly. most of the useful direction comes out of the awkward questions rather than the polite ones.

the upside of a small studio is no layers between the thinking and the making. the downside is that nobody catches the lazy assumptions, so the habit of catching them has to be built in. a sanity pass with fresh eyes, or at least with the screen turned off and a walk in between.

rough beats polished early on. a messy sketch invites correction, while a gorgeous mockup invites either applause or panic. honest feedback on something clearly unfinished is worth more than either.

taste is useful but it's not the same as judgment. taste says "i like this". judgment says "this does its job for the people who have to use it". the work needs both, and they argue, which is healthy.

**process**

- start wide. gather references, but also gather anti-references: things that sit close to the right vibe and still feel wrong. knowing why they feel wrong sharpens direction faster than a pile of things that feel right.
- write before drawing. a paragraph describing the feeling of the finished thing, in plain words, tends to be more reliable than a mood board.
- work in low fidelity until the structure holds up. grayscale, system type, boxes. if it works ugly, it will work pretty.
- let color and type arrive late. they're seductive and they hide structural problems.
- keep a running list of "things that bug me" while working. most of them turn out to be real problems rather than nitpicks.
- name files as if a stranger will open them. future-me counts as a stranger.
- when stuck, change the medium. pen, paper, cut-up printouts, reading the layout out loud, squinting at it from across the room.
- step away from a piece right at the moment it feels finished. the flaws show up in the gap.
- test with people who have no context. the person who already understands the project can't unsee what they know.

**things to keep in mind**

- clients rarely want what they ask for; they want what the thing does for them. translate requests into outcomes before reacting.
- feedback is data about a reaction, not a prescription. "make the logo bigger" usually means "i don't feel confident about this yet". find the feeling underneath the instruction.
- scope creep arrives politely. it sounds like "while you're in there" or "this should be quick".
- being the only person here means every context switch is expensive. long stretches of focus are the scarce resource. batch the admin, resist the pull of the inbox, keep the messy business stuff in its own part of the week's rhythm rather than scattered across it.
- rest is part of the process. the best ideas show up in the shower and on the bus, not at the desk staring.
- consistency beats brilliance in systems work. a good-enough rule applied everywhere is better than a brilliant rule applied in patches.
- accessibility isn't a layer on top. contrast, hit targets, reading order, motion sensitivity: build these in from the start or they never get in.
- write down the reasoning behind choices while it's fresh. in a while the reasoning evaporates and only the artifact remains, and then every question about it turns into archaeology.
- be wary of trends that arrive with a lot of enthusiasm and no explanation. if nobody can say what problem a style solves, it's decoration, and decoration ages fast.
- clarity is a kindness. clear writing, clear hierarchy, clear next steps for the reader. cleverness is allowed only when it doesn't cost clarity.
- say less in the interface. every extra word is something a tired person has to read.
- keep the relationship with a client warm even when the project is hard. most difficult projects are difficult because of fear, and fear responds to steady communication better than to defensiveness.
- over-explaining a design in a presentation usually means it isn't working yet. a good piece mostly speaks for itself, and the talking is for context.

**on working alone**

the isolation is real, and it quietly shapes the work. without a colleague across the room, ideas don't get tested in passing, and small doubts grow in private. it helps to externalize: talk through a problem out loud, write the argument against the current direction, or show half-formed work to a friend from a completely different field. their confusion is more valuable than polite praise.

it's also easy to confuse being busy with being productive. a full calendar can hide the fact that no real thinking has happened. the thinking is the product; the files are just the residue.

there's a tension between wanting every project to be portfolio-worthy and needing the practical projects to simply be solid. most good work comes from the unglamorous ones handled with care, and the portfolio pieces often come out of them as a side effect.

**open-ended ideas**

- a small library of reusable proposal paragraphs written in a human voice rather than contract-speak. the tone of the first conversation sets the tone of the whole relationship.
- a "graveyard" folder for rejected directions. they often get resurrected in other projects, slightly mutated and better for the wait.
- try making a project with a deliberately tiny palette and see what the constraint forces out of the layout.
- short case studies written from the perspective of the problem rather than the solution. what was confusing, what was at stake, what almost went wrong.
- a scrapbook of physical textures: paper, packaging, signage, worn paint, the way old labels fade. a surprising amount of digital design is borrowed from the feel of materials.
- explore sound as a design material for interfaces. what does an error feel like if it's a gentle sound instead of a red box?
- swap critiques with other independent designers on a loose, informal basis. no agenda, just a second pair of eyes and a mutual willingness to be blunt.
- it might be fun to run a loose, informal workshop on turning vague client instincts into a usable brief.
- a personal field guide to common design mistakes, illustrated with the studio's own old work. embarrassing, but instructive.
- look at how non-digital trades handle handoff and maintenance, like signwriters, bookbinders, carpenters. design handoff is often worse than theirs.
- collect the questions that cleared up projects fastest and keep them as a short prompt list.

**questions to chew on**

- how much polish is honest? at what point does finishing touches become a way of avoiding the next hard problem?
- when does a design system stop helping and start acting like a cage?
- what does a calm interface actually feel like, and is calm always the right goal?
- how do you tell a client their idea is weak without making them feel foolish for having it?
- is there such a thing as a neutral design, or is neutrality itself a style with a set of preferences baked in?
- what's the right amount of mystery to leave in a piece of work so people can bring something of their own to it?
- which parts of the craft are getting automated, and which parts become more valuable because of it? probably judgment, taste, and the ability to listen.

**small reminders**

- the best work usually has a point of view, even a modest one.
- people forget what you said and remember how the experience felt.
- the thing that's hard to explain is often the thing worth protecting.
- nothing needs to be perfect to be useful, but it does need to be considered.
- curiosity is a renewable resource as long as it gets fed from outside the field.
