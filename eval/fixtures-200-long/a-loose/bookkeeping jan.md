general working notes

**approach**

the studio runs on a simple belief: most design problems are really clarity problems wearing a disguise. when a project feels stuck, it's usually because nobody has said out loud what the thing is for. before touching a layout, it helps to write a plain sentence about the purpose and the audience, then see whether that sentence survives a rough sketch. if it doesn't, the sketch isn't the problem.

- start from the question behind the request, not the request itself
- sketch badly and quickly before opening anything digital
- keep early work loose enough that nobody gets precious about it
- treat constraints as free direction, since a tight brief is a gift
- when in doubt, take something away

working solo means every role lands on the same desk: designer, project manager, accountant, receptionist, keeper of the shared drive. the trick is not pretending those roles don't exist. each deserves its own mental hat, and switching hats on purpose beats drifting between them without noticing.

**process**

a rough shape that tends to work, though it bends constantly:

- listening: long conversations, plenty of notes, very few opinions. ask what's been tried before and what everybody hated about it.
- research: look at what neighbours in the same field are doing, then deliberately look at fields that have nothing to do with it. good ideas often arrive sideways.
- exploration: make lots of rough options, more than feels reasonable, and let the weak ones die early without ceremony.
- refinement: pick a direction, then sweat the spacing, the rhythm, the type, the way the eye travels across a page.
- handoff: tidy files, clear naming, and a short guide explaining the reasoning so the work doesn't rot after delivery.

the stages overlap. refinement will expose a listening gap. exploration will turn up a research question. that's normal, and it's better to loop back than to push forward on a shaky assumption.

**things to keep in mind**

- people rarely know what they want, but they know instantly what they don't. showing something wrong on purpose can speed up the conversation.
- feedback is data about the viewer, not a verdict on the work. separate the reaction from the proposed fix. the reaction is usually right and the fix usually isn't.
- a good rationale is part of the deliverable. work presented without reasoning gets judged on taste alone, and taste is a coin toss.
- unclear scope is the quietest way to lose goodwill. say what's included and what isn't, in plain words, early and again later.
- clients are busy with their own worlds. make approval easy with clear questions, obvious next steps, and no homework.
- keep a record of why choices were made. future-you will not remember, and future-you will be asked.
- be wary of polishing before the structure is sound. a beautiful wrong answer is still wrong.
- rest is part of the process. the best solution to a stubborn layout is often a long walk away from it.

**talking with clients**

tone matters as much as content. calm, specific, and a little curious goes a long way. when a conversation gets tense, it usually means something unspoken is underneath: worry about the audience, pressure from above, a past bad experience with another designer. ask about that directly instead of defending the work.

- summarize what was heard before answering it
- describe the problem a change would solve, not just the change
- when disagreeing, offer a way to test the idea rather than winning the argument
- explain unfamiliar terms without making anyone feel behind
- bad news travels better early and plainly than late and wrapped in apology

**the unglamorous side**

the money side of a studio is boring until it isn't. the habit that pays off most is keeping records tidy as things happen instead of letting them pile up into a mystery. a little attention often is kinder than a big scramble rarely. it also helps to keep business and personal money apart, to name files so a stranger could find them, and to jot down small recurring costs so they don't quietly accumulate in the background.

- receipts, invoices, and notes about what a payment was for belong together
- a clean trail makes questions from anyone painless, whether an accountant, a tax office, or a curious client
- it's worth noticing which kinds of work are rewarding in spirit as well as in fact, and which drain energy for little return
- slow payers are information, and so is a client who haggles over every small thing

**on value**

without getting into figures, the general idea: charge for the thinking, not the pushing of pixels. the visible effort is a small part of what a client buys. the rest is a long history of judgment compressed into a fast, confident choice. when cost comes up, anchor on the problem being solved and what solving it is worth to the client, not on effort. be careful with discounts, because they teach people the wrong thing about the work.

**craft notes**

- type does most of the heavy lifting. choose a voice and let it carry.
- whitespace is not empty. it's the pause that makes the sentence land.
- limit the palette until it hurts, then loosen only if the work asks for it.
- check everything at small sizes, on cheap screens, in poor lighting, and in grayscale. if it holds there, it holds.
- alignment mistakes are felt before they're seen. trust the itch.
- consistency is a kindness to the user. surprise is a seasoning, not the meal.
- read the copy out loud. awkward phrasing shows up in the mouth before the eye.
- ask what happens when the content is much longer, much shorter, or missing altogether.
- accessibility isn't a layer added at the end. contrast, size, and clarity are the design.
- name layers and files as if a tired stranger will inherit them.

**open-ended ideas**

stuff to chew on, none of it settled:

- a small library of thinking prompts for unsticking early exploration, like "what would this look like if it were embarrassingly simple?" or "what would the opposite brief produce?"
- a habit of collecting visual scraps with a sentence on why each caught the eye. the sentences matter more than the scraps.
- an archive of near-misses: directions that were rejected but still seem alive. some may deserve another life in other projects.
- short postmortems for finished work, focused on what surprised rather than what went wrong
- making things by hand, like printing, cutting, or tearing paper, to loosen up a screen-bound brain
- a self-initiated project with no client, only curiosity, to keep the eye honest
- explaining process through small visual stories instead of slide decks
- how a studio this small might borrow the best habits of larger agencies without inheriting their overhead
- a loose circle of trusted peers who trade honest critique with nothing formal attached
- what a calm studio actually looks like: fewer tabs, fewer notifications, more finished things

**staying sane**

- boundaries are a design decision too. define the edges of working time and protect them.
- say no to work that would require a personality transplant.
- the inbox is not a to-do list.
- save the best energy for the hardest thinking, and leave admin for when the brain is tired anyway.
- comparing yourself with other studios is mostly noise.
- praise is nice but shouldn't steer. neither should silence.
- keep some projects purely for pleasure, even if nobody ever sees them.

**a closing thought**

good work comes from attention, and attention is limited, so a lot of the job is protecting it. clear briefs, tidy records, honest feedback, and enough rest all serve the same purpose: leaving room for the part of the work that actually needs a person.
