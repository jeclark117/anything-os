general working notes

reference material is a conversation starter, not a spec. when a client points at something they admire, the useful part is usually why it feels right to them, and that's rarely the surface stuff. worth asking what they'd miss if the visuals were stripped away. the pace, the calm, the way it makes a visitor behave. people can't always say that outright, but they can react to questions like "what would bug you if it were missing?" or "what would make this feel like the wrong place?"

reading a reference
- separate layout from mood. a page can feel quiet because of spacing, or the writing, or the photography, or because nothing is begging for a click. each is a different lever.
- notice what's absent. missing things are easy to overlook and frequently carry the whole feel.
- check how it behaves on a small screen. plenty of lovely sites fall apart there, and a reference that only works on a big monitor is only partly useful.
- look at the edges: footers, forms, error messages, empty states. that's where a site shows whether the calm is real or just a coat of paint.
- watch the path to booking or signing up. for a service business that's usually the real job of the site.
- keep references loosely organized by feeling instead of by industry. a yoga site and a ceramics shop can share a mood, and that's usually the more useful grouping.

early direction
rough and cheap usually works best here. words on a page, a loose grid, a type direction, a color direction in swatches. the goal at the start is to find the argument, not to polish. a direction should be explainable in plain words before it's ever shown as a picture. if the explanation is mushy, the design will be too.

it helps to build in a way that makes things easy to throw away. the more polished a rough idea looks, the more a client attaches to it, and then it's hard to steer. keeping early work visibly unfinished keeps the conversation candid and open.

talking with the client
- reflect their language back to them. if they say "airy" or "grounded" or "clean", ask what they mean and write down the words they use. those words become a shared vocabulary for feedback later.
- reactions are more reliable than descriptions. showing something and watching their face beats asking them to imagine.
- be wary of taste borrowed from a screenshot. a site can look great and still be wrong for a different business, a different audience, a different voice.
- feedback like "make it pop" or "make it feel more premium" is a prompt for a question, not an instruction. dig for what's missing or what's too much.
- people occasionally want what they've already seen because it's safe. gently check whether they want something that feels like them or something that feels like what they've seen. either is valid, but they lead to different places.

type, space, and restraint
restraint is harder than decoration. when there's little on the page, every choice is exposed: the shape of the letters, the rhythm of the paragraphs, the exact spot where an image sits. there's nowhere to hide a weak choice.
- type carries most of the personality in a quiet layout. worth spending real effort on how heavy it feels, how tight or loose the spacing is, and how it reads at small sizes.
- generous spacing only works if it's consistent. a rhythm that wobbles looks like a mistake instead of a feeling.
- photography can do the emotional work that color and ornament would otherwise do. treat image direction as part of the design, not an afterthought.
- a restrained palette still needs contrast that holds up. soft grey on off-white looks elegant in a mockup and unreadable in bright sunlight.
- motion, if any, should feel like breathing, not performing.

copy and structure
- a sparse design leans hard on the words. thin or vague text shows up immediately when there's no clutter to distract from it.
- worth figuring out early who is writing the words and when they'll exist. placeholder text lies about how a layout will feel.
- navigation should be boring in the best way. people looking for a class, a teacher, a location, or a way to get in touch shouldn't have to think.
- think about what a nervous newcomer needs to see to feel welcome. that's often more useful than designing for the confident regular.
- length matters less than whether each section earns its place.

practical habits
- keep a running list of questions for the client instead of sending them as they pop up. a tidy batch is easier to answer and respects their focus.
- write down why a choice was made while it's fresh. future arguments about the work go better with a reason on file.
- test with real text and real photos as early as possible, even rough ones.
- look at the work on a phone held at arm's length, then on a big screen, then squinted at. squinting shows hierarchy faster than anything.
- step away from a layout and come back with fresh eyes before judging it.
- keep a folder of things that caught your eye for reasons unrelated to any project. it's a quiet source of ideas.

things that go wrong
- copying a reference too closely, then wondering why the result feels hollow.
- confusing empty with calm. a page can be bare and still stressful if the hierarchy is unclear.
- polishing details on a direction nobody has really tested yet.
- letting a template's habits sneak in: stock layouts, stock sections, stock phrases.
- ignoring accessibility because the design is "simple". simple designs can still have tiny targets, weak contrast, and confusing focus states.

open-ended ideas
- what if the site's quietness came from how it reads rather than how it looks? slow phrasing, generous pauses, a voice that doesn't hurry the reader.
- could the booking flow feel like part of the practice rather than a transaction? small gestures, soft wording, no pressure.
- worth wondering how the brand would feel with almost no imagery, and then what the smallest useful dose of imagery would be.
- scroll effects and cursor tricks are usually overdone. maybe the interesting move is a tiny detail that's barely noticed.
- a living style guide that reads more like a short letter about how things should feel than a rulebook.
- how does the work age? trends in calm design cycle quickly, so favor structure and language over fashionable effects.
- there might be value in a short list of what never goes on the page, just as much as what does.

closing thoughts
the best reference conversations end with a sharper question than they started with. the point of collecting examples isn't to imitate them but to triangulate: find where the client's taste overlaps with what their audience needs and what's actually buildable. when those line up, the design usually feels inevitable, and feedback gets easier.
