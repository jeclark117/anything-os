monthly review feb 2026

general working notes

**approach**

the work goes better when the brief is treated as the start of a conversation rather than a spec. what a client asks for and what they need are rarely the same thing, and the gap between them is where most of the interesting design lives. the early stage is mostly listening, asking slightly naive questions, and writing down the words people use without translating them too fast. their vocabulary usually holds more of the identity of a project than any mood board does.

- look for the constraint that everybody is quietly working around. that's often the real brief.
- sketch badly and quickly before opening anything digital. bad sketches are cheap to throw away, which makes them honest.
- ask "what would make this feel inevitable?" more often than "what would make this look good?"
- reference gathering is useful up to a point, then it turns into procrastination wearing a research costume.
- when a direction feels forced, it probably is. back up and look at what was assumed early on.

**process**

the loose shape is understand, wander, narrow, refine, hand over. the wandering stage is the part that gets squeezed when things get busy, and the work shows it. work that skips the wander tends to look competent and forgettable. protecting some room for aimless exploration is a quality decision, not an indulgence.

narrowing is where taste matters most. it helps to lay everything out where it can be seen together and look for the pieces that still hold up after a break. anything that only looked good in the moment of making usually falls away on its own.

refining is mostly subtraction. spacing, weight, color relationships, the rhythm of a layout. a lot of polish is just removing the thing that was nagging at the edge of the frame.

handover deserves more care than it usually gets. a good file structure, clear naming, and a short explanation of the reasoning behind the choices save a client from accidentally undoing the work later.

- keep working files tidy as the project goes, not at the end. cleanup at the end is always rushed.
- write the rationale while the thinking is fresh. reconstructing it later never recovers the nuance.
- leave room in every system for the things the client will inevitably add that nobody planned for.

**things to keep in mind**

- clients are rarely bad at taste, they're bad at articulating it. translating feedback is part of the job. "it feels off" usually points at something specific, like hierarchy or tone, and a gentle follow-up question tends to find it.
- being a studio of a single person is both the best and worst part of this setup. there's no committee to dilute decisions, and also no colleague to say "that's not working." building in outside eyes, even informal ones, is worth the effort.
- the quality of the questions asked at the start sets the ceiling for the whole project.
- scope creep rarely arrives as a big request. it arrives as small, friendly additions that each seem reasonable. noticing the pattern early makes the conversation easier and kinder.
- rest is part of the work. some of the best solutions show up when away from the screen, and forcing output on a foggy brain produces things that need redoing.
- good typography solves more problems than people expect. when a layout feels stuck, the answer is often in the type before it's in the imagery.
- consistency beats cleverness. a slightly plain system applied with discipline looks better than an ambitious system applied loosely.
- always check how a design behaves when the content is worse than the sample content. real text is longer, uglier, and messier than placeholder text.
- accessibility is not a layer added on top. contrast, legibility, and clarity are baked in from the start or they're expensive to retrofit.

presenting is its own craft. a strong piece can be sunk by a muddled explanation, and a modest piece can land well when the story around it is clear. it helps to frame the problem before showing the solution, so the audience arrives at the work already caring about what it solves.

- show fewer directions with more conviction rather than a wall of options. choice overload makes clients retreat into safe, generic answers.
- let the work breathe in presentations. crowded slides make everything look less considered.
- mockups in context do more persuading than flat artboards. seeing the thing where it will live changes the conversation.
- be honest about the weak spots. clients trust a designer who names the tradeoffs before they're discovered.

**the studio itself**

running a small practice means the studio is also a product, with its own identity, voice, and need for upkeep. there's a constant tension between serving client work and maintaining the thing that attracts client work. it's easy for the studio's own presentation to fall behind the quality of what it produces, the cobbler's-children problem. paying attention to how the studio looks from the outside, and whether that matches what it actually does well, is a recurring theme worth sitting with.

- what kind of work does the studio want to be known for, and does what's visible reflect that?
- are the projects that get shown the ones that were most enjoyable to make, or just the most polished?
- is there a gap between how the studio describes itself and how clients describe it back?

**open-ended ideas**

these are loose and unranked, just things that keep resurfacing.

- a small library of reusable thinking tools: question lists, critique prompts, checklists for the stages that tend to get skipped. not templates for the design itself, just scaffolding for the thinking.
- more experiments with constraints. working in a single typeface, a limited palette, or a strict grid for no client reason at all, just to see what falls out.
- writing about the process in a plainer, less polished way. the rough thinking is often more useful to readers than the finished case study.
- collaborating with people from adjacent disciplines, like writers, illustrators, and developers, on small self-initiated projects. the friction of other perspectives is useful.
- a habit of revisiting old work with fresh eyes, asking what would be done differently and why. the answers say a lot about how taste has moved.
- exploring how to make the client feedback loop less painful: shorter cycles, clearer prompts, better ways of capturing reactions as they happen.
- studying work outside design altogether, like architecture, editorial layout, signage, packaging, and old printed ephemera. the best ideas rarely come from other designers' portfolios.
- thinking about how to make quiet periods more generative, treating them as a resource for experimentation instead of a gap to be anxious about.

**small reminders**

- trust the instinct that something is unfinished, even when nobody else can see it.
- name files like a stranger will have to open them.
- keep a scrap file of discarded ideas. they're often the seeds of better work later.
- the best critique often comes from reading the work aloud, describing it as if to somebody who can't see it.
- clarity is a kindness, in design and in communication around it.
- not every idea needs to become a project. some are just there to be turned over and set down.
