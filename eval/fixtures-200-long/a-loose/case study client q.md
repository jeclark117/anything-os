case study client q

general working notes

stuff to reread when things get fuzzy. loose on purpose.

the thing that keeps proving true: people skim. a case study gets scanned long before it gets read, and it only gets read if the scan earns it. so structure matters more than prose. whatever a reader catches while scrolling past should carry the story without help, and the writing underneath is for the people who stay.

what a case study is for
- proof for a stranger deciding whether to trust the studio with their own mess
- not a victory lap. readers don't care that the work was hard, they care that the problem was real and the outcome is easy to see
- a record for me too. writing it up forces me to notice what I actually did versus what I remember doing
- the best ones read like a small mystery: here's what was off, here's what we noticed, here's what changed

framing the problem
- start from the situation the client was in, not the deliverable they ended up with. the deliverable is the answer, and people need the question before the answer means anything
- say what was at stake in plain terms. if the stakes can't be said plainly, maybe I don't understand the project well enough yet
- resist retrofitting a tidy story. real projects wander. a little honest wandering is more believable than a straight line that obviously got drawn afterward
- name the constraint that made it interesting. almost every good project has a constraint that shaped everything, and readers like seeing it
- describe the starting point as understandable, because it was. nobody hires a studio because things were going perfectly

evidence over adjectives
- words like "elegant" and "intuitive" are cheap. a measurable change, a before and after, a quote from a real user are expensive and worth more
- when the evidence is thin, say it's thin. a modest claim that holds up beats a big claim that makes the reader squint
- be careful with causation. if something improved while the studio was involved, that doesn't automatically mean the studio caused it. find the honest version of the sentence
- keep a running folder of artifacts during the project instead of reconstructing afterward: screenshots, rough sketches, messy early versions, odd feedback. the messy early versions are usually the best material because they show movement
- ask whether each piece of evidence answers a question a skeptical reader would actually have
- numbers without context are decoration. if a figure shows up, the reader should know what it's compared against and why it matters

visuals
- every image should earn its place by explaining something. decoration is allowed but it shouldn't pretend to be proof
- captions are underused. a good caption does much of the storytelling
- consistent crops and framing make a set of images feel like a system rather than a pile
- show process fragments too: the abandoned direction, the awkward draft. a polished end result with no history feels suspiciously frictionless
- watch for visuals that rely on context only I have. if I need to be in the room to explain it, the image isn't ready
- accessibility counts: contrast, alt text, not burying meaning in color alone. case studies about careful work shouldn't be sloppy themselves
- white space is part of the argument. crowding every image in makes the reader feel rushed, and rushed readers leave

writing it
- plain sentences, short paragraphs. cut anything that sounds like a pitch deck
- write the way I'd explain it to a smart friend who works in a different field
- use "we" carefully. it's a solo studio, and pretending to be a big team reads as false. but the client's people did real work and deserve visible credit
- verbs over nouns. "reorganized the dashboard so people could find their reports" beats "dashboard information architecture optimization"
- let the ending be quiet. no grand claim about the future of anything, just what changed and what the client can do now that they couldn't before
- read it aloud. anything I stumble over, a reader will stumble over
- headings should say something. "the problem with the old reports" is better than "background"

the client side
- a case study sits in a strange spot: it's my marketing and also their story, and it has to feel right from where they sit
- sensitive details can usually be generalized without killing the point. the shape of a problem often survives even when the specifics are blurred
- the client's reputation is in the frame too, so the story shouldn't make them look foolish, even if the situation was a mess
- think about whether they'd want a version in their own voice, or a quote, or something they can reuse internally
- their words are often better than mine. a blunt sentence from the person who lived with the problem beats my polished summary

working solo
- nobody is around to say "wait, what does that mean?" so I have to play that role deliberately. a skeptical read from a friend outside the field is worth a lot
- it's easy to over-polish because there's no team pushing the work out the door. polish can become a way of hiding
- keep process notes tidy as I go. future me is basically a stranger with no memory of the project
- practice with short versions of old projects just to build the muscle. not every project needs a full case study, some need a paragraph and a picture

after a draft exists
- cut the opening paragraph and see if the piece survives. it usually does, and usually improves
- check for sentences that only exist to sound impressive
- look for places where I explain the same thing in text and in a caption, and keep the stronger version
- read it as the least patient reader imaginable. where would they bail?
- read it again as a curious reader who wants to know how the thing was made. is there enough for them?
- let it sit untouched for a while, then reread with fresh eyes. embarrassing sentences show themselves right away

things to keep in mind
- not every project is a good case study. some are good work with a dull story, and that's fine
- the interesting part is frequently not the part I'm proudest of. check which part the reader would actually find interesting
- jargon is a trap. if a term only means something inside design circles, it's a wall
- avoid the "challenge, solution, result" template unless it truly fits. it flattens every project into the same silhouette
- people remember a concrete detail better than a general claim. hunt for the specific, strange, human detail
- humility reads as confidence. admitting what didn't work makes the rest more credible
- don't write for other designers by default. they're a smaller audience than it feels like, and they're not usually the ones hiring

open-ended ideas
- what if the case study opened with the problem as the client would have described it, in their own awkward words, before any polish?
- a short "what I'd do differently" section, written candidly. it might build more trust than anything else on the page
- an annotated version, where visuals have margin notes explaining choices as if I were walking a visitor through the studio
- a layered format: a skim layer, a read layer, and a deep layer for the curious. same story, different depths
- experiment with showing a failed direction in full, along with the reasons it didn't work
- consider whether a case study could be a conversation instead of a document, an interview-style piece where the client asks me the awkward questions
- try drafting the closing paragraph early, to see if the story actually lands somewhere
- an internal-only version that's brutally honest, kept in the notes as a counterpart to the public one
- look at how other fields tell project stories: architecture, journalism, even cooking shows. the pacing tricks might transfer
- would a small glossary help non-designers, or just add friction?
- a visual index of the whole body of work, arranged by problem type rather than by date or client, so a visitor can find the kind of mess that resembles theirs
- maybe a "field notes" strand, shorter and rougher than the polished studies, that shows how the thinking happens in motion

loose questions
- how much process is too much for a reader who just wants to know if I can solve their problem?
- is there a way to show judgment, not just output? judgment is what people actually hire
- what does a reader need to see to feel safe? usually evidence of care in boring places
- where does storytelling tip into spin, and how would I notice from the inside?
- what would a case study look like if it were written for the client's own team rather than for strangers?
- is there a version of this that works as a conversation starter instead of a portfolio piece?

random reminders
- specifics beat summaries
- the reader is busy and slightly suspicious
- credit generously, claim carefully
- if a sentence could appear on any studio's site, it probably shouldn't appear on this one
- the work is the proof, the writing is just the doorway
