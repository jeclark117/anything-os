client c

general working notes

approach

start by hearing people out before proposing anything. projects mostly go sideways because the real problem got named wrong at the beginning, not because the layout was off. a brief is usually a list of symptoms, and what needs fixing sits underneath. ask what a good outcome looks like in the person's own words, then ask what would make it feel like a failure. the failure answer is frequently more useful.

keep early sketches ugly on purpose. rough shapes on paper or in a throwaway file let people react without feeling they have to be polite about finished work. polish that arrives too soon invites arguments about spacing when the structure is what actually needs debating.

work from material outward. a layout built around placeholder text breaks as soon as real words show up. get actual copy, even rough copy, in front of the design as early as possible. if there isn't any, write stand-ins that have the right length and the right awkwardness instead of tidy dummy text.

treat constraints as friends. a tight palette, a limited set of typefaces, a stubborn grid. they remove fiddling and make the remaining choices count.

process, roughly

- gather: collect whatever exists. old materials, screenshots of things they admire, things they can't stand, the way they talk about their own work. the can't-stand list is gold.
- sort: group it into themes. look for what keeps surfacing without anybody asking for it.
- frame: put the problem in a plain line anybody could repeat back. if the line turns mushy, the project probably is too.
- explore: wide at the start, narrow afterward. resist falling for a direction just because it arrived early.
- pressure-test: show it to somebody who has never seen it, say nothing, and watch where they hesitate.
- refine: spacing, type, contrast, rhythm. this is where taste lives and also where it's easiest to overwork something into blandness.
- hand off: leave the person with something they can maintain without needing to call me.

things to keep in mind

website, finished 2026-08
happy with it

- being a solo studio means every role is mine: strategist, designer, project manager, bookkeeper, bearer of bad news. worth being deliberate about when each hat goes on so the designing doesn't get squeezed out.
- scope drifts quietly. a small favor becomes a pattern. it helps to have what's in and what's out stated in plain language early, and to point back to it kindly when requests creep.
- people judge a website by how the collaboration felt as much as by how it looks. clear communication, no surprises, answers to small questions without delay.
- accessibility isn't a bonus layer. contrast, focus states, readable text sizes, sensible heading structure. much easier to build in than to retrofit.
- performance is part of design. heavy imagery and fancy motion can wreck the feel of a lovely layout on a slow connection.
- the person who commissions the work isn't always the person who uses it. keep the visitors in mind, the ones who never sat in a meeting.
- leave room for change. material will grow, and sections will be added by somebody who wasn't around for the thinking. build systems that forgive that.
- document reasoning, not just output. why this typeface, why this arrangement. future me, or a future collaborator, won't remember.
- good work needs slack. a calendar packed edge to edge leaves no room for the idea that arrives sideways.

presenting work

walk people through the reasoning before showing the visuals. if the logic lands, the look arrives with context instead of being judged cold. show things on a real screen in a realistic setting when possible, since flat mockups flatter everything. name the tradeoffs openly; people trust a designer who points at the weak spots before they do. and avoid a huge menu of directions. too wide a spread turns the meeting into shopping and puts the burden of choosing on the person least equipped for it.

habits that seem to help

- keep a running log of small choices and the reasons behind them. it makes later questions cheap to answer.
- name files plainly. clever names are a tax on future me.
- when stuck, change the medium. draw it, say it out loud, print it, flip it upside down, squint at it.
- step away from the screen. walking, washing up, anything with the hands. solutions have a habit of turning up there.
- borrow from other fields: signage, packaging, editorial layouts, instruction leaflets, old posters, hand-painted shop fronts.
- keep a library of patterns that have held up under real use, with notes on where they strained.
- review finished work with distance. look at it as a stranger would, with no memory of the struggle.

when things feel off

usually it comes down to muddy hierarchy, uneven spacing rhythm, or copy whose voice doesn't match the look. check those before reaching for new colors or fresh fonts. also worth asking whether there's simply too much on the page. most pages are improved by taking something away. remove the decoration and see whether anything was lost. usually nothing was.

feedback

- separate reaction from instruction. "it feels cold" is useful. "make it blue" is a guess at a remedy. ask what the feeling is attached to.
- when feedback conflicts, there are usually different goals hiding behind it. surface them gently.
- don't defend on the spot. write it down, let it settle, answer thoughtfully.
- praise is data too. ask what exactly landed, because that's the thing to protect.
- quiet reactions matter. silence can mean delight or confusion, and the two look identical on a call.

voice and taste

keep a loose sense of what the studio's work feels like without letting it harden into a house style that gets stamped on everybody. the goal is that the work belongs to the people it's for. taste is useful as a filter and dangerous as a signature.

open-ended ideas

- a plain-language handbook for the people the work is made for, covering how to add pages, keep images light, write useful headings, and avoid breaking the layout by accident.
- a short reflection template for after a project: what worked, what dragged, what surprised me, what I'd approach differently. brief enough that it actually gets filled out.
- a swipe file of small interactions that feel good, each with a note about why.
- a way to show process publicly without giving away the whole kitchen.
- a short guide on how to brief a designer. might attract better inquiries by gently filtering out mismatches.
- what if every project came with a note about what to leave alone? the parts that work and shouldn't be tinkered with by well-meaning hands.
- ways to make review sessions less painful: annotated walkthroughs people can watch at their own pace, with room to leave comments in the margins.
- a habit of revisiting older work to see how it has aged, not to critique it but to learn which choices held and which were fashion.
- a collection of failure stories, mine and others', kept specific and truthful.

questions I keep circling

- how much of the craft is invisible, and how to make it legible without being showy?
- would a tighter niche make the work better or only narrower?
- what would it look like to say no more gracefully?
- is there a way to explain the value of restraint to somebody who equates more with better?
- how to stay curious on projects that feel familiar?
- what does a calm studio look like when work arrives in waves?
