scope creep

working notes, loose

approach

- treat the brief like a map rather than a contract nobody reads. clear edges make it easy to talk about what sits outside them without anybody feeling accused.
- ask what the work is supposed to do for the person using it before asking what it should look like. the look usually follows from that.
- write down the assumptions that feel too obvious to write down. those are the ones that end up different inside another person's head.
- keep the conversation warm. most friction in a project is really friction about expectations that were never spoken aloud.
- a small studio has a real advantage in being human. a client who feels heard is far more flexible than a client who feels managed.

how a project tends to breathe

there's usually an opening stretch where curiosity does most of the work: asking questions, collecting references, hearing for the exact words the client uses when they describe what they want. those words matter. they often point at the real brief hiding underneath the stated brief. after that comes a loose exploring stretch where nothing is precious and rough sketches are allowed to be ugly. then the work narrows, and narrowing is where judgment earns its keep. the closing stretch is mostly polish and handoff, and making sure the client can live with the result without needing a guide.

showing work is its own craft. rough work invites big reactions, polished work invites small ones, so match the finish to the kind of feedback that would actually help. if the structure is still open, don't dress it up so much that it feels settled.

feedback arrives as feelings, and part of the job is translating. "it feels off" is real data. ask what they expected to see, what they were worried about. when requests start piling up, look for the shared worry underneath them instead of treating each as a separate task.

things to keep in mind

- small asks are the dangerous ones. each seems harmless, which is exactly why they pile up unnoticed. the work grows a little at a time, like a hedge nobody trims.
- generosity is a real tool, but it sets a precedent. a favour offered without a label starts to look like part of the arrangement. label favours as favours.
- silence isn't agreement. a friendly nod with no follow-up doesn't prove the shared picture still matches.
- enthusiasm from a client is a gift and also a source of drift. excited people think of more things. that's a good sign, not a problem, and it deserves a good answer.
- resentment in the studio is a signal. it usually means a boundary got crossed a while back and nobody said so.
- a worn-out, mid-project mood is a poor setting for discussing boundaries. pick a calm moment.
- working solo means there's no buffer, no account manager to absorb the awkward parts. so the awkward parts have to be made easy by habit and phrasing.
- keep a parking lot for each project. when an idea shows up that doesn't fit, it gets a home instead of being ignored or silently absorbed.

phrasing bank

sentences that sound kind but stay clear:

- "that's a lovely direction, and it's a bit beyond what we sketched out."
- "i can see why that matters. let's look at where it fits."
- "happy to explore that. it changes the shape of the work, so let's talk about how."
- "this is a good problem to have."
- "i want to make sure we're picturing the same thing before i go further."

the client side

name it kindly and early
offer a change order

most drift has a real cause on the client's side: a new worry, a new audience, a conversation with somebody important that changed their mind. understanding that makes the response better, because the request stops being an imposition and becomes information. sometimes the new thing is actually the better project, and the right move is to say so out loud and let the shape change on purpose rather than by accident.

it also helps to remember that clients rarely know what's hard. a request that seems tiny from their chair might ripple through everything. explaining the ripple in plain words, without sounding defensive, tends to land better than just saying it's complicated.

when it gets tense

if a conversation heats up, slow down rather than speed up. restate what the other person said until they agree it's right; being understood lowers the temperature faster than any argument. separate the person from the request. a frustrated message is rarely about the thing it mentions. leave room for a pause, and let a thoughtful reply take its natural shape instead of firing back quickly. it helps to remember that the client wants the project to go well too. the disagreement is usually about the route, not the destination.

there's also a quiet kind of tension that never gets voiced: the studio silently doing extra, the client silently expecting it. bringing that into the open, gently, is a relief for everybody involved.

open-ended ideas

- what if every project began with a friendly page called "what this isn't"? not legalistic, more a sketch of the neighboring territory that's deliberately left unexplored.
- a visual way to show scope: a drawing with a clear outline and a fuzzy cloud nearby labeled "parked for now". clients seem to respond to pictures faster than paragraphs.
- maybe a calm look back at the brief together around the middle of a project, noting what has grown and what has shrunk. growth isn't bad. unexamined growth is.
- could the studio keep a private journal of how requests tend to arrive? patterns might show which kinds of projects drift in which directions, and where in the process.
- is there a way to make the cost of added work visible without it feeling like a bill? maybe describe it in terms of what would have to move or make room.
- think about "trade" language more than "add" language. when something comes in, something else might go out, or the shape shifts.
- an experiment: short reflections after a project wraps, put on paper while memory is fresh, kept small enough that they actually get made.
- test whether a friendly checklist for the opening conversation helps or whether it makes the conversation stiff.
- what would it look like to treat the edges of a project as a design problem in their own right? edges have texture, and they can be drawn well or badly.

small reminders

- the goal isn't to defend a perimeter. it's to keep the work good and the relationship intact. boundaries are a way of caring for the work and the people.
- good boundaries make room for generosity rather than replacing it.
- people remember how a hard conversation felt longer than they remember what was said.
- a plain, gentle sentence beats a clever one.
- rest matters. a studio stretched thin makes worse judgment calls and says yes to things it shouldn't.
- not every extra is a threat. some are the best part of the job, and a studio that treats all of them as threats loses the fun.
- curiosity beats suspicion. assume good faith, stay clear about the shape of the work, and let the rest settle.
