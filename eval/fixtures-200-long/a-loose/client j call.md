general working notes

**approach**

- the work goes better when the brief gets talked through in plain language before anyone opens a design file. most of the trouble in past projects traced back to a fuzzy idea of what the thing was supposed to do for the people using it, not to anything visual.
- early conversations are mostly questions. who is this for, what should it feel like, what would make it a miss. the answers are rarely what the client opens with, so it helps to keep asking in slightly different ways until the real answer shows up.
- a loose sketch beats a polished mockup for getting reactions. people respond more honestly to something rough because they don't feel they're criticizing finished work.
- being small is an advantage. there's no hand-off between departments, no game of telephone, and the person who hears the feedback is the person who acts on it. worth protecting that by keeping the process light.
- the best projects have a client who is curious about the process rather than anxious about the outcome. hard to engineer, but a relaxed tone in early calls seems to help.

**process, roughly**

- listen, then restate. after a call, writing the conversation back in my own words catches misunderstandings while they're still cheap to fix.
- gather references widely before narrowing. the temptation is to grab the obvious inspiration and run with it, but the interesting ideas usually come from sources that have nothing to do with the industry in question.
- explore broadly, then prune hard. the first idea that feels good is often just the most familiar. pushing past it tends to turn up something with more character.
- keep a running file of rejected directions. clients sometimes circle back to something that was set aside, and it's useful to have it intact rather than half remembered.
- show work in context whenever possible. a logo on a blank page tells you little; the same logo on a real page, a real screen, a real piece of packaging tells you a lot.
- leave room for the work to be wrong. a draft that has been allowed to fail privately is stronger than one that was defended too early.
- write down why a choice was made, not just what it was. months later the reasoning is the part that gets lost, and it's the part that settles arguments.

**things to keep in mind**

- scheduling is a design problem too. the quality of the work depends on how much attention each project gets, and attention is the scarcest thing in a studio this size. overlapping commitments quietly erode it.
- a client who is happy to wait is a gift, and it's easy to take that patience for granted. keeping them warm without pestering them is a small art: occasional useful notes, an interesting article, a thought that shows the project hasn't been forgotten.
- gaps in the calendar are not wasted. they're where the thinking happens, and rushing to fill them usually means the next project starts tired.
- the handoff between projects deserves care. closing out one set of files, archiving properly, and clearing the desk mentally makes the next beginning cleaner.
- ambiguity in scope is the most common source of friction. it's better to name the edges of a project out loud than to let them blur and discover the disagreement late.
- feedback that arrives as a feeling ("it's not quite right") is still real feedback. the job is translating it into something workable, not demanding better vocabulary from the client.
- the invisible parts of a project matter: file organization, naming, documentation, consistency. nobody praises them, but everybody suffers without them.
- resist the urge to over-explain a design. if it needs a long defense, something is off, either in the work or in the framing.
- keep the tone of correspondence human. clients are often nervous about spending money on something they can't fully picture, and warmth goes a long way toward easing that.
- pay attention to energy. some projects give energy back and some take it. neither is bad, but it helps to notice the pattern and balance them.

**open-ended ideas**

- a short "how I work" page that sets expectations before a project begins, written in the same voice as these notes, not in contract language. it could cut down on the repeated explanations.
- a reading list for clients: a few thoughtful pieces on design, brand, or communication that prepare them to give better feedback. needs to feel generous, not homework.
- a way to capture small observations from daily life, such as signs, labels, interfaces that annoyed or delighted. a loose collection like that could feed future projects without any extra effort.
- a lightweight retrospective after each project, just a page or less. what surprised me, what dragged, what I'd do differently. over time the patterns could become a quiet guide.
- experiment with presenting concepts as stories rather than as slides. a narrative about a person encountering the finished thing might land better than a list of features.
- consider a habit of making things that no client asked for. small personal projects keep the craft sharp and often seed ideas that end up in paid work.
- explore whether a shared reference board with clients, open from the start, would make feedback more collaborative and less like a series of verdicts.
- think about how to make the studio's own materials less of an afterthought. the cobbler's children problem is real, and the studio's presence says something about the quality of the work.
- a standing list of questions to ask at the start of any engagement, refined each time. something like what has been tried before, what is off limits, who else needs to approve, what does success look like when nobody is watching.
- a quiet way to learn from other disciplines: architecture, editorial work, theater, cooking. the habits of other crafts often point at blind spots in this one.

**loose thoughts**

- good design is mostly good decisions made in the right order. when the order is wrong, even strong work feels forced.
- restraint is underrated. a lot of the best work comes from taking things away until what's left can't be improved by subtraction.
- clients rarely remember the process; they remember how they felt during it. calm, clear, and responsive goes further than clever.
- being honest about limits, whether of time, budget, or my own skills, has almost always built more trust than bluffing ever did.
- the feeling of dread before a blank page usually fades once there's any mark on it at all. the trick is lowering the stakes of the very first mark.
- it's worth remembering why this kind of studio exists in the first place: to make work with care, in a way larger shops often can't. that should shape what gets taken on and how it gets done.
- keep notes like these messy and honest. they're more useful as a place to think out loud than as a polished record.
