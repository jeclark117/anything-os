general working notes

Loose thoughts on how I like to shape write-ups of finished work. Nothing here is a rule, just habits that seem to hold up when I look back at what worked.

**Starting points**

- Start from the reader, not the project. A prospective customer skimming a portfolio between other tasks wants to know whether this studio can handle a situation like theirs. Everything else can wait.
- Tell it as a story with a clear shape. People remember a turn in the plot far better than a list of deliverables.
- Keep the voice plain. If a line would sound strange said out loud across a table, rewrite it.
- Show the thinking, not just the polished frames. Rough sketches, abandoned directions, and awkward in-between states make the finished piece feel earned.
- Be specific about what was hard. Vague praise for a smooth collaboration says nothing; a concrete friction that got worked through says plenty.
- Credit the client's side of the effort. Good outcomes usually come from a partnership, and the write-up should read that way instead of like a heroic tale about the studio.
- Write for the curious stranger, not for the people who were in the room. Anything that needs insider context should either be explained or cut.

**Process notes**

Gather raw material while a project is still warm. Screenshots, early scribbles, messages where a direction changed, before-and-after comparisons: all of it vanishes quickly if nobody saves it. A messy folder of artifacts beats a clean memory every time.

When drafting, it helps to write the ending of the story before the middle. Knowing where the narrative lands makes it easier to decide which details earn a place.

Separate "what happened" from "what it means." An initial pass should just be the facts in plain order. A later pass can decide the meaning and trim whatever doesn't serve it.

Read it back as a skeptic. Where would a doubtful reader push back? Add evidence at that spot, or cut the claim.

Check every claim against something real. If an outcome can't be pointed to, soften the wording or leave it out. A modest true statement is better than an impressive shaky one.

Images carry more than copy. Pick visuals that explain the change without a caption, then write captions that add something the picture can't.

Leave breathing room in the layout of the page itself. The write-up is a sample of the studio's taste as much as a record of the work.

**Things to keep in mind**

- Confidentiality is a spectrum. Some material is fine to show openly, some needs blurring or abstracting, some should stay out entirely. When unsure, lean toward showing less and explaining more.
- A client's comfort with visibility can shift as their circumstances shift. Keep the line of communication easy and friendly so that a change of heart never feels awkward to voice.
- Requests land better when they're concrete. Showing exactly what would appear and where usually gets a clearer answer than a vague ask.
- Be careful with metrics. Figures shared in confidence may not be shareable, and a win that belongs to a whole team shouldn't be framed as the studio's achievement solely.
- Attribution matters for collaborators too: freelancers, photographers, writers, developers who touched the work. Give credit generously and accurately.
- Accessibility applies to the write-up itself: contrast, alt text, readable type sizes, sensible heading order. Practice what gets preached.
- Work ages. Something that looked fresh in its moment can look dated, so frame it in its context so the reader judges it fairly.
- Avoid jargon that only designers use. The reader might be an operations lead, a founder, or a marketer.
- Don't oversell. Readers can smell it, and it makes the genuine strengths harder to believe.
- The best write-ups also quietly filter. They attract the kind of project the studio wants more of and repel the kind it doesn't.

**Craft and voice**

Short paragraphs, active verbs, concrete nouns. When a paragraph starts to sprawl, it usually holds competing ideas fighting for space.

Headings should work as a skim path. A reader taking in only the headings should still catch the gist.

Avoid the trap of narrating the software or the method. Readers care about what changed for the client's customers, not which menu got clicked.

Use quotes from the client sparingly and only when they say something the studio couldn't say itself. A glowing adjective is not evidence; a specific observation is.

Let a little personality through. A studio this small has an advantage here: the voice can be a real voice rather than a committee's average.

**Questions worth asking myself**

- What would the client's customer say changed?
- What was the real strain underneath the brief, as opposed to the one stated on the surface?
- What did we try that failed, and what did that teach?
- Where did the constraints help?
- Would a stranger understand why this mattered without meeting the people involved?
- Is there a detail here that's interesting only to me?
- What am I leaving out because it's awkward, and is that actually the most interesting part?

**Open-ended ideas**

- A recurring structure for write-ups so they feel like a family, with room to break it when a project demands.
- A short reflective note at the end of each: what I'd do differently, what surprised me. Candid hindsight builds trust.
- A "behind the scenes" strand, looser and more personal than the polished pieces, for process-minded visitors.
- Attach a small artifact the reader can take away: a checklist, a template, a question list drawn from the project.
- Record a casual voice walkthrough of the work and transcribe it as a rough draft. Spoken explanations seem to come out clearer than typed ones.
- Invite the client to add lines in their own words. Hearing from their side adds texture.
- Try a visual timeline that shows detours and reversals rather than only milestones.
- Keep a running swipe file of other studios' write-ups that felt convincing, with notes on why, without copying.
- Consider a quiet archive for work that doesn't make the public cut but still teaches something.
- Borrow structure from other fields that document their work well: architecture, documentary film, research, cooking.
- Test a draft on a friendly outsider and watch where their eyes slow or where they ask a question. Those are the gaps.
- Explore a format where the outcome appears up front as a plain image and the story unfolds backward to explain how it came to be.
- Try writing a version in the form of a letter to a future client facing a similar situation.
- Look at how failures are discussed in other disciplines, and whether a studio can share a stumble without losing credibility.

**Stray thoughts**

Good write-ups seem to come from studios that enjoy reflecting, so maybe the habit of reflecting matters more than the format. It might help to keep a notebook for small observations during a project, the kind that feel too minor to mention but later turn out to be the heart of the story.

Also worth wondering: how much polish is right? Too little reads careless, too much reads sterile, and the sweet spot probably moves depending on the audience. And there's the question of whether the work should speak for itself at all, or whether a little guidance makes the reader's job easier. Probably the latter, as long as the guidance stays light.
