general working notes

Mostly reminders about how the work should feel, since how it feels ends up showing in how it looks.

**Approach**

- start with the problem, not the deliverable. Plenty of stress comes from building before the question is clear.
- keep the brief in plain language. If it can't be said simply, it isn't understood yet.
- treat a bad mood about a project as information. It usually points at vague scope, a missing answer, or mismatched expectations.
- make room for ugly drafts. The rough version shows where the real idea is hiding.
- prefer fewer, better directions over a big spread of options. A pile of choices mostly hands the decision fatigue to the client.
- let some work be quiet. Not every project needs to be a showpiece.
- good enough and delivered cleanly beats perfect and resented.

**Process**

Everything goes better with a loose rhythm: absorb, wander, sketch, narrow, refine, hand over. The wandering part gets squeezed first when things are busy, and it's the part that makes the work feel like mine. Worth guarding.

- sketch on paper before touching the screen. It slows things down in a good way, and the screen work afterward is calmer.
- write down the reasoning as I go, in a few plain sentences, so presenting later is easy and I'm not inventing the story under pressure.
- keep a running file of leftover ideas. Unused directions often seed something better later, and they're easier to let go of when they have a home.
- group the shallow stuff (admin, file cleanup, replies) into a small corner of the work instead of letting it leak everywhere.
- finish things properly: tidy layers, sensible file names, clear handoff notes. Future me is also a client.
- step away from a stuck problem physically. Walk, wash dishes, stare at a wall. The answer tends to show up sideways.
- when feedback arrives, let it sit before reacting. The immediate reaction is mostly defensive and rarely useful.

**Things to keep in mind**

- a solo studio means every gap in energy is a gap in the business. Rest isn't a luxury, it's maintenance.
- tiredness disguises itself as lack of talent. When everything looks mediocre, check sleep and food before questioning the work.
- the loudest request isn't always the most important. Urgency and importance get confused constantly, and the confusion is expensive.
- limits are easier to hold when they're stated plainly and kindly, without a long apology attached.
- clients mostly respond well to clarity. Vague availability breeds vague expectations, and vague expectations breed resentment on both sides.
- comparison is a leak. Scrolling through other studios' output while already drained rarely inspires anything. It mostly shrinks things.
- watch for the early signs: irritability about small stuff, dread when a message arrives, losing interest in type or color or whatever used to be fun. These show up well before the actual crash.
- quiet stretches in the work aren't failures. They're also not an invitation to fill every gap with side projects.
- keep some work personal and unbilled, just to remember why this was appealing in the beginning.
- money worry is what pushes toward overcommitting. Worth looking at what builds a cushion, so choices aren't made from fear.
- the home-and-work overlap blurs fast. Without some signal that the work is over, it just keeps humming in the background.

**On clients and communication**

Most friction traces back to something unsaid. A project goes sideways not because the design was wrong but because nobody named what "finished" looked like. Leaning toward over-communicating early, in simple words, saves a lot of awkward repair later. It also helps to separate the person from the request. A demanding message usually comes from someone under their own pressure, and replying to the pressure rather than the tone keeps things from escalating.

There's also the question of how much of myself to put into each project. Caring is the whole point, but caring without edges turns into absorbing everybody else's anxiety. A bit of professional distance isn't coldness. It's what lets the care last.

**Notes on pacing**

Energy isn't flat, and pretending it is causes trouble. Some stretches are generous and fast, and others are slow and foggy. The trick may be matching the kind of work to the kind of energy: heavy conceptual thinking when the mind is clear, production and cleanup when it isn't. Fighting that grain costs more than it gains.

Recovery also seems to work better when it's real. Scrolling is not rest. Rest looks more like being outside, being bored, making food slowly, or letting the mind idle with no input. The work after real rest looks different, looser and braver, and it's hard to fake that with extra effort.

**Open-ended ideas**

- a "minimum viable day" template: the least that counts as a good enough working day when energy is low, so a bad stretch doesn't turn into guilt.
- a short reflection after each project: what drained me, what fed me, what I'd change about the setup. Might reveal patterns across very different jobs.
- a loose energy log, nothing fancy, just a mark for how a day felt, to spot links between kinds of work and mood.
- stock phrases for clarifying scope early, so the awkward part is already written and doesn't need courage in the moment.
- a reading and looking list that has nothing to do with design: gardening books, old maps, field guides, odd museums. Fresh input from outside the field.
- trading notes with other independent designers, informally, about how they handle the lonely parts of the job.
- a small physical ritual that marks the end of a working session: closing a notebook, switching off a lamp, putting the laptop in a drawer.
- working inside deliberate constraints now and then, such as a tight palette or very few typefaces. Constraints reduce decision load and often sharpen the result.
- phone in another room for long stretches. See what the attention feels like afterward.
- a portfolio that shows process and restraint more than sheer volume.
- wondering what a calm studio looks like visually, and whether that could inform how the studio presents itself.
- a folder of kind messages from past clients, to open when the work feels thankless.
- explore whether certain kinds of projects reliably give energy back, and whether the mix of work could lean toward those without losing range.

**Loose questions**

What does sustainable ambition look like for a small studio? Is growth even the right frame, or is depth? How much of the pressure is external, and how much is a habit of proving something? Which parts of the job would still be enjoyable if nobody ever saw the result?

None of this is a system, really. It's a pile of nudges toward working in a way that can keep going, and it probably needs revisiting whenever the work starts to feel heavy again.
