monthly review aug 2026

general working notes

**approach**

i like to start by listening more than designing. the brief is rarely the real problem. the real problem usually hides in a sentence the client says offhand while talking about something else, so it's worth writing those sentences down word for word. they carry the actual language of the business, and language does most of the heavy lifting in a design anyway.

habits that seem to help:

- ask what the project is replacing and what people disliked about it
- ask what a good outcome looks like in plain words, not metrics
- ask who gets embarrassed if it goes badly, because that person has opinions
- let silences run in early conversations, people fill them with the truth
- keep a running list of words the client uses for their own work, and borrow them

working solo means i'm the strategist, the designer, the builder, the bookkeeper, and the person who answers the door. the trick is noticing which hat i'm wearing so i don't make bookkeeping calls with designer feelings, or the other way around.

**process**

the process is less a ladder and more a loop. gather, sketch, test against reality, go back to gathering. the loop shrinks as things get clearer. if it stops shrinking, the problem is probably mislabeled and i should step back rather than push harder.

a rough shape that holds up most of the time:

- discovery: conversations, existing materials, anything the client already loves or hates
- sketching: cheap, ugly, fast, on paper where possible, because paper doesn't tempt me to polish
- structure before surface: content, hierarchy, and flow get sorted before colour and type get any attention
- surface: type, colour, spacing, imagery, motion, roughly in that order of importance
- build: keep it boring under the hood so it survives without me
- handover: write down how it works in language a non-technical person can follow, and leave it somewhere easy to find

a question worth keeping nearby: what would make this feel inevitable? the best work feels like it couldn't have been any other way, even though it very much could have.

**on copy and content**

content is the part that gets postponed and then ends up deciding everything. placeholder text makes designs look better than they are and hides problems until late. use real words as early as possible, even rough ones. if the client can't supply them, drafting them is a genuine service, not a favour.

**on presenting work**

got a testimonial

show the reasoning before the visuals. a sentence about the problem, then the response, then the picture. people judge a layout differently once they understand what it's trying to do. keep the options limited. too many options hands the decision-making back to the client, who hired me to carry some of it.

**things to keep in mind**

- scope creeps in through kindness. a small favour feels free in the moment and then becomes the expectation. notice the favour before it becomes a habit.
- clients rarely hate the design; they hate surprises. showing work early and rough removes most of the surprise.
- feedback is data about a person's worry, not an instruction. "make the logo bigger" often means "i'm not sure people will notice us."
- write down why a choice was made while the reasoning is fresh. future me won't remember, and future me will be annoyed.
- accessibility isn't a layer on top. contrast, focus states, readable sizes, and sensible structure are part of the material.
- performance is a design decision too. heavy pages are a kind of rudeness.
- test on the worst device and the worst connection available, not the nicest setup on the desk.
- leave room. empty space is doing work, and clients sometimes read it as unfinished. explain it before they have to ask.
- keep files tidy as if a stranger will inherit them, because eventually a stranger might.

**when stuck**

when a layout isn't working it's usually because the content isn't clear, not because the grid is wrong. try rewriting the headline. try deleting the thing that feels most precious. try explaining the page out loud to an imaginary friend; the parts that make me stumble are the parts that need work.

changing medium helps too. if it's stuck on screen, print it. if it's stuck in colour, drop it to greyscale. if it's stuck in type, set it in something ugly and see what survives.

**the solo studio reality**

there's a rhythm to being a studio of just me that's hard to describe. the quiet is useful for deep work and dangerous for judgement, because nobody pushes back. i need substitutes for the colleague down the hall: a habit of sleeping on decisions, a trusted friend who will say "hm" at the right moment, and a folder of work i admire to calibrate against.

energy matters more than clock-watching. protect the stretches where thinking is sharp and don't spend them on email. admin expands to fill whatever space is left for it. batching it helps, and so does making it slightly more pleasant than it needs to be.

**relationships**

- good clients tend to become good references without being asked. the way to get there is mostly to be easy to work with and honest about limits
- saying "i don't know yet" builds more trust than a confident guess that falls apart
- keep in touch lightly with people after a project wraps. a short note with something genuinely useful beats a generic check-in

**open-ended ideas**

- a small library of reusable page structures, annotated with when each works and when it falls apart. not templates, more like memory aids
- a plain-language guide for clients on what happens behind the scenes, so the process feels less like magic and more like craft
- a post-mortem page for every project: what surprised me, what i'd do differently, what i'd steal from myself
- experiments with type combinations that break the rules on purpose, then writing down why some of them work
- a collection of before-and-after stories that focus on the reasoning, not the visuals
- short essays about small design problems as a way to think. some might be worth sharing, most are just for me
- making handover documents feel like part of the product rather than an afterthought
- what would a studio look like if it deliberately took a narrower range of work and went deeper on each piece? not sure it fits, but the question is interesting
- a quiet "office hours" format that could replace some of the back-and-forth in email
- a personal swipe file organized by feeling instead of by industry: calm, urgent, warm, serious
- thinking about sound and motion as quieter parts of a brand that most small projects ignore

**questions i keep circling**

- how much explaining is too much when presenting work?
- when is a client's taste a constraint and when is it a gift?
- what's the right amount of polish for something that will be changed by its owner after handover?
- how do i stay curious about projects that look similar on paper?
- what does good rest look like for a person who enjoys the work this much?

**closing thought**

most of this comes down to paying attention: to the client's words, to the user's confusion, to my own tiredness. the craft is mostly noticing, and the rest is cleaning up after the noticing.
