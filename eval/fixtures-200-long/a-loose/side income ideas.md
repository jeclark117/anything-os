side income ideas

general working notes

stuff to keep in the back of my head whenever the extra-income brainstorming comes up. nothing here is a plan, just a pile of thinking.

**approach**

- the studio is the engine. anything on the side has to feed off work that already exists instead of fighting it for attention. the best side projects are byproducts. they fall out of client work almost for free, because the thinking was already paid for.
- be suspicious of anything that needs a new skill, a new audience, and a new set of habits all at the same moment. that's not a side project, that's an entirely different company in a costume.
- energy matters more than hours. a side thing that drains the same creative tank as paid work quietly makes both worse. worth noticing which activities refill the tank and which empty it.
- aim for things that keep earning without needing me in the room, but be honest that nothing is truly passive. there's always upkeep, support, and the odd grumpy email.
- small and finished beats big and imagined. a modest thing that exists teaches more than an ambitious thing that lives in a sketchbook.
- keep the studio's taste intact. if a side project would embarrass the portfolio, it's the wrong side project, no matter how well it might sell.

**process**

when an idea shows up, run it through a loose set of questions before getting attached to it:

- who is it for, specifically? "designers" is too fuzzy. "people who just started freelancing and dread making a proposal look decent" is closer. the sharper the picture, the easier everything downstream gets.
- what problem does it remove for them? saving effort is a real benefit. so is saving embarrassment, or giving someone a head start so they stop staring at a blank canvas.
- what already exists, and what does it get wrong? usually the gap isn't a missing product, it's a missing point of view. lots of stuff out there is competent and soulless.
- can it be made mostly from material that already sits in old project folders? if yes, that's a green flag. if it needs to be built from nothing, it better be exciting.
- what happens after it's made? updates, questions, broken links, people who don't read instructions. the afterlife of a thing is where the real cost hides.
- would it still feel worthwhile if it earned very little? if the answer is no, it might just be a stressful way to chase money.

then, to test without overbuilding:

- make a rough version and put it in front of a few trusted people. watch where they get confused rather than listening to what they say they think.
- write the description before building the thing. if the pitch is boring to write, the thing will be boring to make.
- sketch the smallest possible version that still delivers a real result. then ask what could be cut from that.
- keep a running scraps file of half-ideas, screenshots, phrases, and client questions that came up more than they should have. repeated questions are basically free market research.
- document the making as it happens. process notes, rough drafts, and mistakes often turn into the marketing, and they're far easier to capture in the moment than to reconstruct afterward.

**things to keep in mind**

- sell templates
- the webflow workshop
- print shop for posters

- clients come first, at least in terms of how the studio is perceived. side work can't leak into response speed or quality on paid projects. if it starts to, that's a signal, not a challenge to push through.
- rights and ownership. before reusing anything from client work, check whether it's actually mine to reuse. a lot of the best raw material belongs to someone else, and a clean workaround is almost always available if the thinking is done early.
- the audience is a slow thing to grow and a fast thing to lose. trust gets built by being useful in public over a long stretch, so anything that feels like a cash grab does damage out of proportion to what it earns.
- pricing is a design problem too. it communicates positioning, confidence, and who the thing is meant for. underpricing out of nerves tends to attract the most demanding customers and the least gratitude.
- taxes, invoices, licensing terms, refund policies. boring, necessary, and best handled before the first sale rather than after the first complaint. worth getting proper advice instead of guessing.
- avoid building a dependency on a platform that can change its rules overnight. owning the customer relationship, even in a modest way like an email list, is a quiet kind of insurance.
- watch for scope creep dressed up as ambition. every "while I'm at it" adds weight, and weight is what kills side projects.
- burnout from too many directions is real for a studio this size. there's nobody to hand things off to. each new thing added has to displace something, or it just piles up on top of the person doing everything.
- be careful about the trap of preparing forever. tidy branding, a perfect logo, and a cleverly named folder structure can feel like progress while nothing ships.

**open-ended ideas**

these aren't about any particular item on the list above, just general directions that might be worth poking at:

- teach what the studio already knows how to do, in whatever form suits the material: a walkthrough, a recorded session, a written breakdown of a real project with the awkward bits left in. people tend to value seeing how decisions get made more than seeing polished results.
- turn recurring client headaches into small resources. if the same confusion comes up repeatedly during onboarding, that confusion is probably shared by many people who never became clients.
- there's a possible overlap between physical and digital. things people can hold change how they relate to the studio's taste. a well-made object can do more for reputation than a page of case studies.
- collaboration might beat going solo. a partnership with a writer, an illustrator, a developer, or a photographer could produce something none of them would make alone, and the audience overlap is often generous.
- limited runs and small batches create a nice kind of honesty. nothing sits unsold in a corner, and the maker can learn what resonated before committing to more.
- consider the role of free work as a doorway. a generous freebie that's genuinely good can do the job of advertising, and it filters for people who value craft.
- it could be interesting to flip the usual direction: instead of asking what to sell, ask what the studio is unusually good at that nobody has thought to package. sometimes the answer is a way of seeing, a habit of critique, or a knack for explaining things plainly.
- a recurring newsletter, a small archive, or a public notebook could become its own asset over time, even if it never earns directly. attention built there might make every other idea easier to land.

**loose closing thoughts**

none of this has to become anything. the point of keeping notes like these is to make future thinking less random. when a new idea turns up, it's useful to have a few sharp questions ready rather than a vague feeling of "that sounds good." and when an idea doesn't survive the questions, that's a perfectly fine outcome. cheap rejection is one of the most valuable things a notebook can provide.

worth remembering that the studio's real product is judgment. whatever gets built on the side should carry some of that judgment, in the choices made, the things left out, and the care taken over small details nobody asked for. that's the part people actually pay for, even when they think they're paying for the file.
