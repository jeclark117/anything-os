general working notes

**approach**

start with the problem, not the look. before any sketching, try to write down what the thing is supposed to do for the person using it, in plain words, like explaining it to a friend who doesn't care about design. if the explanation comes out mushy, the work will come out mushy too.

some habits that keep showing up:

- ask what's being replaced. very little gets built on empty ground. there's usually an old habit, an old logo, a drawer full of leftover assets. knowing what's already there saves a lot of reinventing.
- collect references wide, then throw most of them out. the useful ones rarely come from the same field as the project. packaging can teach a website something, signage can teach a book something.
- sketch badly and quickly on paper before touching the screen. screens make everything look more finished than it is, which makes it harder to change direction.
- keep early rounds rough on purpose. rough work invites candid reactions, polished work invites polite ones.
- write the reasoning down next to the visuals. a layout with a sentence about why it exists is easier to defend, and easier to abandon when the reason turns out to be thin.

**process**

the loop is basically look, make, step away, look again. stepping away isn't laziness. work reads differently after a break, and problems that were invisible suddenly shout. a decent rule of thumb: if i can't stop staring at a detail, it's probably the wrong detail to be staring at.

work from big to small. structure and hierarchy before type choices, type choices before color, color before texture and finishing touches. when that order gets flipped the result tends to be pretty but confused, like a nicely dressed person who can't answer a simple question.

limits help. a narrow palette, a restrained set of typefaces, a strict grid. constraints do a lot of the thinking so the energy can go into the parts that matter.

when stuck, change the medium. if it's stuck on screen, print it. if it's stuck in color, flip it to black and white. if the layout won't resolve, cover it with tracing paper and redraw only what matters. the act of redrawing tends to show what was never essential.

also worth remembering that "finished" is a feeling that arrives before the work is actually ready. a last pass with fresh eyes, looking only for small inconsistencies, nearly always turns something up.

**working with the person on the other side**

being a studio of just me means the relationship is the product as much as the files are. clarity beats charm. people relax when they know what's happening next, even if what's happening next is slow.

- explain choices in the language of the client's goals, not design vocabulary. "this makes the important thing easier to find" lands better than "this improves hierarchy."
- show less. presenting a wide spread of options usually produces a muddle of preferences. a tight, considered set gives people something real to react to.
- feedback is data about the viewer, not a verdict on the work. when a person says they dislike something, the useful question is what they were expecting to see.
- vague feedback deserves a gentle follow-up rather than a guess. "make it pop" usually means something specific hiding underneath.
- be careful with surprises. a bold departure is exciting for me and sometimes alarming for them. bring people along with the reasoning.
- boundaries are part of the service. clear edges around what's included make the generous moments feel generous rather than expected.
- tone matters in writing as much as in design. short, warm, specific messages beat long defensive ones.

**keeping the house in order**

- name files like a stranger will have to find them later, because the stranger is me, later, tired.
- keep source files and exports separate, and always keep an untouched original.
- write short handoff notes at the end of a project: what typefaces, what colors, what to avoid, where things live.
- records of what comes in and goes out are easier to keep tidy when they're handled little and often rather than in a heap. same goes for receipts, invoices, and filing. a boring system that gets used beats a clever system that doesn't.
- keep a running list of small annoyances in the workflow. patterns show up and point at what's worth fixing.
- back things up in separate places, so no ordinary mishap can take everything at the same moment.
- leave breathing room in the calendar for the work nobody sees: tidying, learning, answering the awkward emails.

**things to keep in mind**

- good design is often invisible. the best outcome is a person finishing their task without noticing anything.
- accessibility isn't a finishing step. contrast, legibility, and clear structure are part of the foundation.
- trends age fast, clear communication ages slowly. borrow from trends sparingly and with intent.
- rest is part of the craft. tired work is recognizable in the details: spacing drifts, alignments slip, copy gets lazy.
- the urge to over-explain a design usually signals it isn't working yet.
- saying no politely is a skill, and it protects the quality of everything else.
- curiosity outside design feeds the work: cooking, walking, listening to strangers talk on public transport, reading things with no connection to the job.
- comparing myself to larger studios is a trap. the advantage of being small is speed, directness, and care, and none of those need a big team.
- when a project feels stuck, it's often the brief that's stuck, not the design. go back and reread what was actually asked.

**open-ended ideas**

- what if every project ended with a short note to self: what surprised me, what i'd repeat, what i'd avoid? over time that becomes a private handbook.
- a small library of reusable grids and spacing systems, tuned to my own taste, so setup is quick and attention goes to the interesting parts.
- a plain-language page describing how the studio works, for people who are curious but not ready to reach out. might cut down on awkward early conversations.
- a "dead ideas" folder. rejected directions often contain the seed of something later.
- a self-initiated project with no brief, just to remember what it feels like to answer only to myself.
- experiments with printed matter: a small booklet of process sketches, something tactile to hand over.
- ways to make scoping feel less like guessing. maybe describing work in terms of complexity and uncertainty rather than just size.
- is there a way to show the thinking, not just the result, in portfolio pieces? case studies that admit false starts might be more candid and more useful than glossy ones.
- trade critique with other small studios. not sure what form that takes. a loose habit of swapping work-in-progress could keep things from getting insular.
- study how other disciplines document process, like architects, editors, and cooks, and borrow the good parts.
- a visual diary of everyday design that quietly works: door handles, ticket machines, instructions on bottles. a way to train the eye on function rather than style.
- a standing question to ask at the start of any project: what would make this unnecessary? sometimes the best answer is a smaller deliverable.

loose closing thought: the work gets better when the system around it is calm. most of the above is really about making room for attention, and protecting it from noise, admin, and my own second-guessing.
