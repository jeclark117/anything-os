general notes on working with clients

approach

- write like a person, not a system. clients can smell boilerplate from a long way off, and the whole point of staying small is that the relationship feels personal.
- lead with what the reader needs to do or know. context comes after, and only as much as actually helps.
- keep the voice consistent across everything that leaves the studio: proposals, messages, shared files, even the names of exported assets. tone is part of the work, not a wrapper around it.
- assume the reader is busy and slightly distracted. a message that survives being read on a phone between other things is a good message.
- plain words over design jargon. people hire a designer so they don't have to learn the vocabulary.
- warmth and clarity aren't in competition. most of the time the warmest thing you can do is be easy to understand.

how a good message is built

- an opening line that says why the message exists. no throat clearing.
- the ask or the news, given its own space. if something is needed from the reader, it shouldn't be hiding in the middle of a paragraph.
- a short note on what happens after they respond, so they never wonder whether they're blocking something.
- a close that sounds like me, not like a sign-off generator.
- the subject line gets written last. it's much easier to name a message once its point is clear.
- if a message needs a lot of scrolling, it's probably doing too many jobs. split it up.

process thoughts

- it helps to separate the parts that are the same every time from the parts that are specific to the project. the shared parts can be written carefully and polished until they shine. the specific parts deserve fresh attention. the danger is letting the polished parts crowd out the specific ones.
- leave a clearly marked gap in any reusable text for a personal line, something that proves I actually looked at their project. if the gap is easy to skip, it will get skipped, so make it a little awkward to skip.
- read each message back as if I were the recipient with no background. if it sounds like a form letter, it is one.
- keep a running scrap file of phrases that worked and phrases that landed badly. patterns show up quickly once they're written down.
- when a client goes quiet, the cause is usually friction rather than disinterest. making the next step tiny and obvious tends to help more than adding urgency.
- save drafts of difficult messages and look at them again after stepping away. the tone problems are almost always visible with a bit of distance.
- store reusable text somewhere that's quick to reach. if it takes effort to find, it won't be used, and then it's just clutter.

things to keep in mind

- clients are often not experts and often a little nervous about investing in something they can't fully judge. clarity is a kindness.
- polite is not the same as soft. firm and warm can live in the same sentence.
- avoid apologizing for things that don't need an apology. over-apologizing quietly erodes confidence in the work.
- avoid passive phrasing when something is owed. "it would be appreciated if" is weaker than a direct, friendly ask.
- be careful with humor. it travels badly in text, especially with someone who doesn't know the studio yet.
- when attaching or linking something, say what it is and what to do with it. a bare link is a small tax on the reader.
- tone shifts depending on how the relationship feels. looser with people who joke back, tighter with people who write formally. mirror a little, don't mimic.
- messages are the paper trail. if memory differs later, the thread is the record, so be precise about what was said without sounding legalistic.
- never let a message sit half-written in a drafts folder for so long that the moment has passed. a slightly imperfect message sent in good time usually beats a perfect one that arrives awkwardly late.
- avoid exclamation marks as a substitute for real enthusiasm. use them sparingly so they still mean something.
- watch for words that sound defensive. "as I mentioned" and "per my earlier message" can read as a small accusation even when meant neutrally.
- be consistent about how names, project titles and file names are written. small inconsistencies make a studio feel less careful than it is.

when things get tricky

- awkward admin messages are easier when the tone matches the rest of the relationship. a sudden switch to stiff formality reads as a signal that something has gone wrong.
- if a message needs to carry mildly bad news, state it plainly and early, then follow with what can be done. the reader's imagination is worse than the facts.
- when scope starts to drift, name it kindly and early. it's much easier to say "that's a lovely idea, and it's outside what we set up" than to say it after the extra work is done.
- for messages that go unanswered, each nudge should come at a slightly different angle so it doesn't read as nagging. maybe a shorter version, maybe a different question, maybe just a quick check that the message landed at all.
- if a client is upset, resist the urge to explain first. acknowledge what they're feeling, then explain.
- keep emotion out of the writing when I'm irritated. draft it, close it, and write the real version afterward.

open-ended ideas

- a small library of tone variants for the same message: warm, neutral, brisk. pick by gut depending on the person.
- a plain-language glossary clients can be pointed to, so jargon doesn't need explaining in every thread.
- a closing "what happens next" line as a recurring habit, worth testing to see whether it reduces follow-up questions or just adds noise.
- try writing messages as if explaining to a friend who knows nothing about design, then trim.
- think about whether a lightly branded look suits the studio or whether deliberately plain text feels more human. probably depends on the kind of client.
- make it easy for people to react to work by offering prompts instead of a blank invitation. some clients freeze at an open question and thrive with a structured one. worth noticing who prefers which.
- keep a short list of words the studio would never use, a sort of voice check before anything goes out.
- ask clients afterward how the communication felt, separate from how the work felt. they're often different answers.
- read old threads now and then to spot where misunderstandings began. the cause is usually a vague sentence, not a bad actor.
- a gentle ladder for follow-ups that moves from friendly to direct without ever turning cold.
- draft a few ready phrases for declining extra work gracefully, so the response doesn't have to be invented under pressure.
- consider how messages look when forwarded to someone else on the client side. the person who approves things is often not the person who writes to me, and the message has to make sense to them with no context.
- maybe record rough voice notes of tricky messages before writing them, to hear how they naturally sound. spoken phrasing is usually friendlier.

questions to chew on

- what does a client actually remember about working with the studio after the project is done? probably the feel of the communication more than any single deliverable.
- how much structure is too much for a solo studio before it starts to feel corporate?
- is there a way to make routine messages feel like part of the design experience rather than admin around it?
- where does consistency end and staleness begin? reusable text can calcify if it's never questioned.
- what would a client say is the easiest thing about working here, and how do I protect that?
