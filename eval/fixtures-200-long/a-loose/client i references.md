general working notes

approach

- start from the feel, not the form. what should it be like to walk past, to open the door, to pick the thing up. words before shapes, always
- references are a mood, not a spec. worth pulling apart what is really admired (proportion, wear, how the letters sit in their space) from what is incidental (the wall behind it, the weather in the photo, the angle of the shot)
- people usually love a reference for a reason they can't quite name. asking sideways helps: what would you change about it, what bugs you, what would feel like too much, what would make you wince
- look for the through-line in whatever a client collects. when different references share a quality, that quality is the brief, even if nobody wrote it down
- keep early sketches loose and a little ugly on purpose. polished work invites polite approval instead of real reactions
- aim for marks that hold up tiny and huge. test the awkward extremes early instead of leaving them for the end
- treat the client's vocabulary as data. if they keep saying "warm" or "solid" or "friendly", find out what those words look like to them specifically, because they rarely mean what they mean to me

process

- gather before judging. a big messy pile of reference, laid out where it can all be seen together, tells more than a tidy folder ever will
- sort the pile by quality rather than by source: warmth, heaviness, rhythm, roughness, restraint, formality. then look at which piles are thick and which are thin. the thin ones are often where the surprise is
- sketch on paper while the idea is still soft. the screen makes everything look finished too quickly, and finished things are hard to criticize honestly
- redraw the same letterform again and again. the early versions copy, the later ones start to have character. the character shows up in the redrawing, not the planning
- build a small kit of shapes and gestures that can repeat across everything: a curve, a terminal, a way of ending a stroke. consistency comes from a vocabulary, not from rules
- test in context as soon as there's anything to test. put the mark on a mockup of a door, a bag, a screen, a stamp. things that sing on a blank page can die in the real world
- squint test, distance test, bad-photo test, grubby-printout test. if it survives all of those it's probably sturdy
- step away from the work before judging it. fresh eyes catch lumpy spacing and accidental shapes that the tired version of me can't see
- write down the reasoning as it happens, even in scrappy fragments. the why evaporates quickly and it's the part that's hardest to reconstruct when a client circles back
- present direction with a story, not just an image. why this shape, why this restraint, what it's meant to do for the people who see it

things to keep in mind

- when a palette is restrained, contrast and spacing do the heavy lifting. there's nowhere to hide a weak decision about either

Let me fix that bullet in the final:

- anything drawn by hand is easy to fake badly. the tell is perfect repetition. if the same letter looks identical every time it appears, it reads as a font wearing a costume. real drawn work wobbles in believable places
- spacing between letters matters more than the letters themselves. fix the gaps before touching the shapes
- real objects have wear, shadow, dirt, and repairs. imitating that is a craft in itself, and heavy-handed distressing looks worse than none
- materials push back. paint, enamel, wood, metal, and glass each limit what a stroke can do. worth knowing the physical constraints before drawing something that can't be made
- readability beats charm when they fight. a lovely mark that can't be read from across the street is a drawing, not a mark
- reactions to work are about the work, not about me. translate "i don't like it" into "what is it doing that bothers you?" and the conversation gets easier
- watch for scope drift. a logo conversation that quietly turns into building a whole world isn't wrong, just worth noticing before it eats the whole project
- not every reference deserves equal weight. some are there because they were easy to find, or because they're pretty, not because they're right
- working solo means nobody pushes back, so build ways to push back on myself: a list of awkward questions, a walk, a print pinned up where it can be seen from across the room
- protect the boring parts: file naming, folder structure, export habits. future frustration lives there
- keep a record of dead ends. they're useful when a client circles back to something that was abandoned for good reason

open-ended ideas

- a small library of letter skeletons, basic structures to start from, so a new project doesn't begin from nothing
- try drawing with the wrong tool, the wrong hand, or with eyes mostly closed, just to shake loose habits
- photograph real storefront lettering while out walking. look at how paint builds up around edges, where it flakes, how a sign painter handled a tight corner
- a mood glossary to share with clients: opposing words (loud and hushed, crisp and soft, formal and scrappy) they can point to instead of struggling to describe
- an anti-brief: a list of things the work must never feel like. often easier to write than the positive version, and surprisingly clarifying
- swatch cards made of real material instead of screens, so color gets judged in actual light
- make the same mark in a different medium just to see what survives: carved, stitched, stenciled, stamped, printed badly
- imagine the mark aged. what does it look like faded, chipped, repainted by someone else? design for that, not just for the fresh version
- a reading list of ordinary things: old packaging, hardware labels, receipts, ticket stubs, handwritten price cards at markets
- a running list of questions to ask at the start of any project, pruned and sharpened as it gets used
- imagine describing the work out loud to a friend. if a client couldn't explain it in a sentence, it may be too fussy
- a "failed beautifully" folder, for experiments that missed the brief but are worth looking at again

loose thoughts

- restraint is a skill. the urge to add is nearly always stronger than the urge to remove
- the best feedback is often a reaction that arrives before thinking: a wince, a grin, a raised eyebrow
- constraints are friendly. a small palette, a narrow set of shapes, a tight material limit all make the work more decisive, not less
- what clients call taste is usually a tangle of memory and mood. treat it with care, even when it contradicts itself
- good work tends to look obvious afterward. that's the goal, and it's also why it's hard to explain how much effort went into it
- keep curiosity cheap. the more it costs to try something, the less gets tried
