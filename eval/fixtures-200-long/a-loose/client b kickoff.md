client b kickoff

working notes, general

**approach**

the work goes better when the early conversation is mostly asking and absorbing. clients tend to describe what they dislike much more fluently than what they want, so it helps to ask about things they've seen and winced at. those reactions are data. keep a loose pile of them: words they use, brands they envy, brands they'd hate to resemble. the pile gets more useful the more candid it is.

try to understand what the business actually does for people before thinking about how it should look. a mark that looks sharp but says nothing about what the company is for feels hollow once it's out in the world. ask what a customer would say about them to a friend. that line is usually closer to the real brand than anything in a pitch.

stay suspicious of adjectives. "modern", "clean", "friendly", "premium" mean different things to different people. when a client uses one, ask for something to point at: a photograph, a building, a piece of packaging, a song. then work out what they're responding to underneath. the spacing, the boldness, the warmth of the color, the amount of air.

also worth remembering that a brand is not just the visual layer. it's how emails sound, how invoices look, how the place feels when somebody walks in or lands on the page. the visuals are a way in, not the whole house.

**process**

- start small and rough. pencil and paper are still the fastest way to be wrong cheaply, and wrong ideas on paper often point toward the right one.
- explore wide before narrowing. the temptation is to fall for an early idea and spend the rest of the effort defending it. push past the obvious route, and past the clever route too.
- test marks at tiny sizes and huge sizes, in black only, upside down, reversed out of dark backgrounds, squinted at from across the room. if it survives all that, it has bones.
- look at it beside what competitors are using. not to copy, but to find the gap where the client can look different without looking strange.
- show work with a reason attached. a shape with a story is easier to respond to than a shape by itself, and it makes feedback more specific. "i don't like it" turns into "it feels too serious for how we talk to customers", which is something you can act on.
- keep notes on why things got rejected. discarded ideas have a habit of coming back as useful details elsewhere.
- let drafts sit before judging them. a mark that looked brilliant late at night often looks different on fresh eyes, in either direction.

**things to keep in mind**

- scope creeps quietly. a request that sounds tiny ("can you just adjust the...") is rarely tiny. worth noticing the pattern early and naming it kindly, without making the client feel policed.
- feedback from a group is noisy. look for the pattern under the comments instead of reacting to each comment. the person who talks loudest isn't always the person who decides.
- a contact sometimes changes their mind, and the mind they change to is frequently their boss's. try to learn who the real audience for the work is, and who has the final say.
- accessibility isn't a bonus layer. contrast, legibility, and color choices that don't depend on telling hues apart belong in the system from the start, because retrofitting is painful.
- files need to be organized as if a stranger will open them. layers named, colors named, fonts noted, spacing logic written down. the stranger is usually future me.
- licensing on typefaces and imagery is boring until it isn't. check before falling in love with a face.
- leave breathing room in the system. a rules document that's too rigid gets ignored, and one that's too loose gets abused. aim for rules that explain themselves.
- clients rarely have a library of assets in good shape. expect scattered files, low-resolution leftovers, and old versions floating around. asking early what already exists saves a lot of guessing.
- be careful with trends. borrowing a current look can feel safe and then feel dated quickly. ask whether a choice is serving the business or just serving the moment.
- keep the relationship warm. most friction in projects comes from silence, not disagreement.

**ideas, open-ended**

- what if the rules document were written like a conversation instead of a rulebook? short plain lines, right and wrong examples side by side, a bit of personality. people actually read things like that.
- a "what this brand is not" page. sometimes the boundaries communicate better than the instructions.
- build a small family of flexible shapes or patterns derived from the mark, so the identity has something to play with beyond the mark itself and a color swatch.
- think about how the mark behaves on a screen. a gentle reveal, a pause, a little movement might give it character even for a modest client.
- consider how the identity ages. ask whether a choice would still feel considered after a long stretch, or whether it's chasing a mood.
- try designing the worst applications early: a cramped receipt, a tiny browser icon, a rubber stamp, a sign seen from a moving car. if the system holds there, the lovely applications are easy.
- hand-made touches. a texture, an imperfect line, a scan of real paper. digital work can feel sterile, and a little human trace helps.
- look outside design for reference: architecture, textiles, signage, old maps, grocery aisle packaging, hand-painted shop fronts, the labels on hardware.
- build a mood board from the client's own materials (their photos, their products, their workspace) rather than from outside inspiration. it keeps the look rooted in something real.
- maybe explore a simplified version of the mark for the smallest uses and a more expressive version for large uses, as long as they obviously belong together.
- try describing the identity as a person at a party. how do they dress, how do they talk, what do they never say? it's silly but it surfaces gaps fast.
- a short "how to ask for changes" guide for the client, so feedback arrives in a form that's easier to use.

**working habits**

- step away from the screen when stuck. walking, washing dishes, anything that lets the back of the mind work.
- print things. color and scale behave differently on paper, and problems hide on screens.
- write a plain statement describing what the design is trying to do. if the statement is muddy, the design probably is too.
- keep a running list of questions for the client instead of sending them piecemeal. fewer interruptions, better answers.
- be straight about uncertainty. saying "i'm not sure yet, here's how i'd find out" earns more trust than bluffing.
- protect the quiet stretches for thinking and keep admin in its own pocket, so it doesn't leak into the creative work.
- save good references as they turn up, even without a project in mind. the best sources are usually found by accident.
- when the work feels stuck, shrink the problem. forget the whole identity and ask only how the mark should feel in a corner of a page.

**loose thoughts to close**

good identity work seems to come from restraint more than invention. most of the craft is deciding what to leave out, then making the remaining pieces feel inevitable. the best marks look obvious in hindsight, which is exactly why they're hard. the aim is something the client can grow into, that doesn't need explaining every time it appears, and that still feels a bit alive when it's printed small on the back of a box.

also: remember the people on the other end are usually nervous. they're handing over something personal, their business, and hoping it comes back looking like them. a bit of patience and clear explanation goes a long way, and so does admitting when an idea didn't work.
