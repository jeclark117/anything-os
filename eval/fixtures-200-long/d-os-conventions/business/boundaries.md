# Boundaries

approach

start with the problem behind the brief, not the brief itself. what gets asked for is usually a symptom, and the real thing is sitting a layer down. ask what the work needs to do for the people who will meet it, then work backward to what it should look like. if the answer to "what is this for" stays fuzzy, the visuals will be fuzzy too, no matter how polished they get.

prefer making over discussing. a rough sketch on the table beats a long paragraph of adjectives. people react to what is in front of them far better than to descriptions, and the reactions are the useful part.

keep early work cheap on purpose. loose marks, scrappy layouts, throwaway mockups. cheap things are easy to abandon, and easy abandonment is how a good direction gets found instead of the early decent direction getting defended.

watch for decorating instead of solving. if taking an element out changes nothing, it probably does not belong.

treat constraints as material. a limited palette, an awkward format, a stubborn existing mark that cannot be touched: all of it is something to push against, and pushing usually produces the interesting work.

process

- gather before generating. collect references, but also the boring stuff: existing materials, technical limits, the things the client already dislikes.
- say the problem out loud in plain words before opening any software. if it cannot be said simply, it is not understood yet.
- explore wide, then narrow. keep a messy pile of directions and only prune when the pile starts to feel repetitive.
- show fewer options than feels natural. a big spread of choices hands the decision labor back to the person who hired a designer to carry it.
- let work rest between passes. fresh eyes catch what tired eyes defend. even a short walk changes how a layout reads.
- test work at the sizes and in the conditions where it will really live: tiny, enormous, on a bad screen, in grayscale, squinted at from across the room.
- leave a trail. a note about why something was chosen is worth more than the file itself when revisiting it later.
- keep files tidy as I go, because messy layers are a loan taken out against future frustration.

working with people

clients usually do not have the vocabulary to say what is wrong, so "make it pop" and "it feels off" are real information wrapped in vague packaging. the job is to unwrap it. ask what they are picturing, what they have seen that they liked, what they have seen that made them wince.

explain the reasoning along with the work. a design presented without a rationale is just a preference, and preferences get overruled by whoever is loudest. a design presented with a rationale can actually be discussed.

- No weekend work
- Email replies within a day, not an hour

feedback from a committee has a habit of averaging everything into beige. find out who really holds the decision and aim the work at that person's real concerns rather than at the room.

say so early when something will not work, while changing course is cheap.

separate taste from fit. I can dislike a color and still know it is right for the audience. the reverse is also true, and harder to admit.

craft reminders

- hierarchy before polish. decide what leads, what follows, and what is background before choosing anything pretty.
- space is not leftover. empty room is doing work, and crowding it is usually a sign of nerves.
- type carries most of the voice. spend more time on it than feels proportionate.
- limit the palette, then limit it again.
- coherence in a system beats cleverness. a clever exception costs effort every time it shows up.
- contrast should be earned. if everything shouts, nothing is heard.
- alignment problems are visible to people who cannot name them. they just feel that something is slightly wrong.
- accessibility is part of the craft, not a layer added at the end. legibility, contrast, and clear targets make the work better for everybody, including the people who never complain.
- copy and layout are the same conversation. words that arrive late break layouts built around placeholder text.
- a good idea survives being drawn badly. if it only works when rendered beautifully, it is probably a styling trick.

open-ended ideas

- keep a running scrapbook of things that caught my eye for reasons I cannot yet explain: a worn sign, a bit of packaging, the way a receipt is organized. revisit it when stuck.
- try designing something with deliberately wrong tools, like only rectangles, or only the feel of a typewriter, just to see what the limits teach.
- maybe write a short plain-language guide to how the studio works that a new client could read before starting. it could also act as a gentle filter for fit.
- maybe a habit of small unpaid experiments: posters for imaginary events, logos for businesses that do not exist. low stakes, high learning.
- explore how an identity looks as it ages. what happens to a system after a long stretch of small edits by other hands? it might be worth designing with that in mind and leaving room for clumsy updates.
- look back at past work and ask what survived in the world and what got quietly replaced. what did the survivors have in common?
- build a vocabulary of the feelings clients reach for and map each to concrete design moves, so "warm" or "serious" or "friendly" turns into something testable.
- consider making the process visible through a simple shared board, so people see the mess before the polish and trust the route.
- explore sound, motion, and texture more. a lot of brand feeling lives outside the static page.
- try building a small library of my own mistakes: layouts that failed, why they failed, what fixed them.

things to keep in mind

being a studio of a single person means every role lives in the same head: designer, bookkeeper, salesperson, janitor of old files. switching between those roles is expensive, and grouping similar tasks saves energy.

energy is a resource just like focus. notice which kinds of work drain and which kinds restore, and arrange the work around that when there is room to choose.

saying no is part of the craft too. a project that is wrong for the studio usually costs more than it pays, in ways that never show up on an invoice.

solitude is useful and also risky. with nobody to push back, bad ideas can survive a long time. find ways to get outside eyes, like trusted peers, a critique swap, or just explaining the work aloud to a patient friend.

comparison is a trap. other people's finished work is the end of their process, shown next to the middle of mine.

rest is not wasted. a lot of the best solutions show up while doing something unrelated, and a mind that is never idle rarely gets surprised.

questions to chew on

- what makes a piece of work feel inevitable, as if it could not have looked any other way?
- where does the line sit between a distinctive style and a rut?
- how much of the job is design and how much is helping people decide?
- what would the studio look like if it were set up to be quiet and deep rather than busy and broad?
- which parts of the process could be made lighter without making the work thinner?
- how do you tell the difference between a client resisting a good idea and a client correctly sensing a bad one?
- what is the smallest version of a project that would still teach everything the full version would?

loose observations

the best briefs are often the shortest, and the worst are the ones padded with adjectives. when a brief is long, it is worth hunting for the sentence that actually matters and building outward from there.

people forgive a plain design far more readily than a confusing one. clarity is generous, and ornament is optional.

a good critique asks questions before delivering verdicts. "what were you going for here" opens more doors than "I do not like this."

finally, a reminder to self: the work is allowed to be quiet. not every project needs a big idea, and some of the most useful things are simply well organized, well spaced, and easy to read.
