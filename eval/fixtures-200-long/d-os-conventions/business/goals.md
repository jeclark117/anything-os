# Q4 goals

general working notes

stuff i keep circling back to when the work gets murky. nothing here is a plan, more a pile of habits and loose ideas worth keeping within reach.

**approach**

- begin by figuring out what the thing is actually for. not what the brief says, what it's for. a brief is usually a guess at a solution, and the real problem sits a layer underneath.
- ask the annoying questions early while they're cheap. who will look at this, where will they be standing or sitting when they do, what are they already tired of seeing, what would make them trust it.
- keep scope straight. a small studio can make excellent work or a large volume of work, and the skill is noticing which of those a project is really asking for.
- good design is mostly removal. when something feels off the fix is usually taking a thing away, not adding a clever thing.
- let constraints do some of the thinking. a tight palette, a limited set of typefaces, a rigid grid. constraints make choices feel less arbitrary and make the pieces feel like they belong together.
- write the reasoning down in plain words before defending it out loud. if the logic can't survive a plain statement it probably won't survive a presentation.

**process**

i move in loops rather than a line. a rough version of the whole thing shows up early, ugly but complete, and then every pass lifts everything together instead of polishing a corner until it outshines the rest. the ugly complete version is useful because it shows where the real trouble is hiding, and the trouble is almost never where i expected.

some habits that help:

- work small first. thumbnails, scraps of paper, boxes with labels. anything cheap to throw away is easier to think with.
- step away from the screen when stuck. walking, washing dishes, staring out a window. the answer arrives sideways.
- look at work upside down, in grayscale, shrunk to a postage stamp, blown up huge. if the hierarchy survives all of that it's probably sound.
- keep a running pile of references but be suspicious of it. a reference is a flavor, not a recipe. copying the surface of something that works usually misses why it works.
- name files and layers like a stranger will have to open them later, because eventually that stranger is me.
- save versions freely and keep the dead ends. rejected directions have a way of becoming useful when the situation shifts.

**on looking around**

spend real effort in the physical world before looking online. signs, packaging, receipts, the way a shop arranges its shelves, how a stairwell tells you where to go without a single word. most of the best visual ideas are already out there, unnoticed, waiting for a person to ask why they work.

**working with people**

- clear writing beats clever work. a plain summary of what's being shown and what feedback is needed saves a lot of wandering conversations.
- present fewer options than feels natural. too many directions invite people to pick favorites for the wrong reasons, and they dilute the argument for the best idea.
- feedback is data about a reaction, not an instruction. when a person says "make it pop" the useful move is asking what felt missing.
- be kind about taste. people rarely have the vocabulary for what they like, and that's not a flaw, that's why they bring in a designer.
- say the uncomfortable thing early and gently. a worry voiced at the sketch stage is a conversation; the same worry at the end is a crisis.
- remember the person across the table usually has a boss, constraints they don't control, and their own fear of looking foolish. shape the process so they look good.
- finish the thought in feedback. "this is bad" teaches nothing; "this fights the headline" does.

**things to keep in mind**

- Finish Client A.
- Land one more client.
- Decide on rates. See [rates.md](rates.md).

- being a team of me means nobody catches mistakes. build in some way to get fresh eyes: a trusted peer, a pause, a printout, reading text aloud.
- energy is a resource as real as materials. protect the stretches when thinking is sharp for the work that needs it, and push admin into the soggy parts of the schedule.
- inconsistency kills trust faster than imperfection. small details like alignment, spelling, spacing, naming are what people notice without knowing they noticed.
- accessibility is not an extra layer. contrast, legibility, sensible reading order, and thoughtful alt text are part of the craft, and they usually make the design better for everybody.
- don't fall in love with the earliest idea, and don't fall out of love with it either. sometimes it was right and the later detours were just nerves.
- be wary of trends. borrowing a trend is fine, but ask whether it will look stale or merely look familiar.
- document the weird stuff. odd fixes, export settings, anything that took effort to figure out. future me is forgetful and slightly ungrateful.
- rest is part of the process. tired work looks tired.

**open-ended ideas**

- a small library of reusable starting points: layouts, type combinations, structures for proposals. not templates exactly, more like warm-up exercises.
- a habit of looking back at old projects with fresh eyes and writing a short, candid note on what held up and what didn't.
- maybe a recurring practice of making something with no brief just to keep the hands loose. a poster for an imaginary event, an alphabet, a map of a made-up town.
- could be worth writing publicly about process. not self-promotion, more thinking out loud, the way notes like these might turn into something useful for others.
- explore collaborating with writers, illustrators, photographers, developers. a studio of me plus a loose circle of people whose strengths fill my gaps.
- experiment with physical output: printing, binding, small objects. screens flatten everything and tactile work reminds me why this is fun.
- build a better way to capture ideas as they arrive. voice memos, a pocket notebook, photos of things on walls. the problem is rarely having ideas, it's finding them again.
- think about how to explain the studio's way of working in plain language, so the right sort of people recognize themselves in it.
- a reading list: books on typography, behavior, the history of the craft, plus plenty unrelated to design. the best surprises come from sideways sources.

**questions to sit with**

- what does good work look like for this studio specifically, apart from what looks impressive in a portfolio?
- which kinds of problems are energizing and which are merely familiar?
- where does saying yes quietly erode the work?
- how much of the process could be made visible to the people being served, and would that help them trust it or just make them nervous?

**small reminders**

- the brief is a starting point, and so is the design.
- nobody sees the rejected versions, so be generous in making them.
- if a choice feels heavy, shrink it until it feels reversible, then act on the reversible piece.
- clarity before charm, though charm is allowed to show up uninvited.
- good work usually looks obvious afterward. that's the goal, not a complaint.
