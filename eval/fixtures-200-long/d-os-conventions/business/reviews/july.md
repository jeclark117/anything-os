# Review: july

general working notes

stuff that sits underneath the details. mostly here so I can reread it when I've lost the thread.

**approach**

the studio works best when it stays small on purpose. there's nobody to hand things off to, so every habit has to be light enough to survive a bad mood. if a system needs upkeep to stay useful, it quietly dies. so: fewer folders, plain names, and notes written like I'm explaining things to a slightly forgetful friend, who is basically me later.

design work here is mostly listening followed by editing. the listening is where the value lives. people rarely arrive with a clear brief. they arrive with a feeling that something is off and a hazy picture of what better looks like. the job is turning that feeling into something concrete enough to react to. a rough direction shown early beats a polished direction shown late, almost every time, because the rough version invites honesty and the polished version invites politeness.

I keep coming back to the idea that taste is a filter, not a talent. what matters is knowing what to leave out. when a layout feels wrong, the fix is usually removal rather than addition. clutter hides indecision.

**process**

the rhythm that seems to work:

- start with questions, not mockups. who is this for, what should they feel, what should they do next, what would make this embarrassing if it went wrong
- gather references loosely. collect more than needed, then cut hard until the pile has a point of view
- sketch on paper before touching a screen. paper is forgiving and screens tempt me to polish too early
- work in rough passes. each pass should be uglier and braver than feels comfortable
- show work with a short explanation of the thinking, not just the visuals. people respond better when they can see the reasoning
- leave room for feedback to change the direction, not just the details
- write down what was learned when something wraps up, while it's still fresh

there's a trap in over-preparing. a beautiful process document feels like progress but isn't. the real test is whether the thing in front of the client gets clearer after each round. if it doesn't, the process is decoration.

another trap is treating every project like a blank slate. patterns repeat. certain questions always unlock things, certain kinds of feedback always mean something other than what they say. "make it pop" usually means the hierarchy is unclear. "it feels busy" usually means too many things are shouting at the same volume. "I'll know it when I see it" means more listening is needed, not more options.

**things to keep in mind**

- being a one-person shop is a strength when it comes to speed and voice, and a weakness when it comes to perspective. get outside eyes on things regularly, even from friends who aren't designers
- scope creep rarely announces itself. it arrives as a small favor, then another small favor. a polite habit of naming what's inside the work and what's outside it saves a lot of resentment
- clients mostly want to feel taken care of. clear communication, even when there's nothing new to report, does more for trust than a clever solution
- rest is part of the work. ideas arrive when the mind is wandering, not when it's gripping
- don't confuse busyness with health. a full calendar can hide a lack of direction, and an empty one can be a chance to build something
- keep the money side boring. simple records, plain agreements, no improvising when someone is waiting on a response
- say no gently and early. a late no is much more expensive than an early one
- protect the work that makes the portfolio better, not just the work that pays the bills. both matter, but they pull in different directions and need deliberate balancing
- good files are a gift to future me. naming, structure, and notes inside the files themselves

**about energy**

creative stamina isn't constant. there are stretches where everything flows and stretches where every choice feels heavy. it helps to match tasks to energy instead of forcing everything through the same pipe. heavy-thinking work when the mind is sharp, admin and tidying when it isn't. tidying counts as real work, by the way, even if it never shows up in anything shareable.

also worth remembering that comparison is a thief. looking at other studios is useful for calibration and terrible for mood. the point is to be good at a particular kind of work for a particular kind of person, not to look busy on a feed.

**open-ended ideas**

- a small library of reusable questions for early conversations, sorted by what they uncover: goals, fears, taste, constraints
- a habit of writing a short reflection after each project, maybe just a few honest sentences about what went well, what dragged, and what surprised
- experiment with a looser format for sharing work in progress, something between a sketchbook and a newsletter, so people can see how thinking evolves
- try making a few personal projects with no client at all, purely to explore a style or an idea. no brief, no audience, no pressure. see what shows up
- build a collection of before-and-after stories that explain the reasoning rather than just showing the result
- explore whether a short guide on preparing for a design project would be useful to people who are new to hiring a designer. could double as a quiet introduction to how the studio thinks
- think about collaborating loosely with writers, photographers, or developers whose sensibilities match. a small network might make bigger work possible without losing the intimate scale
- maybe a physical object: printed notes, a poster, a set of cards. something tangible that carries the studio's voice
- consider how to make the studio's own materials feel as considered as the work made for others. the cobbler's children problem is real
- play with constraints as a creative tool: limited palettes, limited typefaces, limited space. restriction tends to sharpen decisions

**small reminders**

- curiosity is the engine. when it fades, change the input
- be specific in praise and in critique, including when talking to myself
- done and good are different things, and so are good and finished. know which is being aimed for
- write things down before they evaporate
- ask what the work is for before asking what it should look like
- trust slow thinking. some ideas need to sit before they can be seen clearly
- people remember how a collaboration felt long after they forget the details of the deliverable

**loose threads**

there's always a pile of half-formed thoughts that don't fit anywhere yet. that's fine. a notes system should be able to hold things that aren't ready, without forcing them into tasks or categories. the best use of a place like this is as a drawer for maybes, a place where odd observations can sit until they find a neighbor.

if a note can be understood by a stranger without extra context, it's probably written well enough. if only the author can decode it, it's a diary entry, which has its own value but a different job.

that's about it for now. more will accumulate.
