# Studio values

working notes, loose

how a project tends to open

Most projects begin as a muddle. The brief is part wish, part worry, and part guess about what the person asking actually needs. It helps to sit in the muddle for a while instead of rushing to make something. Ask the plain questions, the ones that feel almost too obvious. Who is this for? What happens if it never exists? What would make the person on the receiving end feel relieved? Those answers shape the work more than any mood board does.

- write the problem down in my own words before touching anything visual
- if I can't explain it to a friend across a table, I don't understand it yet
- notice which words the client keeps reaching for and keep them close
- listen for what's being avoided as much as what's being said
- look at what already exists in the space, and ask why it looks the way it does

sketching and early moves

Early work should be cheap and a little ugly on purpose. Paper, a fat pen, a rough layout dragged around on screen. The point is to find out where the shape wants to go, not to impress anybody, including me. When something looks polished too early, it gets defended instead of questioned. Rough things invite honest reactions, and honest reactions are what the early stage is for.

- make plenty of bad versions quickly; the good version is usually hiding behind them
- step away from a sketch and come back with fresh eyes
- keep the strange offshoots in a folder, because they often turn out useful somewhere else
- don't fuss over spacing until the idea has settled
- try the opposite of the obvious move, just to see what the obvious move was protecting

constraints are friends

A tight limit usually helps. A narrow palette, a short list of typefaces, limited room on the page. Limits force decisions that would otherwise drift. When a project feels shapeless, add a rule and see what happens. When it feels suffocating, loosen a rule and see what breaks. The rule is a way of learning, not a law.

type, color, and space

- type does most of the heavy lifting, so choose it with care and then trust it
- white space isn't empty; it's doing a job
- color needs a reason, and if I can't say why a color is there, try removing it
- contrast first, taste after
- test things in poor conditions: small screens, bad light, tired eyes, a cheap printer
- alignment is a kind of kindness; people feel it even when they can't name it

feedback

Feedback comes in disguises. A comment like "it feels off" is rarely about the thing being pointed at. Ask what the person expected to see instead. Separate the reaction from the proposed fix, since the reaction is usually right and the fix is usually a guess. Stay curious and try not to get prickly. My own gut response to criticism is information, but it isn't an instruction.

- thank people for blunt notes, even when they sting
- write feedback down exactly as it was said before interpreting it
- be wary of design by committee; ask for reactions, not redesigns
- if praise is vague, it's worth asking what specifically landed

working solo

Working solo has its own hazards. Nobody catches a wrong turn in the moment, so it's worth building small habits that do the catching: read the work out loud, print it, turn it upside down, squint at it, look at it in a mirror. Explain it to an empty room and notice where the words get shaky.

It's also easy to drift into hiding. Sending work out before it feels ready is uncomfortable, and it's also how things get better. Distance from the work is useful; isolation from people is not.

energy and fog

Some stretches are loud and productive, others are slow and foggy. The foggy ones aren't failures. Reading, walking, cooking, tidying files, staring out a window: that's often when a knot loosens. Quiet time is what makes the loud time possible, so it deserves some protection. Taste needs feeding too. Go look at old posters, packaging, hand-painted signs, ordinary forms people had to fill in. Good references are everywhere, and most of them weren't made by designers.

- Clear over clever
- Small and direct
- Finish what we start

words matter

- headlines and labels are design, not decoration
- plain verbs beat fancy nouns
- read every bit of copy aloud; if it sounds like a brochure, it probably reads like one
- name files, layers, and ideas so a stranger could follow them
- an honest "I don't know yet" beats a confident guess

files and handoff

- keep source files tidy as though somebody else will open them in a hurry, because somebody will, possibly me
- leave short notes inside complicated files
- export carefully, check the exports, then check them again
- write down the reasoning behind choices that won't be obvious in hindsight
- gather the fonts, images, and links in a place that makes sense

talking about the work

People respond to stories more than specs. When presenting, begin with the problem and the feeling, then show what was made. Avoid jargon that makes people nod without understanding. If a word would feel strange at a kitchen table, find a better one. Show less and explain it well. A confusing presentation can sink good work, and a calm one can rescue modest work.

things to keep in mind

- the person using the thing is usually distracted, in a hurry, and a little skeptical
- nobody reads as carefully as the designer hopes
- accessibility isn't a layer added at the end; it's part of seeing straight
- good defaults beat clever options
- if something is hard to explain, it's probably hard to use
- trends are weather, not climate
- a request that pulls away from the real goal deserves a kind but honest conversation
- take care of the boring parts, because the boring parts are what people live with

open-ended ideas

Questions to wander around, not answers:

- what would a project look like if the deliverable were a tiny set of rules instead of a finished object?
- could a handoff include a recording of me talking through the thinking, so the reasoning travels with the files?
- what if a project closed with a plain-language note about what was learned, for the client and for me?
- is there a way to show the rough stage to clients without scaring them?
- a library of failed experiments, tagged by what they taught
- redrawing an everyday object from memory to notice what the mind keeps and drops
- collecting signs, labels, and forms that work well in awkward places, and wondering why
- how would this look if it had to work with no color, no images, and no sound?
- what if a project began by removing something that already exists rather than adding something new?
- what would a studio website look like if it showed only process and no polished outcomes?
- what does "small" mean for a brand, a poster, an app? Is smallness a size, a tone, or a restraint?
- could silence in a layout be treated as content?

A lot of design is noticing. The shape of a doorway, the way a receipt curls, the awkward label on a jar. The habit of noticing feeds everything else, and it costs nothing. Some of the best ideas arrive sideways, while looking at something unrelated, and the job is mostly to be awake enough to catch them.
