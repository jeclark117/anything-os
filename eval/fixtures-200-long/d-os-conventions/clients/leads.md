working notes, general

**approach**

the studio works best when it stays small and a little opinionated. the pitch, if there is one, is that a real person reads the brief, thinks about it properly, and answers in plain language. larger agencies can't offer that without layers of handoffs, so it makes sense to lean into it rather than act bigger than the studio is. the work should feel considered, not produced.

some habits that seem to hold up:

- start from the problem the client has, not the deliverable they asked for. the request is usually a symptom.
- ask why before asking what. most briefs get sharper after a conversation about why the thing needs to exist at all.
- show less, explain more. a small set of strong directions beats a wall of options, and it keeps the conversation about the idea instead of about taste.
- treat constraints as material. a tight budget, an awkward audience, a logo nobody loves: each of those gives the work a shape.
- be wary of originality for its own sake. clear usually beats clever, and clever only earns its place when it makes the clear thing land harder.

**process**

the skeleton stays roughly the same even though every project bends it a little.

it begins with listening. a conversation, some questions in writing, and a look at what already exists: old materials, competitors, whatever the client is embarrassed by. the embarrassing stuff is often the most useful, because it shows where the friction lives.

then comes a messy stage that should stay messy for a while. sketches, word lists, scraps of reference, collages that make no sense to anybody else. nothing here is meant to be shown. the goal is to find the tension the rest of the work can hang from. if the tension isn't found, polish won't rescue it.

after that, narrow. keep the directions that feel true and let go of the ones that are only pretty. build the survivors out far enough that a client can react to something real, not a mood board with good lighting.

feedback goes better when it's framed up front. say what kind of reaction is useful at this stage (does this feel right for the audience?) and what isn't useful yet (can the button be bluer?). people respect a clear frame and usually relax once they have one.

handoff deserves as much care as the design itself. files organized, naming sensible, a short guide for using the work without the studio in the room. a project that is easy to hand off is easy to recommend.

keep the leftovers. rejected directions go in a folder, because they often become the seed of something else, and sometimes a client circles back to them months later in spirit if not in fact.

**keep in mind**

- the inbox is a pipeline. interest cools quietly, and silence from the studio can read as disinterest even when it's just a busy stretch. a short warm reply beats a perfect late one.
- trust is the real currency early on. people hire the studio because they feel understood in the opening conversation, long before they see any work.
- organizations with many stakeholders tend to have many opinions. clarity about who actually decides things saves pain later.
- scope creep rarely arrives as a big request. it arrives as "while you're in there." have a graceful way to name it without sounding defensive.
- working solo means every role is covered by the same brain: strategist, designer, bookkeeper, receptionist. context switching is the hidden cost. protect long stretches for deep work and batch the admin into its own lane.
- rest is part of the job. the best ideas tend to show up away from the desk, on a walk or in the shower, when the brain stops trying to be productive.
- saying no is a skill. a project that's a poor fit costs more than an empty calendar, in energy as much as money.
- write down what went well and what didn't after a project wraps, while it's fresh. memory edits out the useful bits.
- the work is never only the visible thing. tone of emails, speed of replies, how a invoice looks: all of it is part of the design of the experience.
- don't confuse busy with healthy. a full plate of mismatched work is a slower route to burnout, not a sign of success.

**on clients and communication**

most friction in a project isn't about the design. it's about expectations that were never said out loud. the fix is boring and effective: write things down in plain words, confirm understanding in both directions, and keep a paper trail that is friendly rather than legal.

it helps to speak to the person, not the role. a founder who is nervous about their brand needs reassurance in a different shape than a program manager who needs something to show their board. the same design can be explained in very different ways without changing what it is.

be honest about uncertainty. clients can tell when a confident answer is a bluff, and they remember. "i'm not sure yet, here's how i'd find out" builds more trust than a polished guess.

watch for the client who wants to be told what to do and the client who wants to be left alone. both exist, and both can be a joy once the working rhythm is clear. the trouble starts when the studio guesses wrong about which one it's dealing with.

**open-ended ideas**

- a small library of reusable discovery questions, grouped by theme: audience, tone, fear, ambition, what success would feel like.
- a plain-language explainer for clients about how design decisions get made, so they can take part without needing the jargon.
- a "leftovers" gallery on the site: directions that didn't make it, with a note on why. honest, a bit unusual, shows thinking instead of just results.
- a low-pressure way to share work in progress publicly, no polish required. could feed the social side without turning it into a chore.
- a template for project reflections that doubles as a gentle prompt for a testimonial.
- trading some studio capacity for interesting work with a purpose, as long as it doesn't become a habit that eats the paid side.
- a lightweight onboarding kit: welcome note, what to expect, how communication works, what the studio needs from the client and when.
- a tight, well-defined package that makes early conversations easier, with the tradeoff that it narrows what the studio is known for.
- a swipe file of good proposals, good kickoff messages, and good ways to decline politely. borrow the structure, not the words.
- a short written statement of the studio's voice that a stranger could repeat back accurately.

**loose questions**

- what does the ideal client sound like in their opening message? worth studying the pattern.
- which kind of work makes the studio lose track of everything else? probably worth chasing more of that.
- is it better to be known for a style or for a way of thinking? style is easier to market, thinking is easier to sustain.
- what would make referrals easier to give? people recommend what they can explain in a sentence.
- how much of the process should be visible to clients? transparency builds trust, but too much can invite backseat driving.
- what's the smallest useful version of a new idea? try that before building anything elaborate.

**general reminders**

keep the tone human. keep the files tidy. keep curiosity ahead of ego. the studio's advantage is attention, and attention is a habit, not a talent. protect it by keeping the setup simple, the commitments honest, and the work interesting enough that it's still fun to open the laptop.

when in doubt, make the next small thing clearer for somebody else. that tends to be the right move whether the thing is a layout, an email, or a proposal.
