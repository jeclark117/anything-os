# Client R call

general working notes

loose thoughts on how i work, nothing urgent, just things worth having written down somewhere

**starting out**

Most of the good work comes from listening more than talking at the start. Before any sketching, I want to understand what the people across the table actually struggle with, which is rarely the same as what they say they want. A request for a fresh look is often a request for clarity, or for something that makes an awkward internal conversation easier to have.

Questions that tend to open things up:
- what would make them proud to show the work to their own colleagues
- what are they tired of seeing in their space
- what have they tried before, and why did it fizzle
- what do they get animated about, and what do they skim past
- who will actually live with the result day to day, and do those people have a voice in the process

I try not to open a design file until I can describe the problem in plain language without any design vocabulary. If the description needs jargon, I probably don't understand it yet.

**making the work**

Rough before polished, always. Sketches on paper, scrappy layouts, ugly type pairings thrown together just to see the shape of things. The point is to make bad versions quickly so the good version has somewhere to come from. Polishing too early makes everything feel precious, and precious work is hard to change.

- keep a folder of rejected directions; they often turn out useful for a different problem
- work in grayscale for as long as possible so structure carries the design, not color
- squint at everything; if the hierarchy falls apart when blurred, it isn't working
- step away from a layout and come back with fresh eyes before judging it
- print things out when possible; screens flatter, paper tells the truth
- test type at the sizes people will actually see, not the size that looks nice while zoomed in
- build with real content as soon as it exists, because placeholder text hides most of the problems

When stuck, change the medium. Cut paper, trace over a printout, rebuild the layout from memory, talk through it out loud as if explaining it to a stranger. Anything that breaks the rut.

**showing work and handling feedback**

Presenting is part of the design. A good idea shown badly looks like a bad idea, and a modest idea shown with a clear story can look better than it is. Better to lead with the problem, walk through the thinking, and only then reveal the visuals, so people react to the reasoning and not just to whether they like a color.

- say what kind of feedback is useful before showing anything
- separate taste from function; "i don't love it" and "it doesn't work" are very different notes
- when feedback sounds like a solution, dig for the problem underneath it
- write down every reaction, even the ones that sting, and review them when the sting has faded
- resist defending the work in the moment; absorb first, respond after
- remember that a vague discomfort usually points at something real, even when the suggested fix is wrong

**working with clients in general**

Being a studio of just me means communication is nearly the whole product. People relax when they know what is happening and what is expected of them. Silence breeds worry, even when the work is going well.

- write up what was said after conversations, short and plain, so all parties remember the same thing
- give feedback prompts that are specific, like "does this feel trustworthy" instead of "thoughts?"
- keep the circle of reviewers small; too many voices flatten the work into something safe
- be honest when something isn't working, including when it's my own idea that's failing
- keep relationships warm without being pushy; a useful article or a small observation about their field goes further than a generic nudge

People also have whole other jobs happening around a project. Their priorities shift for reasons that have nothing to do with the work or with me. Taking it personally is a waste of energy, and rushing people rarely produces anything good.

**things to keep in mind**

- scope creeps quietly; notice it early and name it kindly
- good constraints help; ask for them if none are given
- accessibility is not a final polish step, it shapes decisions from the start
- contrast, spacing, and legibility cover most of what makes something feel professional
- people scan rather than read; design for scanning and reward the people who do read
- consistency beats cleverness in systems work
- leave room for the client's team to maintain things without needing me for every small change
- keep source files organized as though a stranger will open them later
- back things up in separate places, and name files so future me isn't confused
- notice when enthusiasm for a project is really enthusiasm for the portfolio piece it might become

**open-ended ideas**

Stuff to poke at when there's room, no pressure, no promises:

- a small library of reusable patterns pulled from past projects, stripped of anything specific to the people they were made for
- a plain-language guide for new clients explaining how a project usually unfolds, so surprises are fewer
- a reading list for the studio: typography, writing, psychology, old print ephemera, anything with good visual rhythm
- short case notes after projects wrap, focused on what went wrong and what was learned, not what looked good
- an experiment in making work with a deliberately restricted palette of type and color, just to see what survives
- a showcase of process instead of outcomes; messy sketches and abandoned directions are often more interesting than the final frames
- collaborations with writers, photographers, or developers who think differently, to widen what the studio can offer
- an informal habit of wandering through shops, markets, and neighborhoods collecting signage and packaging that catches the eye
- posters made purely for fun, no brief, no audience, to keep the hands loose
- a quieter, more sustainable studio rhythm, with room for unbilled thinking and unplanned tangents
- a handful of recurring questions to ask at the end of every project, kept in a plain text file and refined as patterns show up

**on pacing and energy**

Pay attention to which projects give energy back and which drain it. Over the long run, noticing the pattern is as valuable as any finished piece. The same goes for the shape of a working day: some hours are good for making, some for admin, and forcing the wrong task into the wrong stretch of attention just produces mediocre results slowly.

Rest is part of the process. Some of the best solutions arrive during a walk, in the shower, or while washing dishes. Protect the empty space in the schedule rather than filling it automatically.

**small reminders to self**

Keep curiosity ahead of ego. If a client pushes back, there's usually a reason underneath that is worth hearing out. The best work often comes from the friction.

Save the compliments people give. They're useful on a bad day and useful when describing the work to others.

Don't compare the studio to larger agencies. A small place can be nimble, personal, and honest in ways big operations can't, and that's the real advantage.

Look at things that have nothing to do with design. Gardens, machinery, handwriting, the way a good shopkeeper arranges a counter. Most useful ideas arrive sideways.

And keep it playful. The reason for doing this at all was enjoying making things; a studio that loses that has lost its main asset.
