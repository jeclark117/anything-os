# Client N: questions

- One cover or episode templates too?
- Do they have a logo already?
- Who's the audience

**general approach**

- start from what the work is supposed to do for the person looking at it, not from what looks nice. the look usually falls out of the purpose
- translate feelings into visuals and check them back with the client. "friendly" means wildly different things to different people
- collect references early, but treat them as mood and never as a template. if a reference starts to look like the answer, step away from it
- leave room for the work to surprise me. good directions often show up sideways while sketching something unrelated
- it's just me, so every choice has to be explainable without a team in the room. if I can't say why something is there in a sentence, it's probably decoration

**how I tend to work**

The process is loose at the start and tighter as things settle. Early on it's mostly words: a page of adjectives, a list of what the work should not feel like, rough comparisons to things that already exist in the world. The negative list is surprisingly useful. People can usually say what they hate faster than what they love, and the hate list narrows the space quickly.

After that comes a messy sketching stage, on paper if possible, because paper makes it harder to fuss over polish too soon. Lots of bad shapes. I'm looking for rhythm, density, and negative space more than anything specific. Whatever survives goes to the screen and gets pushed in different directions until it either breaks or gets interesting.

Then I like to show the client something deliberately unfinished. Rough work invites honest reactions; polished work invites politeness. The goal of an early share is to learn what they respond to, not to collect approval. I should say that out loud when I share, so nobody mistakes a sketch for a final answer.

Last comes testing in context. Mock the work up in the places it will actually live: tiny thumbnail, big screen, dark background, light background, blurred, squinted at from across the room. If it only works at a large size under perfect conditions, it doesn't work.

**things to keep in mind**

- small sizes are where most visual work fails. check legibility at the smallest realistic scale before falling in love with anything
- type carries more than people expect. a decent typeface used with care beats an exciting typeface used carelessly
- color needs a job. if a color can't be explained by function (emphasis, grouping, mood), it can probably go
- systems beat isolated pieces when the client will keep producing material after I'm out of the picture. whatever I hand over should be usable by a person who isn't a designer
- handoff guidance should be plain language: what to change freely, what to leave alone, what to do when in doubt
- ask about constraints early: technical limits, accessibility needs, where the work will be reproduced, who will maintain it
- watch for scope creep disguised as small favors. name it kindly and early
- don't confuse the client's taste with the audience's taste. they overlap, but not completely
- contrast matters for more than looks. readable work is respectful work
- if a layout feels stuck, the problem is usually hierarchy, not decoration

**working with clients**

Some clients arrive with strong visual opinions and little language for them, and they need examples to react to. Others arrive with plenty of language and no visual instinct, and they need to be nudged toward pointing at things instead of describing them. Neither is a problem. It just changes how I run the conversation.

When feedback is vague ("make it pop"), ask what they feel is missing instead of guessing. Underneath vague feedback there's usually a specific worry, and the worry is the real brief. It also helps to separate taste from problem: "I don't like it" is information about a person, while "it won't work for the people we're trying to reach" is information about the work. Both matter, but they get answered differently.

Keep a record of why choices were made. When a choice gets questioned later, the reasoning should be on hand, written plainly enough that the client can repeat it to their own colleagues. Work that can be defended by the client in rooms I'm not in is work that survives.

Present a tight set of strong directions with a clear rationale rather than a sprawling menu. A big menu feels generous but usually produces mush, because the client ends up borrowing pieces from everything.

**questions worth asking in general**

- what should a person do or feel right after seeing this?
- what would make this a failure, even if it looked great?
- who else will touch it after me?
- what happens when it needs to grow, stretch, or be used somewhere unexpected?
- what's the thing they're afraid of getting wrong?
- what already exists in their world that this has to sit comfortably beside?

**being a solo studio**

Being small is an advantage. Choices move fast, communication is direct, and the client gets the person actually doing the work. The cost is that nobody else catches my blind spots, so I need substitutes: step away and come back with fresh eyes, view the work in grayscale, flip it upside down, describe it aloud as if to a stranger, or ask a trusted outsider for a gut reaction before I've explained anything.

Energy is a material too. Quality drops when I'm stretched across too much, and it's easy to mistake exhaustion for a creative block. Some problems need rest, not more effort. It also helps to keep the studio's own admin simple enough that it never competes with the actual work for headspace.

**open-ended ideas**

- build a small library of reusable kickoff questions, grouped by theme: purpose, audience, mood, constraints, future growth
- try designing the smallest piece at the start and growing outward, instead of starting big and shrinking things down
- sketch with the wrong tool on purpose: thick marker, non-dominant hand, eyes squinted
- explore an identity built around a behavior (a movement, a sound, a way of cropping) rather than a symbol
- stretch every direction toward safe and toward brave so the client can see the range and place themselves in it
- spend time in the audience's world: what they scroll, listen to, carry around, complain about. design for that context rather than for an abstraction
- think about sound and motion even when nobody asked. a thought about how the work moves can sharpen the static pieces
- write a short reflection after each project: what I'd do differently, what I'd steal from myself next time
- keep a folder of things that made me stop and look, and revisit it without any goal
- try a project where I'm only allowed a tiny palette and a single typeface family, just to feel how far restraint can go

**stray thoughts**

- constraints are friends. the tighter the box, the more inventive things get
- consistency is more memorable than cleverness
- leave something slightly unresolved in a layout so the eye has somewhere to travel
- don't overexplain the work in presentations. if it needs a long defense, go back to the drawing board
- good design is often noticed only when it's missing
- the best compliment is when people use the work without commenting on it
- always leave margin for the unexpected, in layouts and in the process itself
