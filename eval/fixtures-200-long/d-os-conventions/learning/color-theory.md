# Color theory

general working notes

**approach**

color is a relationship problem more than a picking problem. a swatch on its own tells me almost nothing. what matters is what it does to whatever sits beside it, and what the eye does when it moves from one area to the next. so I try to start from the feeling the work is meant to give off, put that into plain words (calm, brisk, a little mischievous, dry, generous), and let those words push back on every choice after. when a color feels wrong, I can usually trace it to a mismatch between the mood words and what the hue is actually saying.

I also try to remember that what the person paying likes and what the audience responds to overlap but aren't the same thing. the audience is who the work is for.

**process, roughly**

- begin with too many candidates, then cut. it's easier to see what's working once there's something to compare against.
- pick the quiet colors before the loud ones. the background, the text, the neutrals. these carry most of the surface, and the accent has nothing to push against until they're settled.
- let one color be the boss. everything else supports it or stays out of the way.
- look at palettes at small sizes and large sizes. a color that's lovely in a big block can turn muddy or shrill when it's a thin line or a tiny label.
- look at them next to real content, not just next to each other. actual photos, actual headlines, actual messy paragraphs.
- step away, come back, and check whether the first reaction survives. fresh eyes catch what tired eyes have learned to ignore.
- keep a scrap file of combinations that surprised me, even the ones that didn't fit the project they came from.
- write down why a palette works, in a sentence or so. if I can't explain it, I probably can't defend it or reuse it.

**things to keep in mind**

- Warm colors come forward
- Test palettes in grayscale

- neutrals are never neutral. a gray leans toward something, and it'll pick up whatever is near it. check what it's leaning toward before it surprises me in the layout.
- saturation is usually the thing that makes work feel cheap or feel expensive, more than hue. pulling it back a notch often fixes a palette that felt off for reasons I couldn't name.
- the surrounding color changes the apparent color. the same swatch can look cooler or duller depending on its neighbors, so judging it in isolation is a trap.
- screens lie. brightness settings, ambient light, and different panels all shift things. a palette should hold up on a bad display, not only on a good one.
- printed color and screen color are different animals. anything headed for paper needs its own check, and the expectation should be that it'll shift.
- cultural meaning is real and varies. a color that reads as celebration in one setting can read as mourning in another. when the audience is wide, ask someone who knows the context.
- accessibility isn't a finishing step. people see color differently, and some can't tell certain pairs apart at all. if meaning depends on color alone, add a second signal like shape, weight, or a label.
- text on color is where most palettes quietly fail. a combination that looks sophisticated can be unreadable in practice, especially in small type or for tired eyes.
- restraint is underrated. the urge to use every color I like is strong, and it's usually wrong.
- trends in color date a piece faster than almost anything else. if the work needs to last, lean on relationships and proportions that feel steady rather than on whatever is fashionable right now.
- emotional associations are tendencies, not laws. a color's usual meaning can be bent by context, and bending it on purpose can be the most interesting move available.

**habits worth protecting**

I want to keep looking at the world with a color eye: how light changes a wall, why a cheap object looks cheap, what makes a shop sign feel inviting from across the street. shadows are rarely just darker versions of the surface. they tend to carry a tint from whatever surrounds them, and noticing that makes flat work feel more alive.

I should also keep making ugly palettes on purpose now and then. pushing past what feels acceptable is how the edges get mapped, and it makes the good combinations easier to recognize.

Another habit is separating the question "do I like this?" from "does this do the job?" They get tangled constantly. a palette I love might be wrong for the audience, and one I'm lukewarm about might be exactly right.

**open-ended ideas**

- build a personal library of palettes pulled from places that aren't design: weathered paint, old packaging, fabric, food, rocks, overgrown corners. note what each one feels like, not just what it looks like.
- try designing a whole piece with a deliberately limited set of hues and see how far value and texture can carry the rest of the work.
- try the opposite: an overly wide set, then edit hard, and see what the editing reveals about priorities.
- experiment with colors that clash on purpose, and figure out what makes a clash feel intentional versus accidental. I suspect proportion and spacing matter more than the colors themselves.
- explore how color behaves in motion. things that shift gradually feel different from things that snap, and the same palette can feel gentle or aggressive depending on how it changes.
- see what happens when the palette is chosen from the content itself, say pulled from a photograph or an illustration, instead of imposed on top of it.
- think about color as a sequence, like a story: where does the eye enter, where does it rest, where does it get rewarded? mapping that path could be a useful planning tool before any real choices are made.
- collect failures. a folder of palettes that went wrong, each with a sentence about why, may teach more than a folder of successes.
- play with how much color is truly needed. some of the strongest work uses almost none, and the restraint becomes the statement.
- ask what a palette sounds like, or smells like, or feels like to touch. cross-sense language sometimes unlocks choices that purely visual thinking can't.
- try describing a palette to someone over the phone without naming any colors. whatever words come out, texture, temperature, mood, are probably the real design brief.
- revisit old work and see which palettes aged well and which ones now look stuck in their moment. patterns there should say something about what to trust.

**loose reminders**

keep it simple when unsure. check it on a bad screen. let the quiet colors do the heavy lifting. leave room for the odd choice that doesn't make sense on paper but feels right in the layout, and then test whether that feeling holds up when I'm not the one looking.
