# Typography book

working notes, loose

approach

start from the reader, not the layout. who is looking at this, where are they sitting, how patient are they, are they skimming or settling in? a lot of choices fall out of those questions if I'm honest about the answers. the trap is starting with the fun part, falling for a set of letterforms, and then bending everything else around them.

get real text in as early as possible. placeholder copy hides problems. real headings, real awkward long words, real captions that run on too far. a layout that works with tidy filler usually collapses when the actual words show up, and it's much more annoying to fix after everything else is built on top.

look at work at the size it will really be seen. a zoomed-in screen is a flattering lie. if it's headed for paper, print a draft and hold it at reading distance. step back across the room as well, because the overall texture of a page says things the details don't.

when something feels off, resist the urge to add. usually the fix is removing a rule, a color, a variation in style. subtraction is cheaper and the result looks calmer. a useful question: what is this decoration doing for the reader? if the answer is "making me feel busy," it probably isn't earning its place.

process

- rough pass in plain black on white, no decoration, just hierarchy and rhythm
- check that the hierarchy survives a squint. if headings and body blur together, that's the problem to solve before anything else
- add color and ornament late, only where they earn it
- walk away, come back, and look at it like a stranger would
- read the whole thing start to finish, not just the pretty bits. the dull middle of a document is where most readers spend their lives
- compare against a layout I admire and ask what it's doing that mine isn't, rather than borrowing its surface
- hold a draft up next to something known to be good and notice where mine gets nervous

things to keep in mind

- spacing is part of the design, not what's left over. gaps between lines, between paragraphs, around headings, in the margins all carry as much information as the letters themselves
- line spacing too tight feels anxious; too loose and the lines stop holding together as a paragraph. adjust by eye, not by formula
- contrast between styles should be obvious or absent. a slight difference reads like a mistake
- italic and bold are seasoning. if a paragraph is full of emphasis, nothing is emphasized
- all caps wants a little extra letterspacing so it can breathe. real small caps beat shrunken capitals
- justified text needs hyphenation and patience, otherwise rivers of white open up. ragged right is fine and usually friendlier
- pick an edge and respect it. strong left edges calm a page; centered stacks get weak fast
- proper quotation marks, proper dashes, no extra spaces after a period. little things, but readers feel them even when they can't name them
- watch for awkward breaks: a heading stranded at the bottom of a column, a stray word on the end of a paragraph
- light text on dark backgrounds needs a bit more breathing room and a bit less brightness, or the letters seem to glow and smear
- screens render things differently. check on a cheap display as well as a nice one
- similar-looking letters cause trouble. a capital I and a lowercase l that can't be told apart will bite somebody eventually

on choosing and combining faces

I'm wary of picking by mood alone. a face can look elegant in a specimen and fall apart in a long paragraph. test with a block of real copy, then a headline, then the awkward in-between stuff like captions, footnotes, and labels. a good face survives all of that without needing coaxing.

when combining faces, contrast beats resemblance. if they're nearly alike it looks like an accident. if they're clearly different in structure, a serif next to a sans, say, they can coexist without arguing. shared proportions help: similar x-height, similar feel in the curves. also check what happens to the weaker partner when the text gets small. thin hairlines vanish and tight counters fill in.

on hierarchy

a page needs a clear entry point. the eye needs somewhere to land, then a path. if everything shouts, the reader has to do the sorting, and most won't bother. importance can be signaled by position, spacing, style, color, or scale, but using all of them at once is overkill. a small set of cues, applied steadily, usually does the job.

headings should feel attached to the text beneath them, not floating between sections. more space above than below is a quiet way of saying what belongs to what.

captions, footnotes, and labels deserve as much care as headlines. that's where sloppy work shows.

on screens versus paper

- Measure matters more than size
- Two typefaces is usually enough

screens change the rules a bit: the reader controls the window, the brightness, sometimes the typeface itself. design for flexibility rather than a fixed ideal, and check the awkward in-between widths, not just the extremes. paper is the opposite: a fixed object, every decision locked in the moment it's printed, and the details get judged up close, in the hand.

open-ended ideas

- start a project with a specimen sheet made only of the real copy, no mockup, just to see what the words want
- set a page in a plain, unremarkable face to find out how much work the layout alone is doing
- keep an archive of pages from old books, leaflets, and packaging that feel good, with a note on why. not for copying, for training the eye
- a review checklist short enough that it would actually get used. long checklists get ignored
- how do handwriting-style forms behave in long reading? probably badly, but curious where they break
- what would a style guide look like if it were mostly examples of mistakes?
- swap in a deliberately ugly face temporarily to separate what the words say from how they look
- compare the same paragraph dressed as stern, friendly, and formal. what changes besides the letters?
- make a habit of looking at signs, menus, receipts, and labels. typography under real constraints, made by people in a hurry
- read a page upside down to judge its shape and balance without the meaning getting in the way
- try a page where the whitespace is planned before the text, which then fills whatever is left

questions to sit with

- when does a quirky choice stop being personality and start being a distraction?
- how much character does body text really need?
- how do I explain typographic choices to a client without sounding precious?
- is there such a thing as a neutral face, or is every choice a stance?
- what do readers forgive, and what do they quietly punish?

more loose thoughts

good typography is mostly invisible. the best compliment is that nobody mentions it, they just read, and it felt easy. that makes it hard to show off, which is a small problem for a studio that wants to be noticed. worth thinking about how to demonstrate quiet work: before-and-after comparisons, maybe, or letting people try reading the bad version.

rules are shortcuts for taste, not replacements for it. know why a rule exists and it becomes easy to tell when to bend it. break it on purpose, with a reason, and it reads as confidence. break it by accident and it reads as sloppiness.

it's easy to treat typography as finishing, something sprinkled on after the "real" design. it's closer to the structure. the voice of a piece is carried by it before anybody has read a word.

keep some playfulness around. rigid rules can make work stiff. side experiments, lettering sketches, odd specimens all feed back into the practical work in ways that are hard to predict. a running list of small irritations spotted in the wild, like bad breaks, crowded labels, and cramped headings, sharpens the eye faster than reading theory does.
