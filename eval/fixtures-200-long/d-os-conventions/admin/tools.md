# Tools

working notes

**approach**

start with the mess. before anything gets pretty, get the whole problem out on the page in ugly form: what the thing is for, who will use it, what they're nervous about, what would irritate them. a lot of design trouble comes from solving a nicer problem than the real problem.

keep the brief small and plain. if the purpose can't be said in a short line a stranger would follow, the work isn't ready to be drawn yet.

rough beats refined while an idea is still moving. rough work invites correction, polished work invites defence. working solo, there's nobody to argue with, so the defending happens inside my own head, which is a bad place for it.

look for the constraint that does the most work. a limited palette, a narrow column, a rule about how images get cropped. a good constraint makes a whole pile of small choices disappear without anyone noticing.

treat the build as part of the design. a layout that looks great and is miserable to construct usually gets quietly compromised later, and the compromise is rarely as good as a layout that was built-aware from the start.

**process**

- talk to the person commissioning the work and write down the words they actually use. their vocabulary tends to end up in the navigation labels and headlines, and it lands better than anything invented.
- gather references widely but briefly. a pile of admired work turns into a trap if it's studied for too long. the goal is to find the feeling, not to copy the surface.
- sketch on paper when the structure is unclear. screens make everything look more resolved than it is.
- build the skeleton in grayscale. if the hierarchy works without color, color can only help.
- test layouts with ugly real material instead of tidy placeholder copy. long names, awkward headlines, missing images, a paragraph that runs far too long. real material breaks things early, which is cheaper than breaking them late.
- design the smallest screens alongside the largest rather than as an afterthought. squeezing a wide layout down produces something apologetic.
- keep a running list of questions for the client instead of sending them as they occur. batching respects their inbox and frequently shows that some of the questions answer each other.
- leave room for handoff. name layers sensibly, group things the way a builder would need them, and add short notes where a choice isn't obvious. future me counts as a stranger.
- look at work on a real handheld device, in a real room, at a real arm's length. the monitor flatters everything.

**critique and revision**

feedback is easier to use when it's sorted before it's acted on. some of it points at a real problem, some at a symptom, and some at a preference that would change again if asked on a different afternoon. the useful move is to ask what the comment is trying to protect. "make the logo bigger" usually means "I can't tell who this is from", and that can be fixed in a dozen other ways. (hmm "dozen" - avoid)

I'll restate: "and that can be fixed in plenty of other ways."

revision rounds go better when each round has a theme. structure first, then rhythm and spacing, then type, then color, then the fine polish. reopening structure while fussing over polish wastes everybody's energy.

when a direction isn't working, it helps to name exactly why in plain words before abandoning it. otherwise the same problem comes back wearing different clothes.

**things to keep in mind**

- the work is for the person who'll use the finished thing, not for the portfolio. a case study can be assembled from straightforward work afterward. the reverse rarely turns out well.
- whitespace is a material, not leftover room. protect it from well-meaning requests to fill it.
- type carries most of the voice. when a layout feels off, check the type before touching anything else: size relationships, line length, spacing between lines, how headings sit against body text, how thick or thin the strokes feel.
- contrast and legibility aren't taste questions. check them early, on a cheap screen in bad light.
- motion should explain something. if an animation can be removed and nothing is lost, it was decoration, and decoration costs load time and patience.
- accessibility is cheaper as a starting assumption than as a repair. focus states, heading order, descriptive link text, sensible alt text, keyboard paths. nothing on that list is glamorous, and all of it matters.
- the platform's constraints shape the design whether acknowledged or not. it pays to know what a site builder does well natively and to design with its grain instead of fighting it and papering over the gaps.
- be wary of reusable systems that grow faster than the project needs. a library of variants nobody uses is just clutter with a nice name.
- scope creep nearly always arrives as a small, reasonable-sounding addition. say so gently and early.
- eyes get tired. a layout looked at for too long stops being visible. step away, flip it upside down, or shrink it until only the shapes remain.

**working alone**

- there's nobody to review the work, so explaining it out loud as if to a skeptical colleague is a decent substitute. gaps show up fast when the explanation stumbles.
- a plain log of why things were shaped the way they were is worth keeping. memory is unreliable and clients ask "why" at odd moments.
- the thinking part of the work and the administrative part compete, and admin wins by default unless it's boxed in.
- it's fine for process to wander from project to project as long as the reasoning is sound each time.
- rest and boredom count as inputs. plenty of good solutions arrive away from the desk.
- isolation makes taste drift. looking at unrelated, unfamiliar, even ugly things now and then keeps the eye honest.

**open-ended ideas**

- a standing test kit of awkward real-world material that breaks layouts: absurdly long names, text in other scripts, images in the wrong proportions, empty states, error states.
- a habit of writing a short plain-language paragraph about each project right at the end, as a check that the design still serves the original purpose.
- a personal catalog of section transitions that felt right, with notes on why.
- an intake questionnaire that asks less but sharper things. what would the audience miss most if the site vanished? what does a competitor do that irritates them?
- a way of showing rough thinking without it being mistaken for finished work. maybe a deliberately sketchy visual style for early rounds.
- recording short walkthroughs instead of writing long explanations. emphasis and mood travel better in speech.
- a studio site that shows the failed directions alongside the chosen ones, with commentary.
- designing in the browser earlier, to see whether the gap between mockup and result shrinks.
- how much of a brand can be expressed through behavior (hover, scroll, loading) rather than logos and color?
- a reading list from subjects unrelated to design that feed it anyway: signage, maps, packaging, instruction manuals, hospital wayfinding, old textbooks, field guides.
- what makes a page feel trustworthy? a lot of it seems to be restraint, plain language, and obvious next steps.

**loose thoughts**

good design is mostly care for the boring parts. error messages, empty states, form labels, the page nobody planned for. a polished hero section sitting on a confusing form isn't a polished site.

people forgive plainness more readily than confusion. when stuck between clever and clear, clear tends to age better.

the best feedback usually arrives as a reaction rather than an opinion. watch where a visitor's eyes go, where they hesitate, what they ask about straight away. that's worth more than a stack of adjectives.

there's a difference between being decisive and being early. some questions deserve to stay open until the material has had a chance to answer them.

a good project leaves behind things that make the next project easier: a sharper question, a cleaner habit, a small discovery about how people read. worth noticing those, even when the project itself felt ordinary.

working notes

general approach

the work goes best when i treat a project like a conversation instead of a delivery. most of the job is listening, then handing back what i heard in a shape that makes the client say "yes, that's what we meant." skip the listening and the visuals end up pretty but wrong.

things i keep circling back to:
- name the problem in plain words before opening any design tool. if i can't say what a page is for in a sentence a stranger would follow, the layout won't rescue it.
- keep early rounds rough on purpose. polished mockups invite polite feedback. rough ones invite candid feedback.
- write down assumptions. most late trouble traces back to an assumption nobody said out loud.
- leave room for the project to surprise me. the best ideas rarely arrive on the schedule i planned for them.
- say less on the page. when in doubt, cut the extra line, the extra color, the extra flourish.

process, loosely

discovery is questions, lots of them. what does the audience already believe, what do they need to believe, what's in the way. i like collecting examples the client loves and examples they can't stand, because the reasons behind the reactions say more than the examples do. mood boards are a conversation starter, not a spec.

structure comes before surface. sketch the skeleton: what lives where, what leads to what, what a confused visitor sees on arrival. paper and whiteboards are good here because nothing on paper looks finished, so nobody gets precious about it. wireframes follow, gray and ugly, focused on hierarchy and flow.

then type and color. type does more heavy lifting than people expect, so it gets real care before any palette does. a restrained palette with a confident accent usually beats a clever scheme. test everything in grayscale too. if the hierarchy collapses without color, the color was covering for a weak layout.

after that, real material goes in. placeholder text lies. as soon as actual words and actual images land, a surprising share of the layout assumptions break, and that's healthy. better to find out in the design file than in the build.

working with clients

- clients are experts in their own business and nervous about design. respect the expertise and soothe the nerves.
- show the reasoning along with the work. a screen with no story attached gets judged on taste. a screen with a story gets judged on whether it solves the problem.
- feedback usually arrives as a feeling ("it feels cold"). translate it into something testable before reacting: spacing, contrast, voice, image direction.
- ask for feedback in a form that's easy to answer. open requests for "thoughts" produce vague replies. specific prompts produce useful ones.
- watch for committee drift, where a project gets sanded down until nothing offends and nothing lands. the remedy is usually going back to the original problem statement and asking what it actually needs.
- be clear about what belongs in the current conversation and what is a new conversation. kind, but clear.

the craft side

- spacing is most of the design. generous, consistent spacing makes mediocre components look intentional.
- build a small system early: type scale, spacing rhythm, some reusable components. it saves me from redrawing the same button over and over.
- accessibility belongs in the work from the start, not as a cleanup pass. contrast, focus states, readable sizes, sensible reading order. it makes everything better for everybody.
- motion should explain, not decorate. if an animation doesn't help a person understand what just happened, it's probably noise.
- images carry mood faster than words. photography and illustration direction are part of the design, not an afterthought.
- empty states, error messages, loading moments. the unglamorous corners are where a product shows whether anybody cared.

building and handing off

when the build is part of the job, keep the design file and the live result talking to each other. drift between them is where small disappointments pile up. name layers and components so a stranger could pick the file up cold. leave notes inside the file about purpose, not just measurements.

handoff is a deliverable too. a short guide covering how to edit material, what not to break, and where to ask for help does more for a client's confidence than a pile of source files. screen recordings of "here's how you change this" are underrated.

when a project stalls

- go back to the problem statement and read it slowly. half the time the stall is a sign that it drifted.
- change the medium. if i've been stuck on screen, sketch. if i've been sketching, build something ugly and real.
- show it to a person who knows nothing about it and watch where their eyes go before they say anything.
- lower the stakes. make a deliberately bad version, then see what's wrong with it.
- walk away and come back with a fresh read. the fix is often embarrassingly obvious.

the solo studio part

- the hardest part is having nobody to say "that's not working" at the right moment. build in outside eyes: a trusted peer, a cold read after stepping away, even reading the work out loud.
- separate making from judging. draft in a loose mood, edit in a stricter mood, and try not to do both at once.
- protect long stretches of quiet for deep work, and be honest about how much of that quiet the admin side keeps eating.
- keep a swipe file of things that made me stop scrolling, with a line about why. the why is the useful part.
- rest is part of the process. the best layout fixes often arrive on a walk, not at the desk.
- turn down work that's a poor fit more gracefully than feels natural. a mismatch is rough on everybody.

open-ended ideas

- a reusable discovery questionnaire that feels more like an interview than a form.
- a small library of annotated before-and-after examples to show clients what "refined" actually means.
- a plain-language glossary for clients so words like "responsive" and "hierarchy" stop causing quiet confusion.
- experiments with writing the copy and the design together instead of passing text over a wall.
- a post-project reflection template: what worked, what dragged, what i'd do differently, what the client actually used.
- portfolio case studies that tell the story of the thinking, not just show the finished screens.
- informal testing of layouts with real people who aren't designers, because they notice what i've gone blind to.
- looking at how other small studios explain their process, and noticing which explanations make me trust them.
- a visual diary of small interface details i admire, sorted by the feeling they create rather than by type.
- a way to show clients the cost of complexity, so "can we add this too" becomes a real conversation instead of a reflex.

questions to keep asking

- what is this actually for?
- who gets left out by this layout?
- what would this look like if it were simpler?
- what am i adding because it's fun for me rather than useful for them?
- where is the project quietly telling me what it wants to be?

keep these as a loose list on a card near the desk. not a rule set, just a way of getting unstuck when a project goes foggy.
