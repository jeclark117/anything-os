# Receipts

working notes, loose

**approach**

Start with the problem behind the problem. A brief usually describes a symptom, like "it feels old-fashioned", when the real story is that the business changed and the visuals didn't follow. Ask what shifted, what keeps bugging people, and what would make the whole thing feel finished. The useful answers usually come out sideways, in the middle of a tangent, so leave room for tangents.

Keep early work rough on purpose. Rough sketches invite candid reactions; polished comps invite polite nods. When something looks finished, people hesitate to poke at it, and the problems surface late, when they're hardest to fix.

Lean on constraints. A narrow palette, a small type system, a stubborn grid: these settle things for you and save energy for the parts that matter. When everything is allowed, nothing feels deliberate.

**process thoughts**

- Gather before generating. Collect references from outside the field too: old signage, grocery packaging, textbook diagrams, hand-painted shopfronts. Pulling only from other portfolios produces work that looks like other portfolios.
- Put the goal into plain words before opening any software. If it can't be said in a sentence or a short paragraph, the design won't say it either.
- Sketch on paper when stuck. The screen makes it too easy to polish a bad idea.
- Make the ugly version early. Sometimes it shows what the structure actually needs.
- Step away from a layout, then squint at it. Flip it. View it tiny, then enormous. If the hierarchy survives, it's probably sound.
- Test with real copy as soon as it exists. Placeholder text lies. Headlines run longer in reality, names are awkward, photos never crop the way you hoped.
- Keep a running list of things that bugged you in ordinary objects: doors, forms, menus, appliances. Bad design is everywhere and it's free education.

**working with people**

Clients rarely struggle to say what they dislike; they struggle to say why. Translate. "Too busy" might mean the eye has nowhere to land. "Too plain" might mean it doesn't feel like them. Reflect the translation back and see whether it rings true before changing anything.

Present work with the reasoning attached. A design with a story behind it is easier to defend and easier to react to, because feedback can aim at the reasoning instead of at taste. Taste arguments never end.

- Keep every receipt in the shared folder
- Name them by date

Be wary of feedback that arrives as a committee compromise. It sands off every interesting edge. When possible, find the person whose gut matters most, listen to that gut, and check the rest against it.

Silence after a presentation doesn't mean approval. Sometimes it's confusion, sometimes it's worry about hurting feelings. A specific question gets things moving: what did your eye land on right away? What felt surprising?

Boundaries matter more when the studio is small. Vague requests expand quietly. It helps to notice when a small ask is secretly a large ask, and to say so gently and early, before resentment builds on all sides.

**craft reminders**

- Spacing does more work than decoration. Generous, consistent space reads as confidence.
- Contrast is a tool, not a default. If everything shouts, nothing is heard.
- Alignment errors are felt before they're seen. People can't say what's wrong, only that it seems off.
- Type carries mood before a word is read. Check that mood against the message.
- Color shifts across screens and paper. Assume drift and build in tolerance.
- Accessibility isn't an add-on. Legibility, contrast, and clear targets improve the work for everybody, including the designer squinting at a laptop in bad light.
- Label layers and components as if a stranger will inherit them. Future you is that stranger.
- Check output on the real destination, whether that's a small screen or a printed sheet, before trusting the preview.
- Motion should explain, not decorate. If an animation doesn't clarify something, it's just noise with good manners.

**working solo**

Being a solo studio means wearing every hat, and the hats fight. The designer wants to explore, the project manager wants to wrap up, the salesperson wants to be liked, the bookkeeper wants tidy paperwork. Worth noticing which hat is talking right then. Plenty of bad moods come from the wrong hat making the call.

Creative energy isn't constant. Some stretches produce loads of good material; others produce mush. Pushing through mush rarely helps. A walk, a chore, a messy kitchen project sometimes shakes loose what staring at the screen can't.

Protect deep-focus stretches from small admin interruptions. Batch the tiny stuff, like replies and housekeeping, so it doesn't leak into creative headspace.

Working solo can be isolating in odd ways. Nobody says "that's great" or "that's not working." Cultivate trusted outside eyes, people who are blunt without being cruel. And watch the opposite trap: seeking approval so constantly that the work gets averaged out.

**taste and doubt**

Taste runs ahead of skill, which is uncomfortable but useful. The gap between what you can see is good and what you can currently make is the engine. Don't let it curdle into paralysis.

Doubt shows up right when a project gets interesting. It's usually a sign that something real is at stake, not that the work is bad. Still, check whether the doubt points at an actual flaw or just at the discomfort of not knowing yet.

Revisit old work with a cold eye. A little cringing is healthy. Notice what the cringe is about: usually it's trends, not fundamentals. Fundamentals age well.

**open-ended ideas**

- Start an archive of almost-ideas, the directions that got set aside but still have a pulse. Some deserve another life.
- Try making something with the wrong tool on purpose: a rubber stamp, a cut-paper collage, a photocopier. See what the limits produce.
- After a project wraps, jot a paragraph of case notes: what surprised, what dragged, what's worth stealing. Not a report, just candor.
- Redesign a common object badly, then well, to keep the eye sharp.
- Ask whether a recognizable personal style is an asset or a cage.
- Saying no gracefully is a skill. Maybe there's a warm, brief way of declining that leaves the door open.
- Collect questions that open up briefs, the kind that produce silence followed by a real answer.
- Explain the process to a beginner. Explaining exposes the gaps.
- Look at how other trades hand work over: carpenters, cooks, tailors. What do they pass along besides the object?
- Borrow structure from music or cooking, the way a piece builds and releases. Does a brand have a rhythm?
- Wonder whether the best presentation is sometimes no presentation, just a prototype put into somebody's hands.
- What would the studio look like if it made less but cared more? Unclear. Worth turning over slowly.

**closing thoughts**

Good work usually comes from paying attention, not from inspiration. Notice what people actually do, notice what a page is asking for, notice when you're bored by your own idea. Boredom is data.

Keep the work humane. The people looking at a design are tired, rushed, distracted, and mostly not thinking about the design. Respect that, and make things that are easy to use and quietly pleasant.
