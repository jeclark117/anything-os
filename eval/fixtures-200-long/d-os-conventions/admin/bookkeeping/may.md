# Books: may

general working notes

**approach**

the work goes better when the brief gets argued with early. not in a hostile way, just poked at until the real question shows up underneath the stated one. most projects arrive asking for a surface (a mark, a layout, a refresh) when what's actually missing is a plain sentence about what the thing is for. if that sentence can't be written in ordinary words, no amount of polish rescues it.

keep the early rounds ugly on purpose. rough shapes, blocky type, grey boxes where images go. people react honestly to rough work and politely to finished-looking work, and the honest reaction is the useful one. a clean mockup too soon just invites notes about colors when the structure is still wrong.

- start with the constraint that hurts most, not the one that's easiest to design around
- write the boring copy before the clever copy
- look at what the client's audience already does, not what the client wishes they did
- when stuck, make the thing worse in a specific direction and see what it teaches

**process**

the loop that tends to work: gather, sketch badly, show, listen, narrow. the narrowing is the hard part. it's tempting to keep every promising direction alive because dropping one feels like losing something. but a pile of half-developed options is a tax on whoever has to choose, and that person is usually tired and distracted by their actual job.

show fewer options than feels generous. a small set with clear reasoning behind each beats a wide spread with none. each option should be different in kind, not just in shade, otherwise it's a single idea wearing costumes.

some habits worth keeping:

- keep a running file of decisions made by the client and the reasons given, so nobody has to reconstruct them from memory later
- save every version with a plain-language name, not "final" or "final-really"
- write down what was explicitly left out of scope as it comes up, while it's fresh
- when feedback is vague, ask what they were doing or feeling when they reacted, rather than asking what they'd change

**things to keep in mind**

working alone means there's no one to catch the obvious mistake. so build small checkpoints into the work itself. read copy out loud. view layouts upside down or squinted. print something and tape it to the wall across the room. step away from the screen and come back to it as a stranger would.

also: being the only person in the studio means being the designer, the account manager, the bookkeeper, the janitor, and the receptionist, often inside the same hour. context switching costs more than it looks like it does. batching similar tasks helps. so does protecting the stretches of quiet where the real thinking happens. email is not urgent nearly as often as it pretends to be.

- the client's stress is usually about something other than the design
- silence after sending work is normal and rarely means what the anxious part of the brain says it means
- say no to the wrong fit early; resentment is expensive and shows up in the work
- a project that's a bit boring but well-run is worth more than an exciting one that's chaotic

**on feedback**

feedback arrives wrapped in solutions. "make the logo bigger" is almost never about size. it's about confidence, or visibility, or a worry that nobody will notice the brand. the job is to unwrap the request and respond to what's underneath, then offer a better fix than the one proposed, or sometimes just do the proposed one because it's harmless and buys goodwill.

don't defend work in the room. explain the reasoning, then stop talking. let the silence do some of the persuading. if the reasoning doesn't survive being said out loud, that's information.

when feedback contradicts itself across different stakeholders, don't average it. surface the conflict and let them settle it among themselves. smoothing it over quietly just means taking on a disagreement that isn't yours.

**craft notes**

- spacing is most of what makes a layout feel considered. fix spacing before touching anything else
- limit the type system harder than seems necessary; hierarchy comes from contrast, not variety
- a color palette should survive being printed badly and viewed on a cheap screen
- check everything at the smallest size it will ever appear
- if an element can't explain itself, cut it and see if anyone misses it
- grids are a way of being kind to future decisions
- good defaults beat clever flexibility; handing over a system that is hard to break matters more than handing over a system that is hard to match

handoff deserves more care than it gets. files named clearly, a short plain document about how things are meant to be used, fonts and licenses sorted, and a few examples of the system doing something it wasn't built for. the best compliment is when someone who never met the studio uses the work correctly.

**open-ended ideas**

- a small library of reusable questions for kickoff conversations, sorted by the kind of confusion they uncover
- a private archive of rejected directions, tagged by why they didn't work, to mine when a new project rhymes with an old one
- trying a project where the deliverable is the process, with the finished artifact as a byproduct
- writing short postmortems that focus on what felt easy, since ease is a signal too
- making a set of deliberately awkward constraints to use as warmups: only straight lines, only two typefaces' worth of attitude, only the left side of the page
- asking former clients what they remember about the work, not what they think of it; the gap is revealing
- exploring how much of the pitch could be a working prototype instead of a deck
- keeping a list of things that looked great in the portfolio but made no difference to the client's actual problem

**loose thoughts**

taste is mostly accumulated attention. the more carefully things get looked at, the better the instincts get, so looking at ordinary stuff (packaging, signage, forms, receipts) is part of the job and not a distraction from it.

a studio of one is allowed to be idiosyncratic. the temptation is to imitate bigger agencies with their layers and vocabulary. but the real advantage is directness, speed, and a point of view that doesn't have to pass through a committee. lean on that.

rest matters more than the culture of hustle admits. tired work looks tired. some of the best solutions show up while doing something unrelated, away from the desk, with nothing to prove.

finally, keep the work a little playful. if the studio stops being curious about the problems, the clients will feel it long before anyone says so.
