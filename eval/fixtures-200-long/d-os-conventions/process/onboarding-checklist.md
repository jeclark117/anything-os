# Onboarding checklist

general working notes

the paperwork is the easy part. the real job at the start of any engagement is getting a feel for how the person across the table thinks, what they're worried about, and what they're too polite to say out loud. admin just clears the decks so the actual conversation can be about the work.

**approach**

- start with questions, not solutions. the urge to sketch right away is strong and usually wrong. a drawing made before the problem is understood just turns into something to defend.
- work out what the project is really for. the stated request is rarely the whole story. a request for a new logo might really be a request for confidence, or for a way to stop apologizing for old materials.
- notice what people say when they think they're not saying anything useful. offhand complaints make better briefs than the formal brief.
- keep a running list of the words clients use to describe their own business, and reuse them in the work. people trust what sounds like them.
- ask what they've already tried and what they hated about it. cheaper than guessing.
- look at what surrounds the work: the room it lives in, the people who encounter it, the mood they're in when they do. a design that shines in a presentation and falls flat in the real setting hasn't worked.
- treat constraints as material. an awkward format, a tight brief, an old brand that can't be dropped: all of it gives the work a shape to push against.
- be suspicious of "we want it to feel modern." it means almost nothing until it's pinned to examples.

**process thoughts**

the work moves in loose phases, and the trick is not letting them blur. there's a stretch of absorbing, a stretch of wandering around with rough ideas, a stretch of narrowing, and a stretch of polishing. trouble starts when polish shows up during the wandering, because then nobody can tell whether a direction is wrong or just unfinished.

momentum is fragile in small projects. a stalled review or an unanswered question can drain the energy from everybody involved. a good way to protect it is to leave a clear next step at the end of any exchange, so nobody is left wondering who's holding the ball.

- keep early work deliberately ugly. grayscale, rough type, boxes with labels. it keeps feedback on structure and message instead of color preferences.
- show a restrained spread of directions. a huge spread invites picking favorites from different options and gluing them together, which almost never works.
- explain the reasoning before showing the visual. if the logic lands, the visual has an easier job.
- record feedback in the client's own phrasing rather than a translation of it. translations drift.
- when new feedback contradicts old feedback, say so gently and ask which matters more. don't quietly pick a side.
- leave slack in the schedule for the thing nobody planned. there is always a thing.
- name files and folders so a stranger could follow them. future me counts as a stranger.
- build in a pause between finishing a piece and presenting it. work looks different after a break, and the mistakes show themselves.

**communication**

- plain language wins. jargon makes clients feel outside the room, and a client who feels outside the room stops giving candid feedback.
- write the recap while a conversation is still fresh, and keep it short enough that it actually gets read.
- tell people what's happening before they have to ask. a brief update that says nothing much is still better than a gap.
- match the client's rhythm where possible. some people want constant contact, others prefer to be left to it until there's something to look at. that's a style difference, not a flaw.
- when delivering bad news, like a problem with a file or a missed assumption, lead with the fix, not the apology.
- ask for feedback on specific things. "thoughts?" gets vague answers; "does this feel trustworthy?" gets useful ones.
- the person with the final say who isn't in the room usually shapes the outcome anyway. find out who they are and what they care about.

**keeping the work good**

- taste is a muscle. look widely at things unrelated to the current project: packaging, signage, old book covers, hand-painted shop fronts.
- when stuck, change the medium. pen on paper, cut paper, a different typeface for no reason, a different scale. the block is usually about the tools, not the idea.
- squint at a layout or flip it upside down. hierarchy shows up fast when detail disappears.
- restraint is underrated. most weak designs have too much going on, rarely too little.
- test work in its real context: on a small screen, in grayscale, printed rough, next to a competitor's materials.
- be willing to throw away a lovely idea that doesn't serve the purpose. attachment is expensive.
- reference is fuel, not a recipe. collect examples, then close the folder before drawing.
- the client's customers are the real audience. design for them, not just for the person signing off.

**things to keep in mind**

- a client who goes quiet isn't necessarily happy. silence can mean confusion, a stalled internal debate, or fading interest. a short, low-pressure check-in usually clears it up.
- scope creep rarely arrives as a big request. it arrives as small favors that each seem reasonable. worth spotting the pattern early and naming it kindly before it hardens into an expectation.
- running a studio solo means being the designer, the bookkeeper, the project manager, and the receptionist. it's easy to let the least visible role slide. protect time for the unglamorous work or it eats the glamorous work eventually.
- not every disagreement is about design. now and then it's about trust, or about who feels heard. try to work out which kind it is before responding.
- a tidy handoff makes people feel looked after long after a project wraps. good documentation is a gift to the client too.
- a clean, polite refusal of a bad-fit project respects everybody's time, and it doesn't need a long explanation.
- rest matters. tired work looks tired.

**open-ended ideas**

- a little glossary page per project, where the client's vocabulary and mine get translated for each other. could cut down on misunderstandings.
- a "what we are not doing" list that sits alongside the goals. boundaries are easier to see when they're on paper.
- a questionnaire clients can answer in their own time, for the people who think better in writing than out loud.
- a short narrated walkthrough as a way to show progress, so clients see the thinking and not just the files.
- an archive of rejected ideas worth revisiting. dead ends from a past project can fit a new project perfectly.
- a short reflection after a project wraps: what surprised me, what dragged, what i'd want to know earlier.
- a library of starter questions for different kinds of work: identity, print, interface, signage. each type has its own blind spots.
- a way to capture the feel of a client's space or product in photographs before starting, as a visual vocabulary to return to.
- experiments in presenting type and color choices to people who don't speak design: analogies, mood scenes, texture and sound references.
- ways to make the studio feel calm from the outside even when the inside is chaotic: predictable replies, clear expectations, quiet confidence.
- explaining the process in plain language is itself a design problem. maybe worth treating it as a real design problem, with prototypes and testing on real people.

mostly the goal is for the client to feel understood and for the work to feel inevitable in hindsight, like it couldn't have looked any other way.
