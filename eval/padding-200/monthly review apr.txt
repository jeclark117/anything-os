working notes, general stuff

**approach**

- start from the problem, not the look. most weak work comes from reaching for a style before understanding what the thing has to do for the person on the other end.
- the brief is rarely the real brief. what people ask for is usually a symptom, so it helps to keep asking what happens if this goes wrong, and who notices.
- being small is an advantage. there's no handoff, no translation layer, no meeting about the meeting. protect that by keeping process light enough that it never becomes the work.
- treat constraints as material. a tight palette, a limited set of type styles, a rigid grid: these give the work a spine. blank-page freedom tends to produce mush.
- say less, then say it clearly. if a layout needs a paragraph of explanation to justify itself, it probably isn't finished.

**process**

Most projects seem to move through the same loose phases, even when they feel messy from inside:

- soak. read everything, look at what exists, talk to whoever will talk. resist sketching too early because early sketches harden into answers.
- scatter. get lots of rough ideas out fast and ugly. the goal is range, not quality. bad ideas next to each other show what the good one is.
- narrow. pick the direction that survives being looked at with tired eyes. if it only works when you're feeling generous, it doesn't work.
- build. this is where the craft lives: spacing, rhythm, edge cases, the empty states nobody asked about.
- hand over. package things so the client can live without me. files named sensibly, a short guide, a note about what not to touch.

Some habits that seem to help:

- work at a rough scale first, then zoom in. zooming in too early leads to polishing things that get cut.
- look at work upside down, squinted, in grayscale, shrunk to a thumbnail. hierarchy problems show up fast that way.
- print things. screens flatter. paper is honest about contrast and size.
- step away from a layout, then come back and ask what you notice first. that's the real hierarchy, whatever the file says.
- keep a folder of "almost" work. the leftovers from one project often solve a problem in another.

**things to keep in mind**

- clients rarely hate the work; they hate surprise. showing the thinking early, in plain language, makes the finished piece feel inevitable rather than sprung on them.
- feedback is data about a reaction, not an instruction. when someone says "make the logo bigger," the useful question is what they're feeling that makes bigger seem like the fix.
- consistency beats cleverness. a clever move used once looks like a mistake; a plain move used everywhere looks like a system.
- whitespace is not leftover space. it's doing a job. treat it as deliberately as any element on the page.
- accessibility is not a layer on top. contrast, size, and clarity are part of whether the work functions at all.
- the dull parts of running a studio (admin, bookkeeping, upkeep of tools and assets) quietly decide whether the creative parts get room to breathe. neglect there shows up later as stress that leaks into the work.
- rights and permissions matter more than they feel like they do in the moment. keep a clear record of what's allowed where, because future-me will have forgotten the fine print.
- say no to the project that needs a version of me that doesn't exist. it's tempting to stretch, but a stretched studio makes average work for everybody.
- being the only person means being the only quality check. build in ways to catch mistakes: fresh eyes from a friend, a checklist, a deliberate pause before anything leaves the building.

**on type and image**

- typefaces have temperaments. before choosing, say out loud what the text should sound like. warm, dry, nervous, confident. then audition candidates against that, not against taste.
- pairing is mostly about contrast of structure, not contrast of mood. two faces that fight over the same job look indecisive.
- body text deserves the most attention and gets the least. a beautiful headline over a tiring paragraph is a bad trade.
- photography and illustration should feel like they come from the same world as the type. mismatch reads as stock even when it isn't.
- when an image is weak, cropping harder helps more often than adding effects.

**on working with others**

- write down what was said after a call, in plain words, and send it back. disagreement surfaces cheaply that way.
- make it easy to say yes. a clear recommendation with a reason beats a menu of options that pushes the decision onto the client.
- be generous with credit and specific with praise. collaborators remember how work felt.
- when a project goes sideways, name it early and calmly. most trouble gets worse in silence.

**open-ended ideas, unsorted**

- a small, regular show-and-tell with other solo people, mostly to have someone ask "why did you do it that way?"
- a living document of rules of thumb, kept short enough to reread. prune it as much as it grows.
- a practice of making work with no client attached, just to keep the hands honest. posters, alphabets, odd little tools, anything.
- try writing the case study before the project is done. it exposes what the story actually is, and sometimes shows the project is aimed at the wrong target.
- maybe a "reverse brief" exercise: describe the worst possible version of the thing, then design away from it.
- explore how much of the studio's identity could be carried by behavior (how it answers email, how it presents files) rather than by a logo.
- look at old work with new eyes: what still holds up, and why? what dated, and was it the content or the fashion?
- a shelf for physical references: paper samples, printed oddities, packaging, anything with good texture. inspiration from screens tends to converge.
- consider whether some processes could be templated without making the output feel templated. proposals, onboarding, handoff docs. the trick is automating the boring parts and leaving the thinking raw.
- think about what rest looks like for a one-person operation. not just time off, but the kind of rest that lets taste recover. taste gets tired before the body does.
- try designing something with deliberate imperfection and see whether it feels human or just sloppy. the line is thinner than it looks.
- questions worth sitting with: what kind of client makes the best work happen? what kind of project teaches the most? are those the same?

**small reminders**

- finish the boring details with the same care as the exciting ones.
- ask what the work is for, again, near the end.
- keep a few things unfinished on purpose. they're where the next ideas hide.
- good enough is a real standard, but only when it's chosen with clear eyes rather than reached out of fatigue.
