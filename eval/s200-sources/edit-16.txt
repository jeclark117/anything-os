pricing research, finally

Pasting in my notes from going through this properly. I've been putting it off for months.

What I looked at: the pricing book, the podcast episode about project pricing, my coffee with the other designer in October, and a handful of other studios' sites. Most studios don't publish numbers, which says something on its own. The ones that do are mostly packages, like the three-tier setup she described over coffee.

What I took from the book: price the outcome, not the hours. Anchor with the biggest option first. I've been doing the opposite, starting small and hoping they'll grow into it.

What I took from the podcast: charging for time punishes you for getting faster. I'm definitely faster than I was a year ago, and I've been quietly giving that away.

Where I am now: $95 for an hour of my time, and the $110 I settled on earlier this month for the new year. Client A at $4,200 was a decent project price for six pages, and I said in my ideas note that the next one like it could be $4,500.

Honestly, though, everything I looked at says the $110 is still low for the kind of work I'm doing now. The studios that do publish are well above me, and none of them seem short of work. So I'm moving it again. Starting in the new year, an hour of my time costs $120, not the $110. That's the number that goes on new quotes and on the price sheet I send to leads. Anything already agreed stays at its agreed price. I'm not going back to Pat or anyone else with a new number.

Longer term, I want to move most work to project prices and only use time for small changes and consult calls. That's a 2027 goal, not something to do this month. It also fits with wanting fewer tiny jobs.

Things to do: update the price sheet before the next lead asks. Finish the blog draft about pricing, and keep numbers out of it like I planned. Maybe reread the chapter on anchoring before the next proposal.

Feels like the right call. I've been undercharging, and the research mostly confirmed what I already suspected.
