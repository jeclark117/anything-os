general notes, loose, to sort later

the thing i keep coming back to is that a studio of my size doesn't win by being loud. it wins by being easy to remember and easy to refer. so most of how i think about meeting people is really about leaving a clean, specific impression behind, something a person can repeat to a friend without fumbling. "she does the wayfinding stuff for small clinics" is a good sentence to be on the end of. "she does design" is not.

**approach**

- start from curiosity about the other person's work, not from my pitch. people open up when asked about the hard part of their job, and the hard part is usually where design is quietly needed.
- keep a plain description of what the studio does, free of jargon. test it on a relative who has no idea what a grid is. if their eyes glaze, rewrite.
- aim for deeper conversations over a lap of the room collecting cards. a card in a drawer is clutter. a real exchange about a real problem sticks.
- carry something physical that shows the work without a laptop: a printed piece, a sample of a finished label, a folded mock. things you can hold start conversations that screens never do.
- listen for the sentence that begins "we've been meaning to fix..." that's a seed, not a sale. don't harvest it on the spot.
- let the work be the evidence and keep the adjectives light. "thoughtful, bespoke, strategic" means nothing. a story about a messy menu that got easier to read means a lot.

**conversation openers that aren't terrible**

- "what's keeping you busy lately?"
- "what's the part of your work people don't see?"
- "how did you end up doing this?"
- "what would make your next stretch easier?"
- "what do customers get wrong about what you do?"

the best questions invite a story rather than a label. stories give me something to follow up on, and they tell me whether there's a real design problem underneath or just polite chatter.

**process thoughts**

it helps to get my head in a particular state before walking into a room full of strangers. not performing, just present. some habits that seem to help:

- remind myself that every person there is also slightly uncomfortable. most local business owners would rather talk about their actual work than make small talk.
- have a gentle exit line ready. "i'll let you get to the rest of the room" works and nobody is offended.
- bring a pen. jot a line on the back of a card right after parting, not later, because memory blurs fast. what they make, what they're stuck on, something personal they mentioned.
- eat something beforehand so i'm not hovering at the snack table out of nerves.
- wear things that feel like me and are comfortable. a studio owner who looks at ease reads as competent.
- arrive when the room is thin, find an easy anchor conversation, and leave while the energy is still good rather than limping out.

**materials and presence**

- the studio's own identity should be consistent but not precious. a slightly worn, well-made leave-behind says more than a glossy brochure that screams effort.
- a handwritten line on something printed is warmer than any amount of finish.
- keep the table or tote tidy. a cluttered pile of samples looks like indecision about what i actually do.
- show process in small doses: a rough sketch beside the final, a rejected direction with a note on why it didn't work. people love seeing the thinking.

**after the room**

the follow-up matters more than the room. a short, specific note that references what we actually talked about beats a templated "great to meet you." ideas for what to send that isn't pushy:

- a link to something useful that relates to their problem, with no ask attached
- an observation about their signage or packaging or site, kind, concrete, and offered freely
- an introduction to another person who might help them, which costs me little and builds goodwill
- a simple "this made me think of you" with a small image or reference

nothing about it should read like a hook. if it does, it'll be ignored, and rightly so.

**things to keep in mind**

- generosity compounds quietly. the people who send work my way later are rarely the ones i chased. they're the ones i helped without a ledger in my head.
- a small studio has a real advantage: the person they meet is the person who does the work. say that plainly. it's the opposite of the layered agency handoff many of them dread.
- don't over-promise out loud in a warm moment. enthusiasm is nice, but a throwaway "sure, i can do that" can turn into obligation. keep conversations exploratory.
- be honest about what falls outside the studio's sweet spot. referring a person elsewhere builds trust faster than stretching to take on a poor fit.
- avoid talking down other designers or agencies. rooms are small and word travels.
- notice who's standing at the edge of the room. sometimes the quietest person has the most interesting problem and nobody has asked.
- remember names by repeating them back, and if i blank, just ask again kindly. people forgive that far more readily than a guess.

**mistakes to watch for**

- talking about myself for too long because i'm nervous. silence is fine. let the other person fill it.
- scanning the room over a shoulder while somebody is mid-sentence. it's obvious and it stings.
- treating small businesses as stepping stones toward bigger ones. people can feel when they're a rung.
- giving away real work in disguise. a quick opinion is generous. a full critique with fixes is a free job.
- explaining process in detail to a person who only wants to know the outcome.
- leaving without writing anything down and trusting the glow of a good chat to survive.

**questions worth chewing on**

- what does a good-fit client look like for this studio? maybe write it in plain words, with traits rather than industries: values craft, makes decisions without a committee, has a real audience, is open to being told no.
- which past projects had the best energy, and what did those people have in common? did they arrive through introductions or by accident?
- is there a way to make the studio's point of view visible before a person meets me, so conversations start further along? a short essay, a teardown of a local shopfront, a gallery of process photos.
- how much of the work is explaining design versus listening to business problems? probably the latter is where trust forms.
- would it help to have stock stories, not scripts, about problems solved, told briefly and with a clear before and after?
- what's the smallest useful thing i could offer a stranger that makes them glad we met?

**open-ended ideas**

- an informal gathering for neighbors in the local business scene: a show-and-tell of before and after, or a casual critique where owners bring a menu or flyer and we talk about what's working. low pressure, teaches something, shows how i think.
- a recurring, no-strings, public mini-review of local branding as a content series. kind, constructive, never snarky.
- collaborations with adjacent small practitioners, such as photographers, copywriters, printers, and web builders, so we can refer to each other and pitch together on bigger jobs without becoming a big shop.
- a running file of overheard needs: problems people mention in passing, kept so patterns show up. maybe the same issue keeps surfacing and becomes a packaged offering.
- an experiment worth imagining: showing up at gatherings outside the usual circuit, like hobby groups, maker markets, and neighborhood associations, since the most interesting clients may not be in the obvious rooms.
- a short guide to preparing for a rebrand, given away freely, that doubles as a quiet demonstration of how the studio thinks.
- a "quiet" mode of showing up for the introvert in me: fewer words, better questions, and a plan for recovery afterward.

**reminders to self**

- be useful before being visible.
- the portfolio opens the door. the way i listen keeps it open.
- not every room is the right room, and it's okay to leave a place that doesn't fit.
- stay curious about why people do what they do. the most unexpected projects come out of that.
- rest afterward. social effort drains a solo practice in a way that's easy to underestimate, and tired follow-ups come out flat.
- the goal isn't to be the most impressive person present. it's to be the person somebody thinks of when a problem finally becomes urgent.
