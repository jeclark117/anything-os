general working notes

**approach**

- listen more than talk at the start. what a client asks for is usually a symptom, and the real problem shows up when you ask what happens if nothing changes. keep asking until the answers get boring, because that's usually where the truth is sitting.
- work from constraints outward. a tight room makes for sharper thinking. when a project feels shapeless, invent a limit on purpose: a restricted palette, a narrow set of typefaces, a rule about what's allowed to move.
- show rough work early and polished work late. rough work invites honest reactions. polished work invites politeness, and politeness is useless in a review.
- keep the reasoning visible. a good design can still get rejected if the person looking at it can't see why it looks the way it does. write the why in plain words right beside the visuals.
- be suspicious of anything that feels easy right away. sometimes it's right, but often it's just the most familiar answer wearing a new outfit.

**process**

most projects move through a loose rhythm: absorb, wander, narrow, refine, hand over. the wandering part is what gets squeezed when things get busy, and it's also the part that makes the work feel like it belongs to the client rather than to a template. wander on paper first. paper is slower than a screen, and the slowness is the point, because it forces a decision about what's worth drawing at all.

when narrowing, lay the candidates side by side and ask which ones still make sense without the pitch story attached. if a direction only works while it's being explained, it isn't finished. the good ones can sit on a table in silence and still say something.

refinement is mostly subtraction. take out the flourish that was added out of nervousness. check spacing and alignment with tired eyes and with fresh eyes, since they catch different problems. read every piece of text out loud before it goes anywhere.

handover is its own piece of design. files named so a stranger could find things, notes on why choices were made, and a clear picture of what the client can change without breaking the system. a system that only works while the designer is around isn't really a system.

**things to keep in mind**

- clients rarely read long emails all the way through. lead with the thing they need to react to and put the context underneath.
- scope creep almost never arrives as a big request. it arrives as small "while you're in there" asks, each reasonable on its own. notice the pattern early and name it kindly, without making it feel like an accusation.
- feedback from a committee tends to average out into something nobody loves. try to find out who actually owns the decision and make sure the work speaks to them.
- being solo means nobody catches mistakes by accident. build habits that stand in for a reviewer: print things out, flip them upside down, step away and come back, ask a friendly outsider what they see in a few seconds.
- exhaustion produces safe, flat work. rest is part of the craft, not a reward for finishing it.
- keep a plain record of what was asked and what was delivered, so memory isn't the only source of truth when a conversation gets fuzzy.
- be honest with yourself about work that would require the studio to turn into something else. fit matters more than volume.
- a polite no with a useful pointer elsewhere builds more goodwill than a reluctant yes.

**on feedback**

feedback is information about the viewer as much as about the work. when someone says "it feels cold," they're rarely asking for a different color. they're describing a reaction, and the job is to trace that reaction back to its cause, which might be spacing, tone of voice, image choice, or simply a mismatch with what they imagined.

useful questions to ask back: what did you notice first? what did you expect to see that isn't here? what would you be nervous to show your own customers? these pull out reactions without putting the client in the position of playing designer.

when feedback contradicts itself, don't try to satisfy both sides. lay the contradiction out and ask which outcome matters more. clients often relax when they see the tension described clearly, because they've been feeling it too.

also worth remembering: not every comment needs an action. some are just people thinking out loud, and treating each remark as an instruction leads to work that's been nudged in every direction and ends up going nowhere.

**on craft**

- type does most of the heavy lifting. get the hierarchy right and half the visual problems disappear. get it wrong and no amount of decoration will rescue the piece.
- whitespace is a material, not leftover space. decide where it goes.
- color should have a job. if a hue can't explain what it's doing, it's probably just there to be liked.
- images carry tone faster than words. a mismatched photo can undo a carefully written message.
- consistency builds trust, but rigid consistency builds boredom. leave room for the occasional deliberate exception, and make sure it looks deliberate.
- look at the work small, look at it huge, look at it in poor light, look at it on a bad screen. real life is rarely kind.

**staying sane**

working alone means the boundary between work and everything else is made of habit, not walls. a few soft rituals help: a way of starting that signals the work has begun, and a way of stopping that signals it hasn't followed you home. the exact form matters less than having it.

keep a running list of things that went well, not just things that went wrong. it's easy to remember every stumble and forget every small win, and the imbalance wears on morale.

stay curious about unrelated fields. the best ideas often arrive sideways from architecture, cooking, signage, old manuals, music packaging, plant labels, anything made by people who cared about a problem the designer never thought about.

**open-ended ideas**

- build a small library of thinking prompts instead of templates: questions that unstick a stalled project, like "what would this look like if it were embarrassingly simple?" or "what would the opposite of the obvious choice be?"
- revisit old finished work after enough distance has passed, with a critical eye, to see what aged well and what didn't. maybe write down patterns.
- put together a plain-language guide for clients explaining how the process works, what to expect, and how feedback is most useful. could cut down on repeated explaining and make early conversations calmer.
- try letting chance pick the constraints for a self-initiated project. an arbitrary rule can free up decisions that would otherwise stall.
- collect textures, signage, packaging, handwriting, and other things from the world that weren't designed by designers. sort them by feeling rather than subject, and see what surprising neighbors turn up.
- write short case studies as the work happens rather than reconstructing them afterward. memory tidies the story and drops the interesting mistakes.
- wonder what the studio would make if nobody were paying for it: a typeface, a set of posters, a tiny publication, a game. self-directed work tends to reshape how client work feels.
- think about how to team up with writers, photographers, developers, and printers in a way that stays light and doesn't turn the studio into an agency.
- keep a "questions I don't know the answer to" page and treat it as a map of where to grow.
- explore ways to make the process more visible to clients without making it more burdensome: a shared page that shows sketches and thinking as they develop, perhaps.

**loose thoughts to end on**

good design is mostly attention. attention to the person using the thing, to the details nobody will consciously notice, to the moment where a reader's patience runs out. the rest is craft and stamina.

there's always a temptation to treat each project as a fresh start, but patterns repeat. noticing them, naming them, and keeping them handy is probably the closest thing to a method this studio has, and it's worth keeping loose enough to change.
