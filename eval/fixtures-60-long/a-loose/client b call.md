general working notes

**approach**

the brief is rarely the real problem. there's usually something underneath it, a worry or a bit of internal politics nobody has said out loud. worth asking a slightly odd question early and seeing what comes back. people answer odd questions more honestly than the polite ones.

i try to work out what the work is for before deciding what it should look like. "make it feel premium" isn't a goal, it's a symptom. what is the thing supposed to make happen for the people who see it? if i can't say that in plain words, i'm not ready to open the design file.

small studio advantage: no layers between thinking and making. small studio risk: nobody to tell me I'm wrong. so I lean on a habit of arguing against my own direction before showing it to anybody. if I can't find the weak spot, someone else will, and it'll be more expensive.

- prefer a clear point of view over a safe average
- show less, explain more, but keep the explaining short
- let the constraints do some of the designing
- say the uncomfortable thing early and kindly, not late and sharply

**process**

the early stage should feel messy. loose sketches, rough words, collected references that don't obviously belong together. the mess is where the surprises are. the temptation is to tidy too soon because tidy feels like progress. it isn't always.

when I'm gathering references, I try to collect from outside the obvious category. if the work is for a particular kind of business, looking only at that kind of business gives me their clichés back. architecture, packaging, old signage, textiles, field guides, municipal forms, anything with a strong internal logic. then I ask what the logic is and whether it can be borrowed.

a rough sequence that tends to hold up:

- listen and absorb, with very little output
- write the problem down in plain language and sit with it
- explore wide, then narrow without apologizing
- build a small, honest version of the main idea before building the whole system
- stress-test it against the dull, real-world cases, like the awkward crop, the long name, the cramped space, the bad screen
- present with a story, not a menu

the dull cases matter more than the hero case. anybody can make a hero image look good. the test is what happens on the worst page, in the worst light, in the hands of someone who didn't read the guidelines.

**things to keep in mind**

- clients are experts in their own business and usually not in design. I'm the reverse. the work goes best when both sides treat that as a trade, not a hierarchy.
- feedback is data about a feeling. "make the logo bigger" often means "I don't feel confident yet." fix the confidence, not the logo.
- don't present options I wouldn't be happy to see chosen. a decoy direction will get picked eventually, and then I'm stuck making it.
- write down the reasoning behind choices while it's fresh. future me will not remember why the spacing is the way it is, and neither will the client.
- every file name, layer name, and folder structure is a note to my future self. be kind to that person.
- rest is part of the process. the best solutions often turn up in the shower or on a walk, not at the screen. a tired eye also stops seeing contrast and rhythm, so stepping away is practical, not lazy.
- keep a running list of things I'd do differently. review it before beginning anything new.
- scope drifts quietly. the trick is noticing the drift while it's still a conversation and not yet a problem.
- the handoff is part of the design. if the people receiving the work can't use it confidently, the work isn't finished, however good it looks.

**on communication**

short, specific messages beat long, careful ones. a plain sentence about what I need and why is better than a paragraph of cushioning. when something is unclear, ask right away instead of guessing and hoping. guessing feels efficient and almost never is.

it also helps to separate what's been agreed from what's been discussed. a conversation can feel conclusive and still leave different people with different memories. a quick written recap in plain language keeps everyone on the same page, and it's much easier than untangling things later. that's just a habit worth keeping in general, nothing more.

it's worth noticing how people talk about their own organization. the pronouns they use, whether it's "we" or "they," who they mention without prompting, who they avoid mentioning. that tells me a lot about where decisions really get made and where the work might get stuck.

**craft reminders**

- type does most of the work. get the type right and a lot of other problems quietly disappear.
- whitespace isn't leftover space, it's structure. treat it with the same care as anything else on the page.
- color needs a job. if it's only there for decoration, it's probably noise.
- consistency is a form of kindness to the audience. surprise should be spent deliberately, in a few places, not scattered.
- check everything at small size, in grayscale, and on a bad display. if it survives that, it's sturdy.
- motion should explain, not entertain. if an animation can be removed without anyone missing it, remove it.
- accessibility isn't a layer added at the end. contrast, legibility, and clear hierarchy are just good design wearing practical clothes.

**open-ended ideas**

- a standing habit of making small unpaid experiments, purely to keep the hands curious. nothing precious, nothing shown. a poster that will never be printed, a typeface pairing tested on a made-up headline.
- a personal library of "problems solved well by others," each with a short note on why it works. something to flip through when stuck.
- a plain-language glossary to share with clients, covering terms that get misused in meetings. less jargon, fewer false agreements.
- what would it look like to run a project with the deliverable list written at the end instead of the beginning? probably chaotic, but interesting to think about. maybe a lighter version, where only the purpose is fixed up front and the rest is allowed to emerge.
- a short checklist for the moment a direction feels "almost there" but flat. usually it's one of these: the hierarchy is muddy, the idea is hedged, the details are generic, or the scale is timid.
- ask clients to describe the work as if it already existed and was loved. what do they say about it? what do customers say? it surfaces priorities better than any questionnaire.
- keep a drawer of rejected ideas. they're often right for a different problem later.
- think about how the studio itself comes across. the website, the proposals, the way emails sound. does it show the same care I promise? probably worth a quiet review sometime, when there's a gap in the schedule.
- try explaining a design decision to someone outside the field, like a relative or a neighbor. if they follow it and nod, it's probably grounded. if their eyes glaze, the reasoning is still too abstract.

**loose worries**

- becoming a studio that only repeats what worked before. comfort is a slow leak.
- taking on work out of anxiety instead of interest, then resenting it by the middle.
- letting good relationships slide into vague expectations because they feel friendly enough not to spell out.
- spending so long refining that the work loses its first spark. restraint matters at the end as much as ambition does at the beginning.

**closing thought for the pile**

good work usually comes from paying attention: to the people, to the material, to the small awkward details everybody else has learned to ignore. the rest is craft and stamina. when in doubt, go back to the plain question of what this is supposed to do for the person on the other end, and let the answer shape what comes next.
