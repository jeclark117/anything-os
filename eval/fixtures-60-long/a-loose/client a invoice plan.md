general working notes

**approach**

- start by listening, not drawing. the urge to open a file and push shapes around is strong and almost always premature. the useful early work is figuring out what the person wants to be true after the thing exists.
- ask what they're afraid of. fear tells you more than goals do. "we can't look cheap" or "we don't want to feel like everybody else" shapes a direction faster than any mood board.
- pay attention to the gap between what people say and what they point at. they'll describe minimal and then circle something ornate. believe the pointing.
- write the problem down in plain words and read it back to them. if they wince, that's a cheap correction compared to rebuilding later.
- being small is an advantage. no layers of approval, no handoffs that lose the intent. protect that by keeping the work legible, so the person on the other side can follow what's happening without a translator.
- taste is the product, but taste has to bend toward their world. the work should feel like it belongs to them, not like a souvenir from the studio.

**process**

loose order, not a ritual.

- gather: references, existing materials, the awkward old assets nobody wants to talk about. look at how the thing gets used in real life, not how the brief imagines it.
- sketch badly and quickly. paper still wins for the earliest stage because nothing on paper looks finished enough to defend.
- explore wide, then cut hard. show fewer directions than feels generous. a big spread of options mostly hands the burden of deciding back to the client.
- for every direction shown, say what it's trying to do and what it gives up. every choice trades something away, and naming the trade makes feedback sharper.
- build the unglamorous pieces early: the awkward states, the long text, the empty screen, the error message, the mark that has to survive at tiny size or in dull grey. polish on a showpiece image is easy. consistency in the margins is where quality shows.
- step away from the work before sending it out. fresh eyes catch stray misalignments and the sentence that says too much.
- keep a running file of "why" notes while working. the reasoning evaporates faster than the files do, and it's the part that's hardest to reconstruct when someone asks about a choice.
- when stuck, change the medium. if it's stalled on screen, print it. if it's stalled in layout, write the copy. if it's stalled in words, draw the thing.
- test with real content as soon as possible. placeholder text flatters everything.

**things to keep in mind**

- feedback is usually a symptom. "make the logo bigger" often means "i don't feel seen" or "i'm not sure this is confident enough." ask what's behind it before touching anything.
- scope creeps politely. it arrives as "while you're in there" and "just a small thing." notice it, name it kindly, and keep a running list so the pattern is visible to all sides without anyone feeling accused.
- clear writing in emails beats clever design in decks. be specific about what's needed from them and what happens to the schedule when it doesn't arrive.
- say the awkward thing early. ownership, final say, how decisions get made, who is quietly the person that has to be convinced. these conversations don't improve with age.
- a good working relationship is mostly predictability. people relax when they know what they'll receive and roughly when they'll hear from you.
- don't confuse busy with productive. an inbox full of small replies can feel like work while the real thinking sits untouched.
- rest is part of the job. tired judgment makes confident, wrong calls.
- file hygiene: name things so a stranger could find them. future me counts as a stranger.
- back up everything, then back up the backup.
- keep source files for anything that might be needed again, and export in the formats people actually use, not the ones that look impressive.
- be honest when something falls outside what the studio does well. referring out is a service, not a failure.
- a client who is anxious about the process doesn't need more reassurance, they need more visibility. show the work in progress, rough and labeled as rough.
- watch for the moment when a project stops being about the problem and starts being about the preferences of whoever happens to be loudest in the room.
- praise is as useless as criticism if it can't be traced to something specific. ask what exactly is working so it can be protected.

**open-ended ideas**

- a short plain-language guide handed over at the end of a project: what was made, why, how to use it, what not to do to it. maybe written like a letter rather than a manual.
- a library of "rejected but interesting" directions, kept for the studio's own use as a source of fresh starting points. need to think about how to keep them separate from client work so nothing leaks.
- a standing habit of small experiments with no audience. posters, type studies, odd interfaces, silly tools. see what seeps into the paid work.
- what if proposals read like a story of the project instead of a list of deliverables? it might make the thinking visible earlier and filter for people who care about it.
- voice memos while walking, to capture thoughts before they get tidied into something false.
- a kickoff checklist that's really just a list of good questions, not a form. the aim is to make people talk, not to collect answers.
- find a way to make slow periods useful: tidy the archive, rewrite templates, study work that humbles me.
- show process without it feeling like a performance. maybe rough work presented as is, with notes in the margin, no fake polish.
- a reflection after each project: what felt smooth, what dragged, what i'd tell a friend about it. short enough that it actually gets written.
- explore whether a visible house style helps or hurts. a recognizable hand attracts the right people but can quietly narrow what people ask for.
- try describing the studio's work to a stranger in plain words and see which parts they find interesting. it might not be the parts that get put on the website.
- a habit of deliberately making something ugly before making something good, to loosen up the judgment that kills early ideas.
- what would it look like to let a project end more gracefully? a proper wrap-up, a clear handover, and a moment to acknowledge what was built together.

**loose thoughts**

the hardest part of working alone is that nobody interrupts a bad idea. a collaborator would raise an eyebrow, and that eyebrow is worth replacing with something: a trusted friend who gets shown rough work, a habit of reading the brief aloud, a practice of arguing against my own favorite option.

there's also the matter of confidence. it helps to remember that clients are hiring judgment, and judgment is built from lots of small, unglamorous reps. the work doesn't need to be dazzling every time. it needs to be considered, honest, and well finished.

some projects want to be loud and some want to be almost invisible. the skill is telling which is which before starting, and having the nerve to say so when the brief asks for the opposite of what the situation needs.

finally, keep a little room for play. the best ideas tend to show up sideways, while doing something that wasn't supposed to matter.
