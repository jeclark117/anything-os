client feedback themes

general working notes

**approach**

- start with the problem in the client's own words before any pictures get made. their phrasing carries more than the brief does. words like clean, warm, bold, and trustworthy mean wildly different things depending on who says them, so ask what they'd point at if they had to point
- keep early sketches ugly on purpose. if a rough pass looks finished, people react to the polish instead of the direction. grey boxes, obvious placeholder words, shapes that clearly aren't settled
- work from constraints outward. the content, the audience, and the medium settle a lot of layout questions before taste even enters the room
- treat a client's existing materials as evidence, not baggage. what they already have, even when it's messy, shows what they're comfortable with and what they've been quietly working around
- look at what the neighbors in their space are doing, then deliberately ask what would be strange to copy. sameness is easy to fall into without noticing

**process**

- keep a running scratch file per project for stray observations, loose questions, and half-remembered reactions from calls. most of the useful stuff shows up there rather than in formal notes
- write the reason behind a design choice in a sentence while it's fresh. future me forgets, and so does the client
- separate making from judging. produce a pile of options with the inner critic switched off, then step back and cull hard
- name files for what they are, not how recent they are. version suffixes that pile up are a trap, and nobody can tell which is current
- at the end of a work session, leave a note for the next session: where the thread was, what was nagging, what to poke at. cold starts are expensive when there's nobody else to ask
- review work at a different size and on a different surface than where it was made. problems hide at the scale you built them at, and printing something out still exposes things a screen forgives
- step away from a layout and come back with fresh eyes. the awkward spacing that was invisible becomes obvious almost immediately
- when stuck, change the medium. pencil, a text-only outline, a spoken description. a different format shakes loose a different kind of thinking

**things to keep in mind**

- feedback is usually about a feeling, and the proposed fix is usually a guess. dig for the feeling
- when a client says they dislike something, ask what it reminds them of. the answer is often more useful than the criticism
- consistency is a kindness to whoever uses the thing later. spacing, type scale, color roles, naming
- accessibility isn't a layer that goes on top. contrast, tap targets, focus states, readable sizes, and sensible reading order are part of the basic shape of the work
- the unglamorous screens carry the trust: empty states, error states, loading states, confirmation messages. people remember how a product behaves when something goes wrong
- real content breaks layouts. test with absurdly long names, missing images, awkward translations, and text that someone pasted in from somewhere else
- the urge to add is strong. most improvements come from taking something out
- decoration that can't explain itself is usually noise
- good type does more work than most people credit. before reaching for another color or effect, check whether the hierarchy could be solved with size, weight, and space
- an idea doesn't deserve to survive just because it was hard to find
- be honest about what is taste and what is function. clients can argue with taste all day, but they can't argue with a button nobody can find

**working with clients**

- explain reasoning in their terms, not in design vocabulary. a rationale they can repeat to their own colleagues is worth more than a clever one
- show fewer options with a clear point of view rather than a buffet. a wide spread of choices pushes the decision work onto the person least equipped to do it
- be wary of feedback that arrives as a committee average. it tends to sand off everything interesting
- silence isn't approval. occasionally it's confusion dressed up as politeness
- a short written recap after a call helps, because shared understanding drifts when nobody pins it down
- notice who is actually reacting to the work and who is reacting on behalf of someone else who isn't in the room
- keep a list of the questions clients ask repeatedly. those are candidates for a template, an explainer, or a better opening conversation
- tell people early when a request pulls against another thing they said they cared about. it's easier to surface a tension than to untangle it after it's baked in
- praise is nice but vague praise is useless. try to find out which specific thing landed

**handoff and cleanup**

people like fast turnarounds
mobile always comes up late

- organize files as if a stranger will open them in a panic, because that stranger might be me
- layers, components, and styles get plain names that describe purpose rather than appearance. "primary action" ages better than "blue button"
- leave a short readme-style explanation of the system's logic: why the scale looks the way it does, where the rules bend, what was tried and dropped
- export assets in the formats people will really need, and double-check a few of them in the environment where they'll live
- gather the rationale notes into something readable so the reasoning travels with the work

**open-ended ideas**

- a small library of annotated before-and-after examples for explaining why a change matters without a lecture
- a reusable checklist of states and edge cases to walk through before anything goes out the door
- a pattern notebook of layouts that worked, each with a line about why, so the lessons don't evaporate
- a way to tag feedback by theme so patterns surface across projects rather than living in separate threads
- a plain-language glossary to share with clients so words like system, component, and hierarchy stop sounding like jargon
- presenting work as a short story instead of a slide deck: here's the problem, here's what got tried, here's what held up
- recording a quick voice memo after reviews to capture mood that written notes flatten
- describing a design aloud without showing it. if the description comes out muddled, the design probably is too
- a folder of things that annoy me in other people's interfaces, each with a line on what exactly goes wrong
- a habit of sketching a problem on paper before opening any software, just to see whether the idea survives without polish
- a catalog of small delights: transitions, microcopy, empty-state illustrations that made me smile, and what made them work
- trying a review format where the client narrates what they see out loud while I stay quiet and take notes
- writing the explanation of a design before making it, as a test of whether the idea is clear enough to build

**stray thoughts**

the best work tends to come from a clear question, not a clever answer. a lot of wasted effort comes from solving something that wasn't quite the problem. it's worth spending extra effort at the start on what is really being asked, even when the pull is to start making things right away.

working solo means there's no one to catch mistakes in passing, so habits have to do the job a colleague would. checklists, fresh-eyes reviews, and written reasons are all just ways of simulating a second opinion.

it's easy to confuse being busy with being useful. some of the most valuable work is a short conversation that stops a bad direction before anything gets built.

and finally, keep some room for play. experiments with no client attached are where new habits and unexpected techniques come from, and they quietly feed everything else.
