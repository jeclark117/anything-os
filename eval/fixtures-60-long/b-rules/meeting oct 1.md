general working notes

approach

the studio is just me, which is the best and worst thing about it. everything runs through my head, so the real job is keeping that head uncluttered. when a request comes in it either gets noted down right away or it gets let go. vaguely remembered requests are where trust quietly leaks out.

i try to treat every project as a conversation more than a delivery. the files are evidence of a shared understanding, and when that understanding drifts the files get expensive to fix. so a lot of early effort goes into making sure we're picturing the same thing, even if that means showing something embarrassingly rough and asking "is this the right neighborhood?"

good work usually feels obvious afterward. that's the goal, and also why the value is hard to explain. the obvious-looking result hides plenty of rejected paths. worth keeping a loose record of those paths, because clients sometimes circle back to an idea that got dropped, and it's nicer to say here's why it didn't hold up than to rebuild the argument from memory.

process

- start with the problem in plain words. if i can't say what the thing is for in a plain line a stranger would follow, the visuals will wander.
- gather references loosely, then throw most of them away. the useful references still make sense when i can't remember why i saved them.
- work rough before polished. grayscale, boxes, placeholder text. color and typefaces feel like decisions but they're mostly dressing. structure is where the real choices live.
- show work at the stage where feedback is cheap. a polished comp invites arguments about shade and spacing; a rough sketch invites the real question of whether the direction is right at all.
- step away between pushes of work. a screen that looked finished while i was deep in it usually looks wrong after a proper break.
- keep file names boring and predictable. future me is a stranger in a hurry.
- write the reason for a notable choice right next to the choice. it costs almost nothing and saves a lot of guessing later.
- when stuck, change the medium. pen and paper, a printout taped to the wall, reading the copy aloud. getting stuck is usually just a symptom of staring at the same view.

things to keep in mind

- feedback is information about a feeling, not a specification. when somebody says "make it pop" they're describing a reaction. the work is figuring out which reaction they're after.
- silence is not approval. a quiet client might be happy, busy, confused, or politely unconvinced, and those call for very different responses. a gentle direct question usually beats guessing.
- scope creep rarely announces itself. it shows up as "while you're in there" requests. naming the request out loud and seeing whether it still sounds small is a decent test.
- the people around a project don't always want the same thing. the person who approves, the person who uses it, and the person who has to maintain it all carry different worries. worth tracking who cares about what.
- there's no fresh set of eyes here, so typos and misalignments sail through. read things aloud, flip the layout, shrink it down, view it from across the room.
- real text breaks layouts. designs built around tidy placeholder copy fall apart with long names, awkward titles, and translated strings. test with ugly text early.
- accessibility isn't a layer that goes on top. contrast, readable sizes, clear focus states, and text that survives being resized are part of the structure.
- trends age work quickly. borrowing from them is fine, but build the foundation from something steadier.
- energy is a material too. tired creative work usually comes out defensive and safe.

handoff and housekeeping

- tidy as i go instead of at the end. named layers, shared styles, spacing defined in a central place.
- leave a short note alongside the files explaining the reasoning, the type and color choices, and what's safe to change versus what's holding the rest together.
- ask what the receiving party is actually going to do with the files before deciding what to hand over. a developer, a printer, and a marketing person want very different things.
- label archives properly. old work is a goldmine for reuse, but only if i can find it and remember what it was.
- keep a running list of small annoyances in my own setup, like slow exports and clumsy folders. they add up to real friction.

open-ended ideas

- a standing parking-lot note for ideas that don't belong to any project. sometimes the best idea for a client turns up while working for somebody else.
- a personal library of tiny experiments: type combinations, motion curves, color relationships, each with a line about why it was interesting.
- what would a post-project reflection look like if it were brief and candid? what surprised me, what i'd skip again, what the client actually used versus what they asked for.
- is there a way to show the invisible work, the rejected paths and the reasoning, as part of how the work is presented, so it gets valued instead of assumed?
- try designing with constraints on purpose: a tiny palette, no imagery, no gradients, a rigid grid. see what the limits force.
- review work on the worst screen available, deliberately. small, dim, smudged. if it holds up there it probably holds up anywhere.
- a short recorded walkthrough in place of a long email. talking through work usually carries purpose that text flattens. curious whether clients would actually watch them.
- a standing set of questions for the start of a project, refined with experience. why does this exist? who is upset if it fails? what does success sound like in the client's own words? what would they be embarrassed by?
- a gentler way of saying no. maybe a loose list of the kinds of work that usually go badly, and the early warning signs.
- draw by hand before opening the computer, even when it feels slow. the rough hand sketch has a looseness that rarely comes back once the software is open.
- treat writing as design. button labels, error messages, empty states. the biggest improvements usually come from words rather than pixels.
- maybe a design diary for each project, just loose lines, as raw material for later writing about the work.
- collaborating with a writer or illustrator now and then, just to get friction from a different discipline.
- look at how other fields handle critique, like theater, cooking, and architecture, and borrow the useful rituals.
- study ordinary signage, packaging, and forms out in the world and ask what the designer was dealing with.

loose thoughts

clients rarely remember the process; they remember how the work felt to go through. calm, clear, and responsive counts for more than people admit. a project can have a beautiful outcome and still feel bad if the communication was jumpy.

the best projects seem to have a person on the other side who is curious rather than anxious. that's hard to manufacture, but asking questions that make the work feel like a joint exploration seems to help.

taste is not a ranking. my preferences are an input, not the verdict. the goal is a result that fits the audience, which is frequently not me.

and rest matters. a studio that depends entirely on my own stamina has to treat stamina as infrastructure.
