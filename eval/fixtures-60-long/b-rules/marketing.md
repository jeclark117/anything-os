marketing

general notes

**approach**

marketing has to earn its place when the studio is this small. anything that pulls attention away from design work needs to bring in better conversations, not just more noise. reach for its own sake isn't the goal. the goal is being seen by people who already care about what the studio does well.

- work that shows process tends to land better than finished work alone. people like the messy middle: sketches, rejected directions, the moment a layout finally clicks.
- consistency beats intensity. a steady trickle of decent posts probably does more than a burst of polished ones followed by silence.
- the studio's voice should sound like the person behind it. casual, a bit opinionated, not trying to sound bigger than it is.
- try to describe what the studio does in plain words a stranger would get immediately. if the description needs a paragraph, it's not ready.

**process**

a loose loop seems to work better than a rigid plan:

- notice what's already happening in the design work that could be shown with almost no extra effort. screenshots, before and after comparisons, a type pairing that surprised me.
- save those scraps in one place as they happen instead of trying to remember them later.
- turn the best scraps into something small and complete. a short caption, a clean image, a clear point.
- look at what got a response and what got ignored, then adjust without overreacting to any single result.

keep the review part light. it's easy to start treating every post like an experiment and end up spending more time on analytics than on design. a quick glance at what resonated is enough most of the time.

**paid vs. organic**

paid promotion is best treated as a way to amplify things that already work on their own. if a post gets a warm reception without help, it probably deserves a push. if it falls flat, spending on it just buys more people ignoring it.

- test small variations of the same idea rather than totally different ones, so it's clear what caused a difference.
- watch for the gap between attention and interest. lots of taps and no inquiries means the creative is catchy but the message is off.
- be wary of targeting that's too broad. a smaller audience that fits is worth more than a large one that doesn't.
- pay attention to where people land after they click. a great ad pointing at a confusing page wastes the whole effort.

**things to keep in mind**

- the people who hire a small studio are often buying trust as much as skill. anything that builds trust, like clear writing, honest case studies, and visible care, is marketing even if it never looks like it.
- avoid chasing trends just because they're loud. a format that suits someone else's personality can feel forced on mine.
- don't let marketing become procrastination. polishing a profile is easier than doing hard design thinking, and it's tempting for exactly that reason.
- keep a record of where inquiries actually come from. asking new contacts how they found the studio is cheap and tells more than most dashboards.
- leave room for word of mouth. past collaborators who had a good experience are quietly the strongest channel, and they don't show up in any ad report.
- be careful with client work. showing it publicly needs permission and sensitivity, and some of the best projects can't be shared at all. have a plan for talking about them in general terms.

**content ideas, loosely**

- short breakdowns of a design problem and how it got untangled, told as a small story with a clear ending.
- a series on small details that make interfaces or printed pieces feel considered, like spacing, alignment, and the weight of a line.
- honest posts about what didn't work and what I'd do differently. these probably build more credibility than a wall of wins.
- quick takes on everyday design that people see but rarely notice, like signage, packaging, and menus.
- a recurring format that's cheap to produce, so it can keep going when the schedule gets busy.
- behind-the-scenes glimpses of the workspace, the reference pile, the half-finished boards. these humanize the studio without much effort.
- answers to questions that prospective clients keep asking, written so they're useful even to someone who never hires the studio.

**open questions**

- how much of the studio's personality should show up versus staying polished and professional? the balance might differ depending on the audience.
- is there a way to make the marketing feel like an extension of the design practice rather than a separate chore?
- would a small mailing list be worth building alongside social channels, since it isn't at the mercy of an algorithm?
- what would it look like to partner with other small creative businesses for mutual exposure, without it feeling transactional?
- which kinds of projects does the studio want more of? marketing should lean toward those and quietly stop attracting the rest.
- how to measure whether something is working when the sales cycle is slow and fuzzy. maybe the signals are softer: replies, saved posts, referrals, comments from people in the field.

**reminders to self**

- clarity over cleverness, nearly every time.
- if a piece of marketing feels like a chore, it will read like one.
- show the thinking, not just the output.
- a good portfolio piece with a good explanation outperforms a great piece with none.
- stay curious about what the audience actually responds to, even when it isn't what I expected or wanted.
- keep the whole thing humane. the aim is to meet people who'd enjoy working together, not to squeeze attention out of strangers.

**loose threads**

- there may be value in writing a short, plain statement of what the studio believes about design, then letting every post quietly point back to it.
- case studies could be structured around the problem the client faced rather than the deliverable, since prospects see themselves in problems.
- look at how other small studios present themselves, not to copy, but to spot what feels generic and avoid it.
- consider how the work looks in a thumbnail. so much first contact happens at tiny sizes, and some projects only make sense when you see them large.
- keep a running list of phrases clients use to describe their own needs. that language is better than anything invented in a vacuum.
