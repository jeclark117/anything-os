**approach**

my default is to slow down at the start so the middle isn't a mess. before any layout or palette talk, i want the problem written in plain words that a stranger could follow. if i can't say what the work is for without leaning on words like "brand" or "vibe," i don't understand it yet. so the early part is mostly asking, listening, and writing down what people actually say instead of what i think they meant.

being a small studio means i'm the strategist, the designer, the project manager, and the person who answers the inbox. that's handy because nothing gets lost in a handoff, and risky because nobody is around to poke holes in my assumptions. so i write short summaries after conversations, mostly to catch myself rather than to catch anybody else.

some leanings i keep coming back to:

- rough and wrong beats polished and early. a sketch that is obviously unfinished invites an honest reaction. a polished comp invites politeness.
- show less and explain more. a huge spread of options usually means i'm dodging having a point of view.
- keep a running list of open questions, separate from the task list. tasks get checked off, questions quietly rot if they aren't written down.
- treat limits as material. a narrow palette, awkward content, a tired old logo that has to stay: these shape the work in ways that are often better than total freedom.
- if something feels forced, the problem is probably upstream of the thing i'm staring at.

**process**

this is a loose shape, not a rulebook. it bends depending on the client and how much clarity they bring.

- listen: gather what already exists, who it's for, what's been tried, what people grumble about. old materials are a goldmine for finding out what a client actually values versus what they say they value.
- frame: write the problem, the audience, and what a good outcome would feel like, all in plain language. read it back to the client and watch their face or their reply for hesitation.
- explore: go wide and ugly. words before pictures, paper before screens. nothing here is precious.
- narrow: choose a direction and pressure test it against real content instead of placeholder text. this is where most designs either hold up or fall apart.
- build out: systems, components, edge cases, states nobody mentioned but everybody will hit.
- hand over: tidy files, clear labels, and a short written explanation of why things are the way they are.

real content is the great equalizer. the longest name, the shortest headline, the image that's slightly off, the paragraph nobody proofread. ask for the awkward stuff early and drop it into the layout before getting attached to anything.

when something i depend on is missing, the useful move is to find the work that doesn't need it. there's almost always structure, type, spacing, and naming to sort out while waiting. idle time disguised as patience is still idle time.

**keep in mind**

- people rarely complain about things they can't name. translate vague feedback into questions. "make it pop" really means "what should be noticed first?" and that's something we can actually answer.
- scope creeps in through friendly small asks. none of them feel like a big deal, which is exactly why they add up. note them, name them kindly, and keep a visible record.
- file names, layer names, and folder structure are part of the product. a future version of me, or a stranger, will inherit them.
- accessibility is a design input, not a final pass. contrast, size, and clarity are easier to get right early than to retrofit.
- white space is a feature, but it can also hide indecision. ask whether the emptiness is doing a job.
- step away from a piece before judging it. fresh eyes catch things that effort never will.
- write down why choices were made, because memory of reasoning fades faster than the files do. a sentence or so beside each major move is plenty.
- email tone: warm, direct, short. most misunderstandings come from vague phrasing, not bad intent.
- the client's team usually has more context than they think they're sharing. the casual aside in a call is often the real brief.
- be honest when something isn't working, including my own work. it is cheaper to say so early.
- protect the quiet stretches. a solo practice runs on focus, and every context switch has a cost that never shows up on any list.
- good work can still land badly if the presentation is rushed. how it's shown matters almost as much as what is shown.

**things i find myself wondering about**

a little pile of loose ideas, none of them promised to anybody, including me.

- a small library of reusable kickoff questions, sorted by what they uncover: audience, fear, taste, internal politics, past disasters. some of them could be surprisingly personal in a useful way.
- try building the type system before touching color, and see whether it changes how the whole project feels. type tends to carry more personality than people give it credit for.
- write the about page, or the pitch, before designing anything. if the words are mushy, the visuals will be too.
- print things and pin them on a wall. work reads differently when it's physical and in a room, and flaws that hide on a screen show up fast.
- show a draft to somebody outside the field and ask them to describe what they see, without any setup. their vocabulary is often better than mine.
- keep a "tired of it" list of trends and habits i reach for out of reflex: certain gradients, certain rounded shapes, certain safe font pairings. then deliberately try something else on a small, low-stakes piece.
- experiment with exercises built on strict limits: a restrained palette, no images at all, only what can be made with type and space. see what survives.
- think about how work ages. designs that lean hard on a moment's look tend to age badly, while ones built on clear structure and honest content tend to hold. what are the signs of each?
- a shared glossary with clients, so the words "modern," "clean," and "friendly" mean the same thing to both sides of the table. maybe built out of pictures and examples instead of definitions.
- a gentle template for looking back after a project wraps: what surprised me, what i'd cut, what i'd keep, what i'd do differently if the same situation came back around.
- ways to say no gracefully, including to work that looks appealing but doesn't fit. worth drafting a few phrasings in advance so it doesn't feel so heavy in the moment.
- explore how motion and sound might add meaning without becoming decoration. most of the time less movement is better, but the exceptions are interesting.
- a habit of collecting good mistakes: unplanned results that turned out better than the intended ones. keep them somewhere visible.

**stray reminders**

- clarity first, cleverness second.
- the client isn't the enemy of good work; vagueness is.
- being easy to work with is a design skill.
- leave room for the project to surprise me.
- if the work feels heavy, simplify the question, not the effort.
