general working notes, loose and unfinished

**approach**

- begin with what the person is actually trying to get done, not what the brief says. briefs describe symptoms. the real problem sits a layer underneath and is frequently a little awkward to say out loud.
- look at what already exists before touching anything. old assets, abandoned ideas, things the client keeps apologizing for. the apologies are a map.
- keep the circle of people involved small. extra opinions add drag, and most are reactions to taste rather than to the goal.
- show work rough when possible. polished work invites polite nodding, rough work invites real answers.
- notice what people say when they don't think they're being asked. offhand comments on calls frequently matter more than the formal feedback that follows.
- let the goal be the judge, not my own preferences. if something looks great but doesn't help, it's decoration.

**process, roughly**

nothing rigid, just the shape that keeps repeating.

- absorb. read everything that was sent over, then read it again asking a different question: what are they nervous about?
- collect references but stay suspicious of them. a pile of references becomes a crutch. pull out the feeling, not the surface.
- sketch badly on paper. the screen makes everything look finished too early.
- build a small system of type, color, and spacing before building pages. if the system is sound, pages come easily. if it isn't, polish won't rescue them.
- write real copy as soon as possible. placeholder text hides problems, and layouts built around fake words fall apart when real words arrive.
- check the cramped small-screen version early, since it exposes what actually matters.
- leave breathing room between making and judging. coming back with fresh eyes catches more than more staring.
- keep a trail of why choices were made, even in rough form.

**things to keep in mind**

- clients rarely want exactly what they ask for. they want to feel safe about what they're buying. reassurance is part of the work.
- silence after sending something isn't automatically bad news. people are busy, or unsure how to phrase a reaction.
- ask questions that require a real answer rather than a yes. "does this feel like you?" gets further than "does this look good?"
- scope creeps through kindness. small favors pile up into a whole other job without anybody noticing.
- write down the reasoning behind choices while it's fresh. later the reasoning evaporates and only the artifact remains.
- people trust specifics. a vague promise sounds like a sales line, a concrete observation about their business sounds like care.
- working solo is a strength and a liability. fast, no handoffs, no meetings about meetings, but nobody to catch a blind spot.
- the thing a client is proudest of is usually the thing that most needs gentle handling.
- tired judgment is unreliable judgment. tweaks made while exhausted are wrong in subtle ways.

**on feedback**

feedback comes in layers. the surface layer is about color, size, wording. underneath is a feeling the person can't quite name, like "this doesn't seem like us" or "I'm worried it looks cheap." the surface comment is usually a disguise for the feeling. a useful move is to ask what they imagine a visitor would think, because it gets them talking about the audience instead of defending their taste.

it also helps to separate feedback about the work from feedback about the person giving it. a reaction might say more about their mood, their last frustrating experience, or what a relative said recently than about the design. that doesn't make it wrong, just worth weighing.

when pieces of feedback conflict, resist splitting the difference. a compromise between opposing ideas is frequently weaker than either. better to go back to the goal and see which idea serves it.

**when stuck**

- change the medium. switch from screen to paper, from paper to talking out loud.
- make the worst version deliberately. it unlocks something and shows exactly what's wrong with it.
- remove things until it breaks, then put back only what was missing.
- explain the problem to an imaginary skeptic. holes show up mid-explanation.
- walk away. answers arrive while washing dishes.
- look at work from entirely different fields: signage, packaging, editorial layouts, maps. structure steals well across disciplines.

**quality checks, informal**

- squint test: blur the page. does the hierarchy survive?
- read all text aloud. awkward phrasing shows up immediately.
- check legibility in bad conditions: glare, small devices, tired eyes.
- look at empty states, error states, loading states. that's where a site feels either cared for or abandoned.
- see what happens when text runs longer than expected, or a name has unusual characters.
- turn images off and see if the page still makes sense.
- move through everything using only the keyboard.
- ask whether a stranger would know what to do next right away.

**on restraint**

most projects improve by subtraction. there's a pull toward adding sections, effects, and flourishes to show effort, but visitors don't reward effort, they reward clarity. every element should earn its place by helping somebody do something or feel something. if it only exists because it was fun to make, it belongs in the scrap file instead.

the same goes for explanation. long rationales can read as defensiveness. when the work is working, it needs less justification.

**habits worth protecting**

- a running scrap file of partial thoughts. most are junk, some turn into the best part of a project.
- long stretches of quiet. design thinking needs uninterrupted room, and messages can wait.
- plain file names, so future me isn't decoding. anything named "final final" is a warning sign.
- generous saving of versions. storage is cheaper than regret.
- a clean handoff checklist at the end of a project so nothing gets forgotten: fonts, assets, access, documentation, a plain-language guide for maintaining things.

**open-ended ideas**

- a small library of reusable page sections that are deliberately plain, to speed up early drafts without making every project look alike. risk: sameness.
- a plain-language "how I work" explainer for new clients covering what to expect and what's needed from them. might cut down on repetitive explaining.
- short retrospectives after a project wraps: what was easy, what dragged, what surprised. even rough ones build a useful pattern over time.
- a way of presenting options that doesn't overwhelm: fewer directions that are more distinct from each other, rather than many near-identical variations.
- a personal glossary of phrases clients use and what they usually mean. "make it pop" probably means more contrast. "feels corporate" probably means too stiff.
- presenting work as a story rather than a reveal: what was the problem, what did exploration show, why this direction.
- short recorded walkthroughs instead of long text explanations. voice carries meaning better than paragraphs.
- what would a site look like if it were designed for the slowest connection and the smallest screen before anything else?
- looking back at old projects after enough time has passed, to see what aged well and what didn't.
- ways to say no gracefully to work that's a poor fit, and whether a template for that is worth keeping.
- a playground of low-stakes experiments, like odd typography pairings or unusual layouts, that feeds real projects without risking them.

**loose closing thought**

the work is mostly noticing, asking, and editing. the visible part, the drawing and arranging, is the smaller portion of it.
