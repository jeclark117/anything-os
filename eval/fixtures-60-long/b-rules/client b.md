client b

general working notes

**approach**

- listen more than draw in the early stretch. the way people talk about a business tells you what the visuals need to do, and it's usually different from the brief as written.
- a refresh isn't a rebuild. work out what people already recognize and like about the existing look before touching anything. some of it is real equity, some of it is just habit, and it helps to know which is which.
- keep a running list of words that keep coming up when people describe the brand. those words end up steering the visual choices more than any mood board does.
- gather everything that already exists: signage, packaging, stationery, social avatars, email footers, slide decks, anything printed. laying it all out on a big table shows where the brand has drifted.
- ask what's been annoying about the current look in everyday use. practical complaints (too fiddly when small, awkward on dark backgrounds, doesn't sit well in a round frame) are gold.
- try to understand who the audience really is, not who the client hopes it is. the gap between those says a lot.

**process thoughts**

- sketch on paper and keep it loose and ugly for a good while. screens make everything look finished too early, and finished-looking work gets defended too hard.
- explore wide, then narrow. leave room for directions that feel slightly wrong, because they often clarify what's right.
- stress-test marks properly: tiny, enormous, solid black, reversed out, on busy photos, on textured stock, blurred, squinted at from across the room. if it only works under ideal conditions it doesn't really work.
- build the mark and the guide side by side rather than finishing the mark and then writing rules around it. writing the rules tends to expose weak spots in the mark.
- keep file organization boring and consistent: clear naming, version notes, a folder for rejected ideas. rejected ideas come back more often than expected.
- export work in progress regularly so there's a trail of how the thinking evolved. handy when somebody asks why a choice was made.
- step away from the work and come back with fresh eyes. flipping the artwork upside down or viewing it in grayscale catches problems that familiarity hides.
- print things out. a mark on actual paper, taped to an actual wall, behaves differently than it does on a monitor.

**the style guide side**

- a guide nobody opens is a failed guide. write it for the busiest, least design-minded person who will ever touch the brand.
- show, don't tell. examples of right and wrong beat paragraphs of description every time.
- cover the unglamorous stuff: clear space around the mark, smallest allowed size, what to do over photos, what to do when a background fights the colors, which file formats suit print and which suit screen.
- typography deserves real thought about fallbacks. what happens in a document sent to somebody without the typeface installed? licensing is worth checking early, since it depends on where the type will live (web, apps, print, video).
- name the colors in plain language as well as technical values, and check contrast for readability. consider how they shift between print and screen, and how they look on a cheap, badly calibrated display.
- voice and attitude can sit in the guide if the client wants it, but only if somebody will actually own it. otherwise it goes stale and quietly contradicts what the business does.
- leave room for growth. what happens when a new product line, a partnership, or a sub-brand shows up? a good system bends without snapping.
- keep the language friendly. a guide that scolds gets ignored; a guide that explains the reasoning gets followed.
- include a short section on common mistakes: stretching, recoloring, adding drop shadows, cramming the mark into corners. people do these things innocently.

**things to keep in mind**

- scope creep wears the nicest disguise: "while you're at it, could you also..." keep a parking-lot list for those and raise them as separate conversations instead of absorbing them quietly.
- feedback from groups gets muddy. try to learn who actually holds the final say and who is just weighing in, and steer reactions toward goals rather than personal taste.
- present rationale alongside visuals. a mark shown cold invites gut reactions; a mark shown with its reasoning invites discussion.
- people anchor on whatever they see earliest, so be careful what goes into the early rounds. don't show anything you wouldn't be happy to see chosen.
- guard against the "my nephew could do that" reaction by showing the thinking and the constraints the design had to solve. simple looking is usually the result of a lot of removal.
- check for accidental resemblance to existing marks in the same field. a look around the category is cheap insurance against awkward surprises.
- remember the people who will live with the work: the person who updates the website, the person who orders printing, the person who builds slides in a hurry. design for their reality.
- handover matters. deliver source files, usable exports, and a plain explanation of what's what, so the client isn't stuck needing the studio for trivial changes.
- being a solo studio means nobody is around to bounce ideas off. build in some outside eyes on purpose, even if it's only a trusted friend who's honest about what they see.
- keep notes on what the client reacts to emotionally, not just what they say they like. the two don't always match.
- protect the energy that goes into the work. a refresh can drag when everybody has opinions, and fatigue leads to safe, forgettable choices.

**open-ended ideas**

- what if the guide had a "how it feels" section made of photographs and textures instead of words?
- maybe build a small kit of reusable pattern pieces drawn from the mark, so the brand has something to play with beyond the mark itself.
- could motion be worth a quiet mention even if it isn't requested? a simple animated version of the mark might be a useful extra for social and video.
- an "ugly examples" page: real mistakes spotted in the wild, anonymized, to show what to avoid without pointing fingers.
- ask people who've never heard of the business to describe what the mark suggests. fresh eyes catch odd readings that insiders can't see.
- listen to how the name sounds spoken aloud, in a call or in a hallway, and think about whether the visual identity supports how people naturally say it.
- try drawing the mark from memory repeatedly. whatever keeps getting lost is probably too complicated.
- look at what the brand does when it's quiet: no mark at all, just color, shape, and type. if that still feels like them, the system is strong.
- keep a swipe file of things that feel right but come from far outside the industry: hardware store packaging, old posters, hand-painted signs, seed packets.
- consider a plain-language one-sheet version of the guide for people who just need the essentials, with the full guide behind it for the details.
- an audit template for the studio's own use could be reused across projects: a checklist of what to collect, what to ask, what to test.
- think about how the brand ages. does it still look right when it's faded, scuffed, or printed badly? good identities survive rough treatment.

**loose thoughts**

- the best brand work usually feels obvious in hindsight, and that's the goal even though it never feels obvious while making it.
- consistency beats cleverness. a modest system applied carefully will outperform a brilliant one applied sloppily.
- when stuck, go back to the list of words. the answer is almost always already in there.
- leave some imperfection in. a mark with a little character is easier to love than one that's perfectly sterile.
