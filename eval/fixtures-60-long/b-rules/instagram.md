general working notes

**approach**

Most of the work is listening. A brief rarely says what the person actually needs, so a lot of the early effort goes into finding the question underneath the question. I like to ask what would make the whole thing feel like a success, and then what would make it feel like a failure. The failure answer is usually more honest.

Stay curious about the subject even when it seems dull. Dull subjects often hide the best details: a strange habit particular to an industry, a phrase insiders use without noticing, a ritual that happens constantly and never gets looked at. Distinctive work tends to come from those details, not from the category everyone already recognizes.

Working alone means nobody in the room to argue with, so I have to build ways of arguing with myself. Walk away from a layout and come back cold. Print it and pin it to the wall. Squint at it from across the room. Turn it upside down, flip it in a mirror. If it only works when I'm leaning in close, it doesn't work.

**process**

- start loose, on paper if possible. the hand finds things the cursor doesn't
- build a set of directions that are truly different from each other, not variations on a theme. the temptation is to make several versions of the same idea and call it range
- keep a folder of rejected ideas. plenty of them become useful later, just in a different project
- name files honestly. "final" is a lie waiting to happen
- settle type early. it carries so much personality that I'd rather argue about it before color, not after
- test in black and white before adding color. if the structure holds without it, color is a bonus instead of a crutch
- look at the work at the smallest size it will ever appear and at the largest. each should feel intentional
- let the rough stage stay rough for longer than feels comfortable. polish too soon and every flaw in the thinking gets hidden under nice rendering

Feedback is its own skill. When someone says they don't like something, the useful move is to ask what they were expecting to see. People are usually better at describing expectations than critiquing a design. A comment like "it feels too cold" is a gift if I can work out what warm means to that person, and a trap if I just add orange.

Present work with the reasoning attached, not just the visuals. Not as a defense, more like a map. If a person can follow how I got somewhere, they can disagree with a specific step instead of rejecting the whole thing.

**things to keep in mind**

- restraint is harder than decoration. adding is easy, knowing what to leave out is the craft
- empty space isn't wasted space
- consistency beats cleverness over a long stretch. a clever idea that can't survive repetition isn't a system
- accessibility isn't an extra layer. contrast, legibility, and clear hierarchy are part of whether the work does its job
- be wary of trends. they date quickly and make everybody's work look like everybody else's
- good enough is a real stopping point, but only if I'm honest about whether it's good enough or just tiring
- protect energy. creative work is uneven, and forcing it through a flat stretch tends to produce stuff I have to redo anyway
- keep the boring admin boring. build templates for what repeats so attention goes to what doesn't
- the client's audience matters more than the client's taste, and more than mine

**when stuck**

Change the medium. Change the question. Make the thing worse on purpose and see what survives. Tidy the desk, take a walk, wash dishes. Ask what the work would look like if it were easy. Most blocks are really a fear of committing to a mediocre direction, and the way through is usually to make the mediocre version quickly so it stops haunting the page.

Another trick: explain the problem out loud to an imaginary stranger. Halfway through the explanation the answer often turns up, or at least the part that isn't actually the problem falls away.

**taste**

Taste is a muscle, and also a trap. It grows by looking at lots of things critically, including things I dislike, and working out exactly why I dislike them. It becomes a trap when it hardens into reflex, when I reject anything unfamiliar just because it doesn't match my habits. Worth checking now and then whether a strong opinion is a judgment or just a comfort.

It helps to stay in touch with other makers, especially ones whose work looks nothing like mine. Swapping critiques with peers, or showing rough work to a trusted outsider who will say "I don't get it," does more than any amount of private polishing. Isolation is the real occupational hazard of working solo. Shared reference points fade, and it gets easy to mistake familiarity for quality.

**open ideas**

- a running collection of things noticed: odd lettering on shopfronts, unexpected color pairings, the way materials meet at a seam. raw material for later work, and maybe interesting to share in its own right
- try designing something under an arbitrary constraint, like only straight lines, or only shapes found in a hardware aisle, or only the colors of a rusty gate
- explain a finished project as if talking to a child. if the explanation falls apart, the concept was weaker than it looked
- make something purely for fun with no brief, to remember what the work felt like before it was work
- describe a brand as a sound, a smell, or a texture instead of with adjectives. might be a better way to agree on tone before anything is drawn
- study how old craft trades documented their work: ledgers, pattern books, specimen sheets. there may be a format worth borrowing for how projects get recorded
- think about what a client leaves with when a project wraps up. not just files, but a way of seeing their own thing. maybe a plain-language guide to using the identity with confidence
- look at where good work gets lost between the studio and the real world: bad printing, cropped logos, stretched images. could be valuable to make hand-off materials that anticipate those failures
- experiment with slowness. what if a project had a deliberately quiet phase where nothing gets made, only looked at and talked about
- keep a list of questions that never get asked in briefs but probably should. over time it might turn into a better intake conversation
- notice which projects leave energy behind and which drain it, and look for the pattern. the pattern might say more about what the studio wants to become than any plan would

**loose thoughts**

Good design is mostly a series of small kindnesses to a stranger who will never know they were helped: the line break in the right place, the label that is readable in poor light, the button that is exactly where a thumb expects it. That's a decent test for anything: who is this being kind to, and do they get to feel it?

Also worth remembering that the work doesn't have to be loud to be memorable. Some of the best things are quiet and precise and slightly surprising, the kind of thing a person notices only after living with it for a while.
