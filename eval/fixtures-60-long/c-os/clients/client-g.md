# Client G

Some general working notes, mostly to myself. A lot of the friction in a solo studio comes from the gaps around the work, not the work.

**approach**

- Start from the problem the person has, not the deliverable they asked for. People ask for a logo or a layout or a site because that's the vocabulary they have. Underneath, it's usually trust, clarity, or wanting to be taken seriously. If I can name that early, the rest gets easier to defend.
- Make the thinking visible. A rough diagram, a messy list of constraints, or a page of questions often does more to build confidence than a polished mockup. People relax when they can see how I got somewhere.
- Be wary of the urge to impress. Work that tries too hard to show range tends to confuse the person paying for it. Restraint reads as confidence.
- Treat the brief as a living thing. The first version is almost never right, and that's fine as long as the gaps get noticed early and not discovered at the end.

**process**

Most projects seem to move through the same loose rhythm, even when they feel chaotic from inside:

- Listen far more than I talk. Ask what they're worried about, what they've tried, what they've seen that they hated. Dislikes are often more precise than likes.
- Collect references without judging them. Pin up anything that sparks a reaction, then look for what the reactions have in common.
- Sketch badly and quickly before going near a screen. Screens make everything look more finished than it is, which makes it harder to throw things away.
- Show work in a way that invites a specific kind of feedback. "What do you think?" gets vague answers. "Does this feel too loud for your audience?" gets useful ones.
- Leave room at the end for polish. The last stretch of detail work is where something decent becomes something good, and it's the first thing to get squeezed when everything else runs long.

**things to keep in mind**

- A no is information, not a verdict. It can be about timing, budget pressure, internal politics, a friend of a friend, or a gut feeling that has nothing to do with the work. Resist the temptation to read it as a review of talent.
- Rejection stings more when the studio is a single person, because there's nobody to share the weight with. Worth having some ritual for letting it go: a walk, a clean desk, a note on what I'd do differently if anything.
- Be careful about the stories I tell myself afterward. It's easy to decide I was too expensive or too slow or too opinionated, and just as easy to decide the other side didn't understand design. Both are probably too tidy.
- Pay attention to where conversations lost energy. Often there's a moment where the tone shifts, a question that got a short answer, a silence after a proposal. Those moments are worth remembering.
- Keep a record of what was actually said, in the person's own words. Paraphrasing too early flattens the details that matter later.
- Notice what kind of people stay enthusiastic through the awkward middle of a project. They tend to be the ones who understand that design is a conversation. Those relationships compound.

**on working with other people's expectations**

People rarely know what they want until they see something they don't want. That's not a flaw in them. It's how taste works. So the job is partly to help them discover their own preferences without making them feel led.

It helps to be honest about tradeoffs in plain language. Faster, cheaper, better: the old joke holds, and people respect hearing it said kindly. Vague promises tend to turn into resentment. A clear explanation of what a choice gives up usually earns more goodwill than an eager yes.

Boundaries are part of the craft too. Scope creep rarely arrives as a dramatic request. It arrives as a small favor, then another, then a feeling that the work has no edges. Naming the edges early, and gently, protects the relationship more than it threatens it.

**keeping the studio healthy**

- A solo practice depends on a steady trickle of conversations, not a few big ones. Staying visible, in small and unglamorous ways, is probably more important than any single pitch.
- Share process, not just results. Short write-ups about how a problem got solved bring in the kind of people who value thinking, and they tend to be better to work with.
- Keep a loose library of past work that's easy to browse and honest about what role I played. People can tell when a portfolio is stretching.
- Protect time for unpaid curiosity. Personal projects, experiments, odd typographic studies. They're where new habits and new voices come from, and they keep the paid work from going stale.
- Rest is not a reward for finishing. Tired work shows, usually in the details nobody can quite point at.
- Admin deserves respect. Contracts, handoff notes, tidy files, a clear way of saying what's included. Boring systems make creative work feel lighter.

**open-ended ideas**

- A short, friendly questionnaire that gets at values and fears before any visual conversation begins. Maybe the answers could become a quiet reference throughout a project.
- A way of describing the studio that doesn't lean on adjectives like "bold" or "clean." Something closer to how it feels to work together.
- A habit of writing a brief reflection after every project, regardless of outcome. What surprised me, what I'd skip, what I'd lean into. Over time it could reveal patterns I can't see from inside any one job.
- Exploring whether smaller, well-defined offerings could make it easier for people to say yes. A narrow starting point can lower the stakes for someone who's nervous about committing to a full engagement.
- Thinking about how to talk about value without sounding defensive. What does the work make possible for the person, and how can that be shown with a story instead of an argument?
- Finding other solo practitioners to trade honest feedback with. Isolation makes it easy to lose perspective, and a trusted outside eye can catch things a client never will.
- Looking at the way other fields explain their work. Architects, editors, chefs, and carpenters all have language for craft that might translate.
- Wondering what it would look like to design the process itself as carefully as the output: the emails, the first call, the way feedback is gathered, the way a project closes. Those touchpoints are part of the product, even if they never appear in a portfolio.

**a loose reminder**

Good work and good business don't always line up neatly. Sometimes the best project of the year goes nowhere, and a mediocre one pays for everything. The only dependable thing is to keep the craft sharp, keep the relationships honest, and keep noticing what feels alive. The rest is weather.
