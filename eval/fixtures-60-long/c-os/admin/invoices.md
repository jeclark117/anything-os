general working notes

approach

- begin with the problem behind the request, not the request itself. people ask for a logo when what they mean is "i want to be taken seriously." people ask for a refresh when the business has quietly changed and the visuals haven't caught up.
- ask the boring questions early. who is this for, what do they already believe, what would make them walk away. boring questions save the most rework.
- keep the brief short enough to hold in my head. if i can't say what the work is meant to do in a breath, the brief isn't finished.
- treat constraints as material. a tight palette, an awkward format, a stubborn brand history: these shape the work more than any mood board does.
- default to less. most work improves when something is removed and rarely when something is added.
- good design is mostly good listening with a visual output. the drawing part is almost the easy part.

process

this is a loose flow, not a ladder. things loop back and that's fine.

- gather: collect whatever already exists. old files, screenshots, things the client loves, things they can't stand. the hated things are more informative than the loved ones.
- wander: sketch badly and fast on paper before touching a screen. the goal is volume and ugliness. the screen makes everything look finished too early, and finished-looking things are hard to throw away.
- narrow: pick directions that feel genuinely different from each other, not variations on a theme. if the directions look like cousins, the client ends up choosing between shades instead of ideas.
- test in context: put the work where it will actually live. a mark on a blank page lies. a mark on a cluttered small screen, a rough packaging mockup, or a tiny browser tab tells the truth.
- refine: spacing, rhythm, alignment, the dull details people feel without noticing. this is where the work earns its keep.
- hand over: tidy files, clear naming, and a short guide for using the thing without breaking it.
- afterward, leave a little room to reflect on what the project taught me, while the memory is still warm.

things to keep in mind

- name files as if a stranger will inherit them. future me is a stranger.
- never overwrite the version that worked. keep a trail.
- keep a running log of why choices were made. the reasoning disappears faster than the files do.
- feedback is data about a reaction, not an instruction. "make it pop" usually means something feels flat. find out what.
- a quiet client is not necessarily a happy client. silence can mean confusion, politeness, or distraction.
- say the awkward thing early. scope creep almost always starts as a friendly aside.
- rest the work. looking again after a break catches problems that staring never will.
- small regular care for the admin side beats rare heroic catch-up. a pile of loose ends turns into fog quickly.
- the client's audience is not the client. design for the person who will meet the work cold, with no context and no patience.
- legibility and contrast before personality. a clever thing nobody can read is just a puzzle.
- white space is a decision. so is crowding.
- be wary of falling in love with a direction. attach to the problem instead, because the problem doesn't change its mind about what it needs.

working with clients

- some people want to be led, some want to be consulted, some want to be left to get on with it. read which, then adjust.
- show the rationale along with the work. a design with a reason survives taste arguments better than a design with a flourish.
- present a narrow set of options with real conviction rather than a buffet. a buffet hands the hard thinking back to the client.
- separate "i don't like it" from "it doesn't work." the former is taste, the latter is a problem worth solving.
- when a client is slow to respond, assume busy before assuming unhappy.
- recurring arrangements want a different rhythm than project work: steadier, less theatrical, more about maintenance and small improvements than big reveals.
- keep relationships warm between projects. a quick note sharing something relevant costs nothing and keeps the door open.
- be clear about what a request touches. a small tweak that quietly reshapes the whole system isn't small, and it's kinder to say so before the work starts.
- explain choices in the client's language, not design jargon. "this helps people find the button" lands better than a lecture about hierarchy.

working solo

- nobody is around to catch mistakes, so build in checks: read the text aloud, flip the artwork upside down, view it tiny, view it in grayscale, view it with squinting eyes.
- no colleagues means taste can drift without anybody noticing. look at work outside the field on purpose: architecture, textiles, old signage, shop packaging, hand-painted lettering.
- guard energy. creative output isn't steady. some stretches are fast and fertile, others are for sorting and sharpening. a quiet stretch isn't a failure.
- keep a drawer of unfinished experiments. it works like a savings account for ideas.
- be kind to the version of me who has to explain the value of the work to a skeptic. keep notes that make that easier.
- tools are replaceable. habits aren't. invest in habits.
- being small is an advantage: fast turns, direct conversations, no layers. protect that.
- being small is also a risk: everything depends on a single person's health and mood. build gentle buffers into how the studio runs.

open-ended ideas

- a small library of reusable discovery questions, sorted by what they uncover rather than by project type.
- a reflection habit at the close of each project: what surprised me, what i'd repeat, what i'd skip.
- typographic studies purely for fun, no client, just to stretch the eye and the hand.
- a standard handover note so every project ends gracefully and the client always knows how to look after what they've been given.
- a collection of "bad brief" patterns, with notes on how each tends to be defused.
- constraint exercises: work only in black and white until the structure is solid, or limit a layout to a restricted typeface family and see what breaks.
- a quiet visual timeline to show clients how the thinking evolved, so the final result feels earned rather than conjured.
- thinking through what a good offboarding looks like: what a client needs in order to keep the work healthy without me nearby.
- short essays on design decisions. writing might sharpen my own thinking and attract the right sort of enquiry.
- play with texture: scanned paper, ink bleed, torn edges, used as imperfect counterpoints to clean layouts.
- how could sound, motion, and print echo each other in a brand so it feels coherent beyond the static page?
- a stuck-moment prompt: what would i make if nobody were going to judge it?
- a shelf of physical samples, such as paper stocks, foils, and fabric swatches, to touch when the screen starts to feel flat.
- a habit of photographing good and bad lettering in the wild, then sorting the pile now and then to see what patterns emerge.

small reminders

- the best idea often arrives while doing something unrelated: walking, washing up, staring out of a window. leave room for that.
- sameness is a risk, but novelty for its own sake is a bigger one.
- if a design needs a long explanation, it probably needs another pass.
- be generous with credit and honest about limits.
- taste is trained by looking, and looking is free.
- the point is not to impress other designers. the point is that the work does its job and the person on the other side feels something good.
