# Routine

general working notes

**approach**

the work is mostly listening before it is drawing. projects tend to wobble early, not late, and the wobble usually traces back to a brief that was really a polite guess about what somebody wanted. so the instinct is to slow down at the start and ask the slightly awkward questions. what is this actually for? who gets annoyed if it goes wrong? what would make it feel like a waste of effort?

being a studio of just me is a strength and a trap. the strength is that there are no handoffs and no telephone game, so the thinking stays intact from the earliest scribble to the final file. the trap is that nobody walks past and says "that's overcooked." so it helps to build stand-ins for another opinion: print the thing, flip it upside down, view it in a mirror, squint at it, or show it to a patient friend who doesn't work in design and will say what they actually see.

fewer, stronger directions beat a spread of safe ones. a wide menu of options feels generous, but mostly it hands the decision back to the client. having a point of view is part of what people are paying for, even when they don't say so.

also worth remembering that taste is not the same as fit. something I love might be wrong for the job, and something plain might be exactly right. the job is to serve the problem, not to decorate my own portfolio of preferences.

**process, roughly**

- begin on paper. rough, ugly, fast. the screen makes everything look finished too early, and that kills exploration.
- collect references, but sort them by what they do rather than what they look like. a reference for "calm" is more useful than a reference for "a nice serif."
- write the problem in plain sentences before opening any software. if the sentences are mushy, the layout will be too.
- work in rounds of widening and narrowing. when widening, make lots of bad ideas and refuse to judge them. when narrowing, keep what has a spine and drop the rest without sentiment.
- test at the extremes: tiny, enormous, black and white, with the worst possible content dropped in. a layout that only works with perfect placeholder text isn't a layout yet.
- name and tidy files as I go, because future me is a stranger who is tired and in a hurry.
- let work sit before judging it. fresh eyes catch more than extra effort ever does.
- jot down why a choice was made, in a line or two. rationale evaporates quickly, and people always ask for it later.
- when stuck, change the medium rather than pushing harder. cut paper, a marker, a different canvas size, a different grid. the block is often just the habit of the current tool.
- show work in context whenever possible. a logo floating on white means little; the same logo on a cluttered shelf, a phone screen, or a rainy window sticker tells the truth.

**things to keep in mind**

- clients rarely dislike a design as much as they dislike surprise. narrate the thinking as it develops so nothing lands cold.
- feedback is data about a reaction, not an instruction. "make it bigger" often means "I don't feel confident about this." dig for the feeling underneath the note.
- scope creep arrives politely, dressed as a small favor. notice it early, name it kindly, keep the boundary visible without making it awkward.
- constraints are friends. a tight palette, a limited set of type styles, a rigid grid: they make decisions on my behalf and free up energy for the interesting parts.
- accessibility isn't a layer applied at the end. contrast, legibility, and clarity are part of the craft from the beginning, and they usually make the work better for everybody.
- rest is a working tool. the good ideas tend to show up while washing dishes or walking, not while staring harder at the screen.
- be wary of comparing my behind-the-scenes mess with other people's polished showcases. they have a mess too; it just isn't on display.
- be wary of work that would require pretending to be something I'm not. it usually shows in the result.
- isolation creeps in quietly when working alone. loose contact with other makers matters more than it seems to, even if it's only swapping a sketch and a grumble.
- not every problem is a design problem. sometimes the real issue is a muddled offering, an unclear audience, or a disagreement inside the client's own team, and no amount of typography will fix that. better to say so gently than to polish around it.
- good defaults are underrated. a sensible type scale, sensible spacing, a sensible way of naming layers. boring infrastructure buys freedom for the parts that deserve attention.
- keep a bias toward finishing. an idea that ships imperfectly teaches more than an idea that stays perfect in a drawer.

**open-ended ideas**

- a small library of reusable questions for kickoff conversations, grown from whatever turned out to matter on past projects and trimmed whenever one turns out to be dead weight.
- a post-mortem habit: after a project wraps, a short paragraph on what surprised me. not a report, just a note to leave for later.
- designing under deliberately odd limits. just a typeface and a rule. only circles. only what could be made with a photocopier. see what the restriction produces that comfort wouldn't.
- redoing an old piece of work from scratch with what I know now, then comparing the two without any intention of publishing either.
- a swipe file of things that are well made but aren't design in the usual sense: packaging, signage, handwritten shop notices, instructions printed on old appliances, the labels on tins.
- imagining how a piece would be described over the phone. if it can't be explained out loud, it may not be clear enough on the page.
- writing or teaching as a way to sharpen thinking. explaining a process out loud exposes the gaps faster than anything else.
- a folder for dead ends that deserve another life. half of them were just ahead of their moment, or in the wrong project.
- occasional collaboration experiments with a writer, illustrator, or developer on a self-started piece, mainly to feel other rhythms and borrow other habits.
- thinking of a brand as a voice. how would it talk to a stranger, to a friend, to somebody who is angry? useful for tone, and surprisingly useful for layout.
- presenting with less slideshow and more conversation: a prototype to hold, a printed object to touch, a rough model to push around the table.
- a personal glossary of words clients use that mean something different from what I assume. "clean," "modern," "pop," "friendly," "premium." each one hides a whole set of expectations.
- paying more attention to endings: the last screen of a flow, the back of a package, the sign-off of a letter. those quiet places are often where care is most visible.

**questions to chew on**

what does "finished" actually mean for a project? how much polish is enough before it becomes fussing? when does a favorite template turn into a rut? how do I tell the difference between a style and a habit? and what would the work look like if I trusted my first instinct more, or trusted it less?

none of this is a rulebook. it's more a pile of reminders and curiosities that I keep coming back to, and the pile is allowed to contradict itself.

# working notes

## approach

start with the problem, not the look. a brief usually shows up as a request for a thing, a mark or a site or a deck, when what somebody actually wants is a change in how people feel about something, or a way to make a muddled situation less muddled. worth asking what the thing is supposed to do before any pixels get pushed around.

the upside of being small is no handoffs and no telephone game. the person who hears the problem is the person who draws the answer. that's the reason the work can be sharper than what a bigger shop produces. it's also the reason it can drift sideways with nobody around to catch it, so build in ways to catch it yourself.

prefer fewer, stronger ideas over a wide spread. showing a pile of directions mostly shows indecision. a clear recommendation with the reasoning attached usually gets a better conversation than a menu does.

## process

- ask plenty of questions at the start and write down the exact words people use. their vocabulary is already a big part of the design language.
- gather references widely, then throw most of them away. keep only what answers a real question.
- rough before refined. paper, bad sketches, ugly gray boxes. if the structure doesn't work in boxes, color won't rescue it.
- show work early, while it's cheap to change. polished work invites polite approval instead of honest reaction.
- write down the why behind every meaningful choice, in plain language, right when it's made. future me never remembers.
- keep a running file of leftovers: rejected ideas, odd shapes, color combos that almost worked. they often find a home on a later project.
- let work sit before judging it. fresh eyes catch more than extra effort does.
- test things at the size and in the setting where they'll actually live. a mark that sings on a big screen can turn to mush on a tiny label or a tired phone screen.
- check everything in grayscale, then squinted, then upside down. cheap tricks, still the quickest way to see what's really going on with hierarchy and balance.

## things to keep in mind

**scope creeps quietly.** it rarely arrives as a big ask. it arrives as "while you're in there" and "can it also". notice the pattern early, name it kindly, and keep a clear line between refining what exists and adding what's new.

**clients are not the audience.** their taste matters, but they're a proxy for the people the work is meant to reach. when feedback is purely personal preference, translate it back into a question about the audience and see if it still holds.

**vague feedback is a gift if you dig.** "it feels off" usually points at something real: spacing, rhythm, a mismatch between the words and the visuals. ask what they expected to see, not what they want changed.

**consistency beats cleverness.** a clever idea applied unevenly looks worse than a modest idea applied with care everywhere. systems, not moments.

**type does most of the work.** when a layout feels wrong, the culprit is more often the type than the imagery. look at size relationships, line length, and how much room the text has to breathe before reaching for anything decorative.

**restraint is a skill, not a lack of effort.** empty space is doing a job. so is a limited palette. resist the urge to prove effort by adding things.

**accessibility isn't a layer on top.** contrast, legibility, motion, plain language. build it in from the start because retrofitting is clumsy and usually shows.

**the boring parts matter.** file naming, clean layers, tidy exports, clear handover notes. a beautiful design that's miserable to use or maintain is only half a design. well, mostly a sketch.

**energy is a material.** work made while frazzled has a different quality, usually a safer and flatter one. notice when the well is low and be honest about what kind of work suits that state: tidy-up, admin, organizing references, rather than big concept leaps.

**say what you don't know.** it builds more trust than bluffing, and it opens room to bring in somebody better suited for the gap.

## working with people

- be plain about how the work will go and what's needed from them. surprises feel worse than slow answers.
- put decisions in writing in a short recap so nobody has to remember a conversation differently.
- explain choices in terms of their goals, not design jargon. nobody wants a lecture on kerning.
- when something goes wrong, say so early, with a proposed way forward. the awkwardness shrinks when it's raised sooner.
- good questions to keep handy: what would make this a success for you? what would make it a disappointment? who's going to be quietly unhappy if it goes a certain way?
- thank people for clear, generous feedback. it encourages more of it.

## open-ended ideas

- a small library of reusable starting points: grid setups, type pairings, spacing scales, document skeletons. not templates to fill in, just a head start that keeps early hours for thinking rather than setup.
- a habit of making something purely for curiosity between projects. a poster, a typeface experiment, a weird little interface. no audience, no brief. it keeps the hand loose and feeds the leftovers file.
- a reflective write-up after finishing a project: what worked, what dragged, what was a surprise. kept short and honest, and not shared unless useful.
- a studio reading list built from things that changed how work gets approached, including stuff far outside design: architecture, cooking, mapmaking, bookbinding, sound.
- trying to explain a project out loud to a stranger, or to a houseplant, before presenting it. if the story wobbles, the design probably does too.
- experimenting with physical process more: printing, cutting, folding, tracing. the screen makes everything feel infinitely adjustable, and sometimes that's the problem.
- a way to document the studio's own way of working so that, if help is ever brought in, the thinking is easy to hand over and not just the files.
- collecting good examples of bad design, with a note on exactly why they fail. a sharp eye is partly built from knowing what to avoid.
- exploring how to price and describe work around outcomes rather than deliverables, without making it feel slippery.
- more attention to sound and motion in otherwise static work. small transitions can carry a lot of personality.

## loose thoughts

the best projects tend to have a clear person on the other side who cares and can decide. the hard ones usually have a committee and no owner. notice that early, because it shapes everything after.

stay curious about the unglamorous. forms, error messages, empty states, receipts, signage. that's where people actually spend their time, and where thoughtful design is most noticed by its absence.

keep the portfolio honest. show the mess and the reasoning, not just the glossy result. the thinking is the product.

rest isn't separate from the work. a lot of the real problem-solving happens while walking, washing dishes, staring out a window. leave gaps for that.

when stuck, change the medium, the scale, or the question. almost always one of those unsticks it.

maybe worth remembering that nobody is waiting for perfect. a clear, kind, well-made thing that exists beats a flawless thing that doesn't.
