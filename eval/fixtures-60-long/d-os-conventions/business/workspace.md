# Workspace

working notes

**how i come at things**

i try to start from the problem rather than the look. most of the early effort goes into working out what a piece of work is supposed to do for the people who end up meeting it, and the visual side mostly falls out of that if the groundwork is solid. when i skip straight to styling i end up redoing it anyway once the real question shows up.

- ask what the work is for before asking what it should look like
- find out who will actually be looking at it, and in what situation
- put the idea into plain words before drawing anything
- keep early rounds rough on purpose so nobody (me included) gets attached to polish too soon
- notice what the brief leaves out. the gaps are usually where the interesting choices hide

working solo means wearing every hat. that's a strength and a trap. the strength is that nothing gets lost in a handoff, so the thinking stays coherent from brief through delivery. the trap is that there's no built-in pushback, so i have to invent ways of disagreeing with myself. a habit that helps: write down the strongest objection a skeptical stranger would raise, then see whether the work answers it.

**process, loosely**

not a rigid pipeline, more a rhythm that loops back on itself.

- soaking it in. read whatever the client sends, then read around it. how does their audience talk, what do they grumble about, what do they praise without being asked
- gathering references without copying. collect widely, then ask what each reference is doing rather than what it looks like
- sketching badly and fast. paper still wins for speed. ugly thumbnails are cheap and show what the structure wants to be
- narrowing to directions that are genuinely different from each other, not variations of the same idea in new colors
- testing in context. a mark or a layout in isolation lies. put it on the real thing, in the real setting, at the size people will meet it
- stepping away until the work looks unfamiliar, then coming back with a colder eye
- tidying files and naming things properly while the logic is still fresh, because future me is a stranger who forgets everything

there's a recurring pattern where the work feels worst right in the middle. it's usually a sign that the rough idea has run out of road and the real craft is about to start, not a sign that it's failing. worth remembering when the urge to bail on a direction shows up.

**working with clients**

- clients usually describe solutions when what they really have is a problem. "make the logo bigger" is a symptom. ask what's happening that makes bigger seem like the cure
- show the reasoning along with the work. people accept an unfamiliar choice far more readily when they can see the path that led there
- present fewer options with stronger conviction. a wide menu invites taste-testing instead of thinking
- vague feedback ("it needs more pop") is still information. translate it back into a question about feel or priority
- keep a record of why a direction was picked. it saves arguments later when memories drift
- be upfront early about what's flexible and what isn't. surprises are expensive for everybody

**things to keep in mind**

- restraint is a skill. removing the part that's almost working often improves the whole
- hierarchy first, decoration after. if the eye doesn't know where to land, no amount of finish rescues it
- spacing does more work than it gets credit for. when a layout feels off, check the gaps before touching anything else
- type carries personality faster than color does. treat typeface choice as a major move, not a finishing touch
- accessibility isn't an add-on. contrast, legibility, and clear structure are part of whether the work functions at all
- constraints are friends. a tight palette, a limited set of shapes, a rule about what's allowed: they speed up work and give it identity
- the thing that's hard to explain is often the thing worth keeping. don't sand off every odd edge
- good work is quiet about its effort. the audience should feel clarity, not labor
- protect the boring parts: backups, tidy naming, version history. losing a stretch of work to a careless mistake hurts more than any creative setback
- rest is part of the job. tired judgment produces tidy, safe, forgettable results

**revising and finishing**

finishing is its own skill, not just more of the middle. the middle is about exploring; the end is about care. that means looking at edges, alignments, awkward line breaks in text, how things behave when copy runs longer or shorter than expected, what happens when an image fails to load, and what a stranger sees when they land on it with no context.

- read everything out loud. awkward phrasing shows up in the mouth before it shows up to the eye
- look at the work squinted, upside down, or very small to check the structure without the details
- check the unglamorous states: empty, error, loading, too much text, too little
- ask what would be embarrassing if a sharp-eyed person pointed it out, and fix that
- know when to stop. there's a point where more tweaking makes the work worse, and it's usually earlier than it feels

**on inspiration**

inspiration is mostly a byproduct of paying attention. the useful sources are rarely other design work. signage in odd places, packaging from far away, old manuals, handwriting, the way worn objects show how they were used. a mind that has been feeding on varied material has more to combine. when stuck, change the input rather than forcing the output: go for a walk, read something unrelated, rearrange the room, make the thing in a different medium than usual.

**staying sane as a solo studio**

- keep some kind of boundary between work space and the rest of life, even if it's only a habit rather than a room
- batch the admin so it doesn't leak into thinking space
- say no to work that clearly doesn't fit. it clogs the pipeline for work that does
- keep a running list of lessons from each project, short and candid, and reread it before starting something new
- find outside eyes: other designers, friendly critics, people outside the field. fresh readers catch what familiarity hides
- avoid comparing the studio to bigger outfits. different shape, different advantages: speed, directness, a personal touch

**open ideas, unsorted**

- build a small library of reusable thinking prompts, not templates: questions to ask at the start of any project, questions to ask when stuck
- try writing the case study before the work is finished, as a way to see whether the story holds together
- make something purely for the pleasure of it, with no brief, and see what habits show up when nobody is asking
- a personal archive of textures, sounds, and scraps from the physical world that can seed future work
- explore teaching or writing about the process. explaining a method is a good test of whether it's really understood
- look at how other crafts handle critique (cooking, carpentry, music) and borrow rituals
- develop a better way to show work-in-progress so clients feel included without feeling they must judge unfinished things
- a habit of post-project reflection: what surprised me, what stayed hard, what i'd quietly change
- sketch a "studio voice" document describing how the studio sounds in emails, proposals, and invoices, so the voice feels deliberate
- think about collaborating with specialists: illustrators, writers, developers, photographers. the studio stays small but the reach doesn't have to
- try a deliberately odd constraint, like only shapes with no curves, just to see what breaks open
- keep a folder of rejected ideas. they often come back in a better form

**closing thought**

the work that holds up usually comes from clear thinking, patient iteration, and a willingness to leave things out. the habits around the work matter more than anything else, so it's worth looking after them.

- Laptop bought 2025, plus a second monitor.
- Work from home, in the spare room.
- Finish the motion design course.
- Reading list: a book on pricing creative work, a book on typography.

Most of the useful thinking happens before anything gets drawn. I try to spend the early stretch just collecting: what the thing is for, who will meet it, and what it should feel like when someone encounters it cold. A loose pile of words and scraps beats a tidy brief at this point, because a tidy brief tends to lock in assumptions nobody has tested yet.

**Approach**

- Begin with the problem as it was described, then ask what sits underneath it. The stated request is often a symptom of something quieter.
- Look at what already exists before adding to it. Old material usually holds clues about what people actually respond to.
- Keep the reasoning visible. If I can't explain a choice in plain words, it's probably decoration.
- Make rough things early and make them ugly on purpose. Polish is a good way of hiding weak ideas from yourself.
- Prefer a narrow set of moves done well over a wide set done halfway.
- Let constraints do some of the work. A limited palette, a limited type system, a limited grid. They make decisions faster and make the result feel intentional rather than accumulated.

**Process**

Sketching comes before screens. Paper is slow in a useful way: it stops me from fiddling with details that don't matter yet. I fill pages with bad thumbnails, circle whatever has a pulse, and drop the rest without sentiment. Only when the structure feels sturdy enough to survive being rendered cleanly do I move to anything digital.

From there it becomes a loop of showing, listening, and adjusting. Work shared while it's still slightly unfinished gets better feedback, because people feel allowed to change it. The questions matter as much as the work. "Do you like it" gets polite noise. "What does this make you expect" gets something usable. Reactions are worth writing down word for word, since paraphrasing smooths away the interesting parts.

Details get their own pass, separate from the big shapes. Type, spacing, alignment, contrast, the way edges meet. Check things at the size they'll actually appear, on a worse screen than mine, in bad light, and on paper if paper is where they'll live. A squint test and a grayscale test catch more problems than any amount of staring at full color.

Handoff deserves real care. Files should be organized so a stranger could open them and understand the logic. Layers get names written by a considerate person. A short note of intentions travels with the work, explaining the why and not only the what, since the why is what disappears first.

**Things to keep in mind**

- Scope creep in a small studio rarely announces itself. It arrives as favors, small additions, and "while you're in there" requests. Naming what is not included, as clearly as what is, keeps everyone honest.
- The loudest voice in the feedback pile is not necessarily the most representative. Weigh comments by how closely the person matches the audience.
- Accessibility is a starting condition, not a finishing pass. Retrofitting it is always clumsier than building with it.
- Keep a record of rejected directions. They often come back later as the right answer for a different problem.
- Fresh eyes catch more than extra effort. Stepping away is part of the work, not a break from it.
- Busy is not the same as progressing. Some of the best days produce very little visible output and a lot of clarity.
- If something isn't working, say so early, while changing it is still cheap and nobody is attached to it.
- Administrative chores deserve a small standing place in the routine, or they quietly take over the whole week of attention.
- Taste is useful but it's not evidence. When possible, back opinions with something observed.
- Be wary of trends. They feel like relevance but age like milk.

**Working alone**

Without colleagues to bounce things off, I need substitutes. Talking out loud helps. So does writing the pitch as if addressing a skeptic, reading a layout from the bottom up, or printing something just to see it differently. Explaining a problem to an imaginary newcomer often reveals the answer halfway through the sentence.

Boundaries between working and not working don't exist by default, so they have to be built from small rituals: clearing the desk surface, closing every open window, changing the lighting, putting the headphones down. A visible end to a session matters more than it sounds. Without it, the work leaks into everything else and nothing gets proper rest.

Physical reference material is worth keeping close: swatches, printed samples, type specimens, objects with a good texture or an interesting weight. Screens flatten everything, and a quick touch of something real recalibrates judgment. The surface in front of me should hold only what belongs to the current problem. Clutter is a form of unresolved decisions.

**Open-ended ideas**

- Keep a running collection of everyday annoyances: confusing signage, awkward forms, packaging that fights back. Patterns in the irritation point to opportunities.
- Build a small library of starting points, such as grids, type pairings, and color relationships, without letting them harden into crutches. Review and prune them regularly.
- Try designing for the ear or the hand instead of the eye. What would this feel like with the screen off? What would it sound like read aloud?
- Write a product's voice before designing its look. Decide what it would never say, then let the visuals inherit that restraint.
- Work for a stretch with deliberately worse tools and see which ideas survive. The ones that do are probably structural rather than cosmetic.
- Keep a process diary of short entries about what surprised me. Surprise is a decent proxy for learning.
- Test how an identity behaves when shrunk to almost nothing and when blown up enormous. Most systems are only good at a narrow range of scales.
- Hand finished work to a stranger with no context and ask them to describe it back. The gap between their description and the intention is the real brief for the next round.
- Borrow thinking from crafts far from design, like sign painting, bookbinding, or cooking. They have mature ideas about finish, patience, and what counts as careful.
- Imagine a case study that opens with the failures and works toward the result. What would that honesty change about how work gets presented?
- Check whether a system can be explained over the phone. If it can't, it may be too clever.
- Photograph surfaces and textures without any project in mind, then see what they suggest months later.
- Explore what a studio's own materials would look like if they were treated as a client project: the invoices, the emails, the way a first message lands.
- Consider what it means for design to be finished at all. Some things want to stay a little open, so they can be adapted by whoever inherits them.

**A loose closing thought**

None of this is a method so much as a posture: curious early, skeptical in the middle, generous at the end. The aim is for the work to feel considered without feeling fussy, and for the process behind it to stay light enough that it can bend when a project asks for something unexpected. These notes are meant to be added to, argued with, and occasionally ignored.

general working notes

**approach**

- start with the problem in plain language. who's looking, what they need to understand, what should happen afterward. if i can't say it simply, i'm not ready to open the design file.
- make rough things early. ugly sketches, boxes and arrows, scribbles on paper. rough work is cheap to throw away, and that's the whole point.
- let constraints do some of the thinking. a tight palette, a limited type pairing, a strict grid. a blank canvas with no rules is harder than it looks.
- keep the reasoning visible. when i show work, the why matters as much as the what. people who understand the reasoning push back on the right things.
- protect the quiet stretches. the best work happens when nothing is pinging me, so uninterrupted focus is part of the toolkit, not a luxury.

**process**

my process is loose phases that blur together. early on it's mostly listening and collecting: how people talk about their work, what they're proud of, what they keep apologizing for. the apologies are usually where the real brief lives. i want to write those down word for word, because paraphrasing flattens them.

then comes the messy middle, where i explore wide. lots of directions, most of them wrong, and that's fine. the trick is to resist falling in love with a direction before it has been tested against the actual problem. a good check is to squint at the work, or shrink it, or strip the color out. if the idea survives, it's real. if it only works at full size with all the polish on, it's decoration.

the late phase is narrowing, refining, and handing things over cleanly. this is where sloppy file names, unlabeled layers, and undocumented choices come back to bite. tidy as i go, because future me is a stranger with no memory of why anything is the way it is.

**movement**

things worth remembering about motion in general:

- movement should explain something. if it doesn't clarify a relationship, show a change, or guide attention, it's probably noise.
- timing carries personality. snappy feels confident, slow and soft feels calm, and anything that lingers too long feels like it's wasting the viewer's patience.
- easing is where most of the feeling lives. linear movement looks mechanical and a little lifeless. real objects speed up and settle, and viewers feel it even if they can't name it.
- hierarchy applies to movement just like it does to type. if everything is moving, nothing is important. decide what leads and let the rest follow or stay still.
- stillness is a tool. a held moment before a change makes the change land harder.
- some people get sick or distracted by motion, so every piece needs a calmer fallback. design that version on purpose rather than as an afterthought.
- sound changes how movement reads. even imagining the audio while sketching helps me judge rhythm.

watching things move in the real world is underrated training. how a door swings, how a leaf drops, how a cat decides to jump. weight, anticipation, follow-through, and overshoot are everywhere once i start paying attention.

**working with people**

- ask what success looks like for them before showing anything. their answer is usually different from what the brief implies.
- feedback is data about a reaction, not a set of instructions. when someone says "make it pop," the useful question is what felt flat to them.
- show fewer options with stronger reasoning rather than a wall of variations. a wide spread invites taste arguments instead of problem solving.
- be honest early when something isn't working. awkward conversations get more expensive the longer they wait.
- keep a clear boundary between what i'm confident about and what i'm guessing at, and say which is which.
- write things down after conversations. memory is generous to whoever is remembering.

**small reminders**

- good enough to ship and good enough to be proud of are different bars, and it's worth knowing which one a given project deserves.
- don't mistake busy for productive. a long list of small tasks can hide the fact that the important thing is being avoided.
- copying a technique is learning, copying a result is theft. study how something was made, then make something of my own with it.
- rest is part of the work. ideas tend to show up on walks, in the shower, or while doing dishes, not while staring at the screen.
- when stuck, change the medium. pencil, cut paper, a different app, a voice memo. a new tool shakes loose a new angle.
- taste grows by looking at a lot of work and asking why it works or doesn't, not just whether i like it.
- the portfolio is a story about how i think, not a trophy case. show the problem, the mess, and the reasoning along with the polished end.

**open-ended ideas**

- a running collection of tiny studies: a button that responds like a physical object, a loader with personality, a transition that tells a little story. no client, no brief, just practice that might turn into reusable ideas.
- take apart interfaces i admire and write down what makes them feel good. then try to reproduce the feeling with entirely different visuals.
- a swipe file organized by feeling instead of by medium. "playful but trustworthy," "quiet and expensive-looking," "loud on purpose."
- write short process notes after finishing projects, even rough ones. a public version could double as marketing without feeling like marketing.
- build a kickoff questionnaire that surfaces the apologies and the unspoken worries early.
- explore type that moves. letterforms that breathe, stretch, or assemble themselves could be a nice signature direction.
- pair with a sound person or musician on an experiment, just to see how audio and movement pull each other around.
- keep an "unfinished experiments" folder and revisit it now and then. some abandoned ideas only needed time, or a different problem to attach to.
- try designing an entire piece with a deliberately absurd constraint, like a single typeface and a single color, to see what that forces.
- find a way to get honest outside critique without it feeling like a performance. maybe a small circle of peers who trade work in progress.
- think about what a studio of this size does better than a big agency, and lean into it: speed, directness, a person who actually cares about the details, nobody lost in a handoff chain.

none of this is a plan, just things to circle back to when the work needs fresh air.

working notes

approach

the work goes better when the problem is understood before anything gets drawn. plenty of mistakes come from solving a problem that was slightly different from the real problem the client had. asking questions up front is worth the awkwardness, and playing the answers back in plain words catches gaps early.

keep the early thinking messy on purpose. rough marks on paper, words stuck on a wall, ugly boxes in a layout file. polish too soon makes everything feel precious, and precious things are hard to change when they need changing.

habits that seem to help:

- begin with the constraints, since constraints are where the interesting ideas hide
- write down what the work should make people feel, then check each idea against that phrase
- look at what competitors do, then go somewhere else on purpose
- keep the strange idea that doesn't fit the brief; it frequently turns out to be the useful one
- stay curious about the client's world, because good solutions usually borrow from it

process

a project has a rhythm worth protecting: discovery, exploration, refinement, handoff. the edges blur, but it helps to know which mode is active so feedback arrives at the right moment. comments about colour while the structure is still unsettled are just noise, and they pull the work in circles.

working solo means nobody else is around to catch drift, so the process has to do that job. a short summary at each stage, in plain language, makes it obvious when the direction has wandered. it also gives something to point at when a conversation gets fuzzy.

things that smooth the flow:

- show work in context whenever possible. a logo floating on white space says very little; the same mark on a shopfront, a receipt, or a tiny avatar says a lot
- present a small set of options with a clear point of view rather than a buffet. a buffet hands the choosing back to the client, which is the opposite of what they came for
- explain the reasoning in the client's language, not design jargon
- keep files tidy as the work goes: named layers, sensible folders, clear versions. a future self is also a collaborator
- leave a pause before sending anything out, because work looks different after some distance

when stuck

every project hits a wall where nothing seems right. pushing harder at the same angle rarely helps. a change of angle works better: sketch instead of arranging words, arrange words instead of sketching, explain the problem aloud to an imaginary stranger, or build the ugliest possible version on purpose. bad versions are useful because they show what is specifically wrong, and wrong is easier to fix than blank.

working with clients

most friction in this business isn't about taste. it's about expectations that were never said out loud: what's included, what counts as a revision, who gives feedback, who has the final say. the sooner these get talked through, the less awkward they feel. a calm, friendly, slightly boring conversation about scope beats a tense exchange later.

clients are experts in their own field and nervous in this one. they might not have the vocabulary to say what's wrong, only that something feels off. translating that feeling into something actionable is a big part of the job.

feedback from a committee is its own animal. it helps to find out whose opinion actually carries weight, and to stay kind to the rest.

things to keep in mind:

- say no gently and early, with a reason and an alternative
- keep a clear record of what gets discussed so memory isn't doing the heavy lifting
- thank people for useful feedback; it encourages more of it
- separate taste from problems. taste is personal and negotiable, while poor legibility or confusing structure is worth defending
- be candid when something isn't working, even when that's uncomfortable
- it's fine for a project to be a poor fit, and saying so kindly is part of the service

craft

spacing, rhythm, hierarchy. these are the quiet things that separate work that feels considered from work that merely looks decorated. squint at a layout until the details blur; whatever still stands out is the real hierarchy. if that isn't the thing that should stand out, the layout needs adjusting.

letterforms deserve patience. the shapes between the letters matter as much as the letters themselves. check text at the sizes people actually meet it, in the places they actually meet it, on the devices they actually own.

colour is relational. a shade looks different beside everything around it, so judge it in company, never in isolation.

restraint is underrated. every extra flourish asks the viewer to do more work. ask what happens if something is removed; if nothing is lost, it probably didn't belong.

questions worth asking of any piece of work:

- what is this trying to say, in the plainest terms?
- who is it for, and what do they already know?
- what would make it feel wrong?
- would it survive if the colour drained out of it?
- would it hold up if it were tiny, or enormous?

the business side

running a studio solo means wearing every hat, and the unglamorous hats matter: admin, invoicing, follow-ups, bookkeeping, paperwork. when these slide, stress rises and the work suffers. it helps to treat them as part of the craft rather than a tax on it.

talking about value without flinching is a skill that gets built, not a trait people are born with. value comes from what changes for the client, not from the effort spent inside the studio.

- explain the outcome in terms of the client's own business
- leave space for rest, because tired work shows
- notice which kinds of projects give energy and which drain it, and let that quietly shape taste and direction
- relationships built on trust bring better work than advertising does

open-ended ideas

things to mull over, with no pressure to act on them:

- a collection of small experiments made purely out of curiosity, with no client and no brief, to keep the hands loose
- a habit of writing up what went well and badly after a project wraps, in plain language, for future reference

- a set of reusable questions for kickoff conversations, refined as it goes
- thinking about how the studio explains itself to strangers, and whether the explanation is truthful, warm, and clear
- a gentle way of showing process publicly, so people see the thinking behind the work and not only the result
- making accessible design the default rather than an extra step: contrast, legibility, clear structure, kindness to tired eyes
- mentoring or teaching, even informally; explaining something out loud is a good test of whether it's understood
- what a studio looks like if it deliberately stays small, and what that protects

small reminders

- good ideas arrive during dull tasks: walks, washing up, staring out of windows. leave room for that
- good enough, delivered with care, beats flawless and stuck
- imitation is a decent way to learn and a poor way to practise professionally
- the client's customers are the real audience, so keep them in the room mentally
- keep asking why, especially about habits nobody has questioned
- look at things that have nothing to do with design; they usually have plenty to do with it
- notes like these are only useful if they stay loose enough to change
