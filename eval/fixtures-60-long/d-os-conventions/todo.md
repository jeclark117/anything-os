# To do

working notes, general

**approach**

the work goes better when the brief gets pushed on a bit before anything gets drawn. not combative, just poking at the edges to separate what a client asked for from what they're actually trying to get done. those are rarely the same sentence. a request for a new logo is usually a request for people to take the business more seriously, and that changes what you make.

working solo means there's nobody to bounce rough thinking off, so the notebook has to do that job. write the messy version of the problem in plain words before opening any software. if it can't be said plainly it probably can't be designed plainly either.

keep the early rounds ugly on purpose. rough shapes, grayscale, boxes with labels. polish makes people react to surface details and stops them reacting to structure, and structure is where the real disagreements live.

also worth remembering that taste is a muscle. it gets better by looking at a lot of things critically, not just collecting them. ask why something works, and be specific about it.

**process**

- gather everything the client already has: old files, brand scraps, screenshots of things they like and things they can't stand. the stuff they hate is usually more informative.
- write down constraints early. technical limits, accessibility needs, who has to maintain the thing after handoff, what the budget of attention looks like on the other end.
- explore wide, then narrow. wide means genuinely different directions, not variations on the same idea wearing different colors.
- when showing work, say what kind of feedback is wanted at this stage. direction, not kerning. otherwise the loudest small detail eats the whole conversation.
- keep a running log of why choices were made. everybody forgets, and the reasoning is the valuable part when somebody asks "why is it like this?" months down the line.
- save versions instead of overwriting. disk space is cheaper than regret.
- name files so they make sense when tired. "final" is a lie more often than not, so add something that actually describes the state.
- before handoff, clean up: layers named, styles consolidated, fonts and licenses sorted, and a plain note explaining how the pieces fit together.
- test the work at small sizes, in grayscale, on a bad screen, printed badly. if it survives all that it's probably sturdy.

**working with clients**

- scope creep arrives politely. it usually sounds like "while you're in there." worth having a stock friendly way to say that's a good idea and also a separate piece of work.
- vague feedback is normal and not a sign of a bad client. translate it back: when you say it feels heavy, do you mean the type, the spacing, or the mood?
- silence is information but not always bad news. it can mean busy, unsure, or quietly fine. a gentle nudge with something easy to answer works better than a general check-in.
- put things in writing after calls. a short recap of what was talked about lets misunderstandings surface while they're still cheap to fix.
- be honest early about what's unclear or risky. surprises damage trust, bad news mostly doesn't.
- clients differ in how much process they want. some like being involved at every turn, others want to be handed a finished thing and left in peace. reading which is which saves a lot of friction.
- people are more generous with feedback when they feel the designer has actually heard them. repeating their concern back in your own words goes a long way.
- a good question is often better than a good answer. "what would make this a success for you?" opens more doors than any mood board.

**the business side**

- admin is boring and easy to let drift, so it helps to have a standing habit of glancing at it rather than waiting for it to become urgent. paperwork, records, receipts, who has paid and who hasn't.
- keep a clear line between creative mode and admin mode. mixing them means neither gets proper attention, and the creative stuff especially suffers from being done in the gaps.
- pricing conversations are easier when scope is written clearly. vague scope makes any figure feel arbitrary to both sides.
- gaps between projects aren't wasted. that's where tidying, self-initiated work, and thinking happen. treating every empty stretch as a crisis leads to taking work that isn't a good fit.
- being willing to turn down mismatched work is part of the job. the wrong project costs more than its invoice suggests, in energy and in what it crowds out.
- a studio of this shape lives on reputation and referrals, so how a project ends matters as much as how it goes. a clean, kind wrap-up is marketing.
- track where effort actually goes versus where it was expected to go. over time that's the most honest guide to how to scope things.

**things to keep in mind**

- accessibility isn't an extra. contrast, legibility, motion sensitivity, and clear language are part of the basic quality bar.
- trends fade quickly and clients live with the work long after. lean toward what will still make sense when the current fashion has moved on.
- look at the work upside down, squinting, from across the room, and in a mirror. each trick exposes something different about balance and hierarchy.
- walk away from stuck problems. the answer often shows up during something unrelated, like washing dishes or staring out a window.
- the best work usually has a point of view. safe and generic is easy to produce and easy to forget.
- ask for help or a second pair of eyes when needed, even though working independently makes that feel awkward. a fresh view catches things that familiarity hides.
- energy is a resource too. protect the stretches when ideas flow, and don't schedule demanding creative work into the dead zones.
- remember that not everything needs to be reinvented. good conventions exist because they work, and breaking them should be on purpose.

**open-ended ideas**

- build a library of small reusable pieces: spacing systems, type pairings, color relationships, layout skeletons. refine them across projects without making everything look the same.
- adopt a short reflection habit after each project wraps. what went smoothly, what dragged, what the client really valued, what to do differently.
- write more openly about process. case studies that focus on the problem and the reasoning rather than just glossy final images.
- find a better way of collecting reference material than a pile of screenshots. attach a sentence about why each thing caught the eye, so the collection becomes a record of taste.
- play with constraints as a creative exercise: a tiny palette, a lone typeface, no imagery at all, only type and space.
- trade critiques with other independent designers. a loose circle of peers could offer perspective without the weight of formal mentorship.
- make something purely for fun, with no client and no brief, and see what it teaches about instincts that client work tends to suppress.
- sketch a kickoff question template that can be adapted per project, so the early conversation reliably covers goals, audience, constraints, and fears.
- think about how to document handoffs so clients can keep a project alive without needing to call, perhaps with short recorded walkthroughs.
- keep a voice memo habit at the end of a working session to catch loose thoughts before they evaporate.
- explore what a more generous, teaching-oriented side of the practice might look like: workshops, small guides, or open files that help others learn.
- consider how the physical workspace shapes the work. light, clutter, where the screen sits, whether there's a surface for printing and pinning things up.
- wonder about slowing down on purpose: fewer projects, more depth, and what that would do to the quality of the thinking.

mostly these are reminders to self. the point isn't a system, just a few handrails for when things get busy and judgment gets fuzzy.
