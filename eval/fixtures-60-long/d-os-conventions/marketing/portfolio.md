# Portfolio

general working notes

**how a new piece of work tends to start**

start with the problem, not the look. before anything gets drawn, write out in plain sentences what the thing is supposed to do for the person using it. if that paragraph is hard to write, the design will be hard too. keep it pinned somewhere visible and check back against it whenever a decision feels arbitrary.

then gather reference without judging it. screenshots, scraps of packaging, type specimens, photos of signs, textures, odd bits of ephemera. sort afterwards by feeling rather than category. patterns show up on their own if the pile is big enough and the sorting is loose enough.

draw rough thumbnails on purpose, and draw them badly. paper, pen instead of pencil, so there's no erasing and nothing precious. the goal is volume and surprise, not polish. the ugly ones often hold the best structural idea, and the polished ones tend to hide weak thinking behind nice edges.

**process habits worth keeping**

- work in greyscale for as long as possible. color is a seasoning, and it covers up hierarchy problems that should be solved with size, weight, and space.
- squint test. blur the screen or step back from it. if the main thing isn't obvious, the layout isn't finished.
- type does most of the heavy lifting. put real effort into pairing, spacing, line length, and rhythm before reaching for decoration.
- build a tiny system early: a small set of sizes, a restrained palette, a spacing rule. then break it deliberately, and only when breaking it earns something.
- name layers and files as if a stranger will open them. that stranger is usually future me, tired and confused.
- keep a "not this" board next to the inspiration board. knowing what to avoid sharpens direction faster than collecting more of what's liked.
- when a layout feels stuck, change the scale of something dramatically. huge or microscopic, it doesn't matter. the shock usually reveals what the composition was missing.
- look at work upside down and in a mirror. odd, but it exposes balance problems the eye has learned to forgive.

**working with clients**

being a studio of just me means the whole relationship runs through me: listening, making, explaining, invoicing. the explaining part is underrated. a good rationale makes feedback better, because people react to reasoning instead of pure taste.

- show work with a story: here's the problem, here's what was tried, here's why this direction. don't just drop an image into a thread and wait.
- ask what the work needs to accomplish for their audience, not what they like. liking comes later and is much easier to manage once the goal is stated in their own words.
- feedback phrased as a solution ("make the logo bigger") is usually a symptom. dig for the worry underneath it.
- present fewer options than feels natural. too many choices dilute the thinking and invite mixing and matching into mush.
- write down what was said after calls. memory edits conversations in flattering ways.
- be honest early about what's unclear. surprises late in a project cost more goodwill than awkward questions at the start.
- let people see work in progress when they're nervous, and keep it back when they're prone to fixating on details that haven't been resolved yet.
- remember that the person approving the work often has to defend it to others. give them language they can borrow.

**things to keep in mind**

- Add Client C.
- Add Client A once it launches.

- constraints are friends. a narrow brief is easier to do well than an open field.
- rest matters. the best fixes tend to arrive on a walk, in the shower, or while washing dishes. stepping away is part of the process, not a break from it.
- consistency beats cleverness. a clever idea that can't survive being applied across a whole system of materials is a trap.
- accessibility isn't an extra layer. contrast, legible sizes, clear focus states, and a sensible reading order belong in the earliest drawings.
- test on real content. placeholder text hides every problem. long names, awkward headlines, empty states, error messages: put the ugly real material in early.
- test on small screens and bad screens. test on paper too, if it will ever be printed.
- be wary of work that pulls the studio away from what it's good at, even when it looks easy. easy work that doesn't fit has a way of becoming hard.
- taste is a muscle. it gets better by looking at a lot of things critically, including things that are disliked, and asking why.
- the thing being designed is almost never the thing the audience cares about. they care about what it lets them do.

**keeping the studio healthy**

- leave breathing room in the calendar for the unexpected. things take longer than predicted, and a studio with no room has no space for good surprises either.
- record the process while it happens: screenshots of rough stages, notes on why choices were made. case studies are far easier to assemble from a trail than from memory.
- keep a running file of kind words, small wins, and lessons from things that went sideways. useful on bad days, and useful when writing about the work.
- protect a little unpaid, unbriefed making. personal experiments feed the paid work in ways that are hard to trace but easy to feel.
- tidy the asset library regularly: fonts, icons, templates, textures, reusable components. write licenses and sources down beside each.
- back everything up, in more than a single way. boring, essential.
- notice when the work is starting to look like itself too much. a recognizable voice is good; a rut is not.
- set up templates for the dull parts of running things, like proposals, handoff documents, and check-in messages, so the energy goes to the design.

**when stuck**

- explain the problem out loud to an empty room, or write it as a letter. the answer often appears halfway through the sentence.
- remove things. the fix is more often subtraction than addition.
- switch media. if it's stuck on a screen, move to paper or cut shapes out of card. if it's stuck on paper, build it in the browser.
- make the worst version deliberately. it clears the path of expectation.
- look at how a completely different field solves a similar problem: signage, cooking, architecture, board games, instruction manuals.
- ask what the design would look like if it had to work with no color, no images, and no clever copy. whatever survives is the real structure.

**open-ended ideas**

- a set of reusable questions for kickoff conversations, refined through use. maybe a template that reads like a conversation rather than a form.
- a library of typographic studies: how a sentence feels in different weights, spacings, and settings. a visual aid for explaining choices to people who don't speak design.
- a habit of redrawing admired work from scratch to understand how it was built, kept private, never published.
- exploring motion more seriously, even in small touches, to see where it clarifies and where it only decorates.
- short essays on process, as a way of thinking and as a quiet way for the right people to find the studio.
- exercises with strict limits: type only, circles only, no color at all, a palette pulled from a photograph of something ordinary.
- a physical sample shelf of paper stocks, finishes, and printed swatches, so print conversations feel less abstract.
- a design diary of small observations from everyday life: bad wayfinding, delightful labels, confusing forms. training data for the eye.
- lighter feedback rounds, such as recorded screen walkthroughs with spoken comments instead of long email threads.
- a guide for people who will maintain the work after handoff, written warmly and plainly, with a clear way to ask for help later.
- a collection of "before and after" pairs that show how small changes in spacing and hierarchy transform a layout.
- thinking about how the studio's own materials, like proposals and invoices, could be treated as design work instead of paperwork.

**closing thought**

the work is mostly listening, then removing things. when stuck, make it plainer and more honest and see what's missing. when it feels too safe, push on one thing until it's uncomfortable, then pull back a little. the goal is work that feels inevitable, as if it could never have looked any other way.
