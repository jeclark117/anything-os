# Client C

general working notes

approach

start by hearing people out before proposing anything. projects mostly go sideways because the real problem got named wrong at the beginning, not because the layout was off. a brief is usually a list of symptoms, and what needs fixing sits underneath. ask what a good outcome looks like in the person's own words, then ask what would make it feel like a failure. the failure answer is frequently more useful.

keep early sketches ugly on purpose. rough shapes on paper or in a throwaway file let people react without feeling they have to be polite about finished work. polish that arrives too soon invites arguments about spacing when the structure is what actually needs debating.

work from material outward. a layout built around placeholder text breaks as soon as real words show up. get actual copy, even rough copy, in front of the design as early as possible. if there isn't any, write stand-ins that have the right length and the right awkwardness instead of tidy dummy text.

treat constraints as friends. a tight palette, a limited set of typefaces, a stubborn grid. they remove fiddling and make the remaining choices count.

process, roughly

- gather: collect whatever exists. old materials, screenshots of things they admire, things they can't stand, the way they talk about their own work. the can't-stand list is gold.
- sort: group it into themes. look for what keeps surfacing without anybody asking for it.
- frame: put the problem in a plain line anybody could repeat back. if the line turns mushy, the project probably is too.
- explore: wide at the start, narrow afterward. resist falling for a direction just because it arrived early.
- pressure-test: show it to somebody who has never seen it, say nothing, and watch where they hesitate.
- refine: spacing, type, contrast, rhythm. this is where taste lives and also where it's easiest to overwork something into blandness.
- hand off: leave the person with something they can maintain without needing to call me.

**approach**

The work goes better when the problem gets stated in plain language before any pixels move. If I can't explain what the thing is for in a sentence a stranger would follow, no amount of layout will rescue it. So the habit is to write the problem down in the roughest words possible, then argue with it until it sounds less like a brief and more like something a real person wants.

Show the weak version early. A rough sketch gets honest reactions, while a polished comp gets politeness. Polish makes people reluctant to poke at the work, and the poking is the useful part. It's uncomfortable to put unfinished work in front of a client, but the discomfort is cheaper than building a whole direction on a misunderstanding.

Keep the reference pile small and strange. Looking at other design work tends to produce more of the same design work. Better material comes from hand-painted shop signs, old instruction leaflets, packaging from odd corner stores, worn labels on tools, ticket stubs, the lettering on a delivery van. Anything made by somebody who needed it to function and only then cared how it looked.

Treat constraints as free ideas. A limited palette, an awkward format, a reader with almost no attention to spare: these all narrow the field so the interesting move becomes obvious. Wide-open briefs are harder, not easier, and usually the first useful thing to do with one is to invent some walls.

Prefer systems over isolated pieces. When a logo, a page, and a label all come from the same few rules, the client can extend the work without calling me, and that's a kind of respect for them. A good system should survive being used badly by somebody in a hurry.

**process notes**

- Listen for the thing the client keeps circling without naming. That's usually the real brief hiding underneath the stated one.
- Write the constraints down before the ideas. Format, audience, where it will be seen, what it has to survive. Ideas that ignore these are decoration.
- Make the ugly version on purpose. It loosens the grip on whatever clever thing I was already attached to.
- Squint test: blur the screen, step back, see what still reads. If the hierarchy dies when it's small or fuzzy, it wasn't hierarchy.
- Print things when possible. Paper tells the truth about scale and spacing in a way a bright screen never does.
- Name files as if a stranger has to open them in a rush. Plain words, clear versions, no cute labels.
- Leave breadcrumbs inside working files: a short line on why a color is there, why a grid is off by a hair, what was tried and abandoned.
- Look at the work upside down, mirrored, in grayscale, and at the size of a thumbnail. Each trick exposes a different kind of lie.
- Separate making from judging. Making mode is generous and messy; judging mode is cold and picky. Mixing them produces timid work.
- Write the rationale for a piece in a few plain sentences before presenting it. If the sentences won't come, the work probably isn't ready.

**approach**

- make the boring parts good. the forms, the error messages, the empty states, the file that gets sent around. people spend more time there than on the showpiece

**process**

projects seem to move through a loose rhythm: listening, wandering, narrowing, refining, handing over. the edges blur and that's fine, but it helps to know which mode I'm actually in. wandering with a refining mindset kills ideas too early. refining with a wandering mindset never ends.

*listening.* let people keep talking past the point where they've said the useful thing. the useful thing usually shows up right after the polite summary. pay attention to the words they repeat, and to what they avoid saying.

*wandering.* collect references without judging them. a pile of things that feel adjacent: old signage, packaging, type specimens, odd interfaces, the way a shop window is lit. look for the feeling underneath, not the surface that can be copied.

*narrowing.* name what's being left out. every direction that gets dropped deserves a sentence about why, otherwise it creeps back in at the worst moment and wastes everyone's energy.

*refining.* zoom out regularly. squint at it, flip it upside down, look at it tiny on a phone and enormous on a big screen. most flaws show up when the scale changes. step away, then look again with fresh eyes.

when things feel off

usually it comes down to muddy hierarchy, uneven spacing rhythm, or copy whose voice doesn't match the look. check those before reaching for new colors or fresh fonts. also worth asking whether there's simply too much on the page. most pages are improved by taking something away. remove the decoration and see whether anything was lost. usually nothing was.

feedback

- separate reaction from instruction. "it feels cold" is useful. "make it blue" is a guess at a remedy. ask what the feeling is attached to.
- when feedback conflicts, there are usually different goals hiding behind it. surface them gently.
- don't defend on the spot. write it down, let it settle, answer thoughtfully.
- praise is data too. ask what exactly landed, because that's the thing to protect.
- quiet reactions matter. silence can mean delight or confusion, and the two look identical on a call.

voice and taste

keep a loose sense of what the studio's work feels like without letting it harden into a house style that gets stamped on everybody. the goal is that the work belongs to the people it's for. taste is useful as a filter and dangerous as a signature.

**things to keep in mind**

Clients rarely want exactly what they ask for. They ask for a bigger logo when they mean they don't feel seen, or for a brighter color when they mean the thing feels untrustworthy. Asking "what would that fix?" is often more productive than doing the request literally. Feedback is nearly always accurate about the feeling and unreliable about the remedy, so take the feeling seriously and treat the suggested fix as a hint.

Working solo means there's no built-in friction. Nobody rolls their eyes at a weak idea, which is nice, but it also means weak ideas can survive far too long. It helps to build in outside eyes on purpose, even informal ones: a friend who doesn't care about design, a relative who will say "I don't get it" without apology. The person who doesn't get it is giving a gift.

Taste is not craft. Taste tells me what's good; craft is the slow, physical ability to make it so. They grow at different speeds and the gap between them can be frustrating. A long stretch of feeling like the work is worse than what I can imagine is normal, and probably a sign that taste is ahead, which is the right order.

Legibility and contrast are not optional extras. Plenty of beautiful work fails because nobody checked whether a tired person with poor light could read it. Accessibility belongs in the opening sketch, not the cleanup pass.

Saying "I don't know yet" is fine and often builds more trust than a confident guess. Clients mostly want to feel the work is in careful hands, not that every answer is instant.

Scope creeps quietly. It usually arrives as a small, friendly, reasonable ask. The trick is noticing the shape of it early and naming it kindly, so the conversation about it is easy rather than tense.

When stuck, change the medium before changing the idea. Pencil instead of mouse, cut paper instead of vectors, a loud voice memo instead of a written outline. Often the idea was fine and the tool was just holding it hostage.

Rest is part of the process, not a reward after it. Some of the best solutions show up during a walk, a chore, or a bad night's sleep, once the problem has been loaded and then left alone.

Be kind to future me. Tidy folders, readable notes, and honest labels are gifts to somebody who will have forgotten everything and will be a little annoyed.

**loose thoughts**

Sometimes the most valuable thing in a project is the dull middle, where nothing looks impressive and the real decisions about spacing, wording, and rhythm get made. Nobody posts about that part, but it's where the work gets its quality.

There's a temptation to mistake busyness for progress. A full desk, a long list, and a lot of open files can feel productive while the central question sits untouched. Occasionally it's worth closing everything and asking, plainly, what the work is trying to say.

Finally, remember why this kind of work is appealing at all: making ordinary things feel considered. A label, a form, a sign, a page. People rarely notice good design directly, but they feel easier around it, and that quiet effect is the point.

**things to keep in mind**

- people are often more attached to their own words than to any visual. handle copy gently and explain edits instead of just making them
- consistency beats cleverness. a system that's slightly plain but holds together is more useful than something dazzling that falls apart at the edges
- accessibility isn't a layer that gets added at the end. contrast, reading size, motion, keyboard use, how things sound when read aloud. build it in from the start and it costs almost nothing extra
- tell people early when something is more complicated than it looked. surprises damage trust far more than bad news does
- keep the edges of the scope visible. small requests accumulate quietly and then suddenly the project is a different shape
- feedback is information about a reaction, not a set of instructions. "make it pop" means something is missing, and the job is to work out what
- good taste is mostly restraint. when in doubt, remove something and see if anyone misses it
- a design that only works in the presentation mockup isn't finished. test it with real text, real images, awkward lengths, and slow connections
- trends are fine as seasoning, but they date quickly. ask whether it would still feel right long after the excitement fades
